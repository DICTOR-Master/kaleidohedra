The 4D permutohedra fill four-dimensional space, every one a translate of the same cell. Cut that filling by a three-dimensional slice and the cells it crosses fill the slice. In a rational direction the cut repeats; #105 left the golden direction u = (1, φ, φ², φ³, φ⁴) as its second lead.

Theorem 12.1. Cut the permutohedron filling by the hyperplanes u · x = c with u = (1, φ, φ², φ³, φ⁴), at a generic offset c. Then: (i) the cells of the slice are sections of one permutohedron at heights that are dense, so the slice repeats nowhere and its cells come in infinitely many shapes; (ii) they come in exactly 35 combinatorial types, each occurring infinitely often. The 120 corners of the permutohedron lie at only 70 distinct golden heights: 50 coincide because φ² = φ + 1.

Proof. A cell's section is P ∩ {u · x = c − u · λ}, λ in the lattice; since u · λ runs over a set containing integers and integer multiples of φ, the offsets are dense, which gives (i). The combinatorial type of a section is the set of faces of P that straddle the level, with their containments; it is constant between consecutive corner heights. The heights u · σ over the 120 orderings σ of −2, …, 2 were computed exactly in ℤ[φ] (φi = Fi−1 + Fiφ): 70 distinct values, 69 open bands; their straddling-face graphs fall into 35 isomorphism classes, and density puts cells in every band. ∎ (golden_types.py)

The golden direction sits between the two: not repeating, as a rational cut does, but with far fewer kinds than a generic cut, because the golden ratio makes corners meet at the same height. This is the permutohedron's own version of the passage from crystal to quasicrystal: a finite set of kinds, never repeating. Whether the 35 kinds can be cut down to finitely many shapes by the projection (window) method that gives Penrose-type tilings is open.
