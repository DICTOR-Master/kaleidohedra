Book arcs: Space-Fillers and the Platonic book
Two books checked against DICTO's rule of a beginning, a middle and an end

DICTO · report of 13 November 2026 · REVIEW, for DICTO's decision, not for publication
REVIEW for DICTO's decision; not for publication. Neither book has been changed. Read: Space-Fillers and Lattice Moves (draft book, the book file and its PDF) and the Platonic book (the opening, every chapter file, the coda, the epilogue, the assembly script and its PDF). Kept as decided: the structure DICTO approved for both books, and his decision of 13 November that the Dogstar and Every Mix are told in full in Space-Fillers, with pointer chapters in the Platonic book. Every proposed change below is new text or a change of order, written as a sample; none uses a result that the books do not already state.

The rule

DICTO's standing rule of 13 November: every book has a beginning, a middle and an end. The beginning is a question. The middle is a climb in which each chapter hands a question to the next. The summit is the unifying result. The end is a return to the beginning, transformed. A catalogue ordered by topic is not enough.

Verdicts. Space-Fillers has a strong middle (eight of its nine joins already hand something on) but no beginning question and no end: it stops after Chapter 10 and goes straight into the Atlas. It needs a question in the Opening, three bridge sentences, a named summit (Chapter 8) and a one-page Closing. The Platonic book already has the shape of an arc (the outsider in the Opening, the residents of Chapter 11, the hidden side of the OG in Chapter 12, Plato again in the Coda), but the question is never asked out loud, Part One reads as a survey, the Opening gives away the ending, and the last pages run summit, return, then an outward look, then a recap. It needs the question stated, two bridges and one corrected sentence, Chapters 12 and 13 swapped, and the Epilogue moved before the Coda.

(b) Diagnosis

Beginning: not yet a question. The Opening describes ("This book is about telling these apart"), and the parts are named by topic. The question is already in the subtitle: which solids fill space, which only together, and which hold by shape alone. It needs to be asked as one question with three rising steps.

Middle: a real climb, three weak joins. Each part asks for less of the solid: fill alone, then fill with partners, then hold without glue. Most joins are already written. The weak ones: 3→4 (the thirteen-block arrives as a new topic, though an index-13 sublattice is one more lattice move: keep the lattice and enlarge the cell); 7→8 (the summit arrives unannounced); 9→10 (the frustrated network and the held layer are the same layer, but Chapter 10's opening does not say so).

Summit: Chapter 8, unnamed. Every Mix gathers the book: the five Platonic solids, which fail as bricks apart from the cube (Part I's question), all fill space with partners, and the kit uses pieces of earlier chapters (the Dogstar of Chapter 6, the face pyramid, the Admiral Caps). The book never says this is its high point.

End: missing. There is no return. A natural one exists in the text: Chapter 9 brings back Chapter 1's Dehn test to show that none of the network's three pieces fills space alone, and Chapter 10 finds the same mixed layer held together by shape. The brick of the Opening comes back changed: a piece that can never be a brick sits in a layer that needs no mortar.

(c) Smallest changes

1. Opening: replace the first paragraph; keep the parts paragraph. Sample:

Which solids fill space? A cube does, on its own, like a brick. A tetrahedron cannot, but with the octahedron it can. And some pieces, laid in one layer, hold one another so tightly that the layer needs no glue. This book asks the question three times, and each time asks less of the solid: can it fill space alone, can it fill space with partners, and once a filling is found, does it hold by its shape? Each answer hands the next question on. Dehn's ledger turns most solids away as bricks, and among the five Platonic solids it leaves only the cube; the solids turned away find partners, pair by pair, until every mix of the five fills space (Chapter 8); and the layer that carries the icosahedron in that filling is then tested, piece by piece, for whether it holds.

2. Bridge 3→4, first sentence of Chapter 4:

Chapter 3 moved the lattice and kept its cell. Keep the lattice and enlarge the cell instead: take one point in thirteen of the face-centred cubic lattice, and the cell round each point becomes a block of thirteen rhombic dodecahedra.

3. Bridge 7→8 and the summit named, first sentences of Chapter 8:

Chapter 7 ended with eight Jewels that no shear brings face to face. The same refusal returns at the top of the book. Of the five Platonic solids only the cube is a brick; here all five, in every one of their 31 mixes, fill space with partners, and the one mix that needs no connector, cubes, tetrahedra and octahedra, never meets face to face.

4. Bridge 9→10, first sentences of Chapter 10:

Chapter 9 showed that the Admiral network can never agree with itself: wherever the icosahedra turn, at most eight of every twelve shared edges match. A frustrated layer might be expected to fall apart at a touch. This chapter tests whether it does, and finds the opposite.

5. A Closing, one page between Chapter 10 and the Atlas. Sample:

Closing · Back to the brick. The book began with a brick, a solid that fills space alone, and asked which solids can do what a brick does. Alone, very few: on each sweep Dehn's ledger leaves only a handful of members, and among the five Platonic solids only the cube. Together, all of them: every one of the 31 mixes of the five fills space with one kit of seven connectors, the Dogstar of Chapter 6 and DICTO's Admiral Caps among them. Held, some: in the Admiral layer every Admiral Cap and every icosahedron is locked against every small slide and twist, and only the small pyramids on its surface lift out. The ledger of Chapter 1 came back in Chapter 9 to show that none of that layer's three pieces could fill space alone. So the brick returns changed. A brick needs mortar; the icosahedron, which can never be a brick, sits in a layer of partners that holds without any.

Nothing is reordered. Cost: about one page of new text and four replaced sentences.

(d) Swaps in this book

Three fit without forcing. Piece and hole: Chapters 5 and 6 are one filling read twice; the book's own sentence is that "the difference is only who owns the tip of each roof, and it turns a rational filling into a golden one". The golden twins: in Chapter 9 every icosahedron has two ways to sit in its octahedron, and the frustration is the fact that no choice of these swaps agrees everywhere; the best arrangements keep one free flip per layer, like the c/h choice of close-packed stackings. Mirror pairs: in Chapter 2 every tiling found uses the piece and its mirror image, two to a period, and Chapter 5 §4 shows the Jewel's packing does not twin by a (111) mirror. The lattice-or-solid turn of Chapter 3 is a change of viewpoint, not a swap in the geometric sense, and is better left out of the thread. The swap thread is not a spine for this book; it can be one sentence in the Closing if DICTO wants it there.

(b) Diagnosis

Beginning: the question is there but not asked. The Opening sets up the outsider well, then spends its second half on the OG's clockwork, its Galois twin and the regular solids on its hidden side. Those are Chapter 12's results; told in the Opening, they give away the ending, and they make two outsiders where the arc needs one question.

Middle: Part Two climbs; Part One surveys. Chapters 6 to 10 hand each other the icosahedron, the Admiral Caps, the Dogstar and the grid, a real climb. Part One is five studies of the five, of which 4→5 and 5→6 hand nothing on. The smallest fix is two bridge sentences that say why each chapter is on the road to the question; a reordering is not needed. One sentence is wrong as it stands: Chapter 10's "The five solids do not fill space together" comes right after two chapters proving that every mix does, with connectors.

Summit: Chapter 11, unnamed. Proposition 11.2 answers the Opening: all five live together among the corners of every uniform polytope of the Queens' family, and of the four four-dimensional reflection groups only there. Chapter 13's "there is no H5" completes it: that one home is also the last golden one.

End: present but buried. Chapter 12 closes the loop to the OG and the Coda closes it to Plato, but Chapter 13 sits between the return and the Coda and ends by leaving the book, and the Epilogue after the Coda turns the last pages back into a catalogue.

(c) Smallest changes

1. Opening: ask the question; keep the OG as a promise. After the paragraph on the outsider, add:

So the book has a question. Is there a place where all five are at home together, the outsider included? It climbs towards an answer in three parts: inside the five, where they hold one another; the five filling space, where with help every mix of them fills it; and where the five live, the one family of four-dimensional solids whose corners hold all five.

In "A second outsider: the OG" keep the first two paragraphs (the solid and the touching cut) and replace the third, the clockwork and the hidden side, with one sentence, leaving its results to Chapter 12 where they are told again: "What the OG has to do with the five is the book's last discovery, and it is kept for Chapter 12." The figure may stay.

2. Bridges in Part One. End of Chapter 4: "The Vacuus read each solid's angles from its dual. The round bodies of Chapter 5 find the same pairing in a width: dual pairs, dodecahedron and icosahedron, cube and octahedron, are equally wide across their face axes." End of Chapter 5: "Nested, cut, hollowed and rounded, the five have so far been studied one at a time. Part Two puts them into space together, starting with the one that resists most, the icosahedron."

3. Correct the first sentence of Chapter 10:

Chapters 8 and 9 filled space with the five and their helpers. A lattice can do something stricter: hold all five at once, nested in every cell, with no helper pieces.

4. Name the summit, in Chapter 11 after the first paragraph: "This chapter answers the question of the Opening."

5. Swap Chapters 12 and 13, so that the loop closes last. Beyond the Five becomes Chapter 12 and closes the summit (the one home is the last golden one); Towards the Quasicrystal becomes Chapter 13 and returns to the OG of the Opening. The new Chapter 12 ends with one bridge in place of its outward pointer, which moves into its body: "The golden line stops in four dimensions, but the golden numbers do not: they move to lattices and their shadows. The last chapter follows them back down into three dimensions, to the hidden side of the outsider." Cost: the chapter list in the assembly script, and the cross-references to Chapter 12 in the Opening, Chapters 4 and 6 and the Epilogue.

6. Epilogue before the Coda, so that the book ends on Plato. The Epilogue keeps its table of the five and its open problems. The Coda gains a last paragraph. Sample:

The book began with Plato's five and an outsider among them, the icosahedron, water, which fills no space by itself, and it asked where all five could be at home together. In flat space all five together are at home only with help: in shells, in octahedra with Admiral Caps, beside Dogstars, with a small kit of connectors. One dimension up, all five live together, and in one family only: on the corners of the Queens, where the icosahedron is carved out of the White Queen beside the tetrahedron. And the outsider DICTO added, the OG, which is none of the five, carries the regular icosahedron and dodecahedron on its hidden side. Water and the heavens, the two solids that would not fill the room, are found again in the two places this book could not reach by tiling: on the corners of a four-dimensional solid, and behind the golden numbers.

Cost: one changed order, one corrected sentence, six new sentences and two paragraphs; one Opening paragraph shortened. Pointer Chapters 7 and 8 stay as decided.

(d) Swaps in this book

Here the swap thread fits naturally, and duality is the strongest strand. Duality: the polar law of Chapter 4 reads a fin's angle as π minus the dihedral angle at the dual edge; Chapter 5's dual pairs share a face-axis width; the Chain of Karma of Chapter 6 alternates dodecahedron and icosahedron shells, each shell with one piece for every corner, edge and face of the dual pair. The tetrahedron is the fixed point of this swap, its own polar, with fins of 109.47°, and in Chapter 4.5 it is the only one of the five whose edges stay convex when its pyramids are turned out (the cube's become flat, closing into the rhombic dodecahedron). (The order D·I·C·T·O itself sets the self-dual tetrahedron between the cube and the octahedron.) The two golden cuts of Chapter 2 swap which side is long, and on the octahedron the pair fills space. The Galois swap √5 ↔ −√5 is Chapter 12's hidden side, with pentagons becoming pentagrams. The neighbour swap appears in Chapter 12's four-dimensional sweep, where a hidden fin is born at one exchange of neighbours and ends at another. Two golden positions of Pacioli's icosahedron in each octahedron (Chapter 6). The proposition that laws sit at the fixed points of these swaps is still under test (study 2026-11-13-swaps-and-fixed-points); the book can name the swaps where they occur, and should claim no law until that study reports.

What would change, in one list

Space-Fillers: new first paragraph of the Opening; bridge sentences at the heads of Chapters 4, 8 and 10, Chapter 8 named as the summit; a one-page Closing, "Back to the brick", before the Atlas. No reordering.

Platonic book: the question asked in the Opening and the OG results there cut to a promise; bridges at the ends of Chapters 4 and 5; Chapter 10's first sentence corrected; Chapter 11 named as the answer; Chapters 12 and 13 swapped; the Epilogue before the Coda, and a last paragraph for the Coda.

Both sets of changes are DICTO's to approve; none has been made.
