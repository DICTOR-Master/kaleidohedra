Book plan: A Brief Geometry of Space
DICTO's three aims as one story, from pieces to crystals to the quasicrystal, and the geometry that gives laws

Working title only; the title is DICTO's: "A Brief Geometry of Space", preferred to his first idea "A Brief History of Space" (after Stephen Hawking); his subtitle idea "The Space of Quanta"; the title-clash check is still pending. DICTO's decision of 13 November: all books before new papers; this is the fourth and last book, the capstone, after Space-Fillers, The Permutohedron Paradigm and the Platonic book. Nothing below claims more than the sources prove; open items are marked open.

Opening for the reader. There are two ways to fill a room with tiles. One is the bathroom floor: a pattern that repeats, so that a small patch tells you the whole floor. Every salt grain and every diamond is built like this, and the patch is called a crystal's cell. The other way was thought impossible in nature until 1982, when Dan Shechtman found a metal whose atoms are perfectly ordered and yet never repeat, with a five-fold symmetry no repeating pattern can have: a quasicrystal. This book asks how the two are joined. It starts with single pieces, the shapes that fill space on their own, and asks whether one piece could force the never-repeating kind. It stacks them into crystals and finds that every crystal sits on a line of crystals that walks out, turns, and walks back. It finds the quasicrystal at the turning point. And it ends with a question from modern physics: can a shape hand out laws, the way one polyhedron, the amplituhedron, hands out the rules of particle collisions?

The arc. Pieces → crystals → the quasicrystal at the turn → laws. These are DICTO's three aims (9 November 2026), told in the order a reader meets them: aim 3, one piece, any stacking (Part One); aim 1, the periodic–aperiodic bridge (Parts Two and Three); aim 2, geometry gives laws (Part Four). DICTO's sentence for the whole: the quasicrystal is the turning point of a line of crystals. The book is popular in voice (no proofs in the text, each theorem stated in words with one picture) and exact in substance (every claim carries its paper or study, every open question is said to be open).

Part Four: geometry gives laws

Part Four opens with DICTO's proposition that law-giving shapes order space at a distance (Chapter 12): it carries the crystals of Part Two into the language of laws and points back to the one piece of Part One.

12. Shapes that order space at a distance (the bridge chapter; title is DICTO's to change)The founding insight (DICTO, 13 November; spelling tidied, words unchanged): "This, from the very beginning of my node work, is what set the DICTOspheres work apart: everyone always believed the vertices of the shapes defined the network structure, and were therefore the inverse of how they operated." In DICTO's work the faces of a shape direct its connections to its neighbours; the usual view is the inverse, with the vertices defining the network and the edges as its struts. The face view is the polar (dual) view: faces point at neighbours, as in Voronoi–Delaunay duality, known mathematics, which DICTO reached independently. The canonical form has its poles exactly on the face planes, so the proposition below grows from this insight and links to the polar of aim 2. DICTO's proposition: "Law-giving shapes in general, as a proposition: like DICTOspheres, my PET-bottle nodes, they order space between their faces at a distance, like the PET bottles that DICTOspheres connect in any given grouping." The nodes and bottles are a structural metaphor only (DICTO). The proposition is about law-giving shapes in general: a shape's faces fix the directions to its neighbours and a distance fixes how far they sit, so the shape orders the space between shapes, not only the space it fills. DICTO's sharpening: "so the law-giving shapes act at a distance governed by their amplitude", where amplitude is the canonical form of Part Four. The canonical form ΩP is a rational function on all of space: its poles are exactly the face planes, inside P it equals d! times the volume of the polar body about that point, and it falls off with distance outside. So the faces are the sources of a field the shape spreads around itself. Statement: "A law-giving shape acts at a distance through its canonical form: the field its faces create decides where its neighbours can sit, and so which grouping it forces."Known mathematics, by name: Delone's local theorem (Delone, Dolbilin, Shtogrin, Galiulin 1976: identical neighbourhoods out to a radius force a regular system; order at a distance made exact, the likely backbone of a proof); Voronoi–Delaunay duality (faces point at neighbours); crystal nets (RCSR, O'Keeffe et al.). 12, test outcomeFirst test (study 2026-11-13-amplitude-at-distance, 13 November): negative. In the pre-registered form (does the RD's field choose fcc and the TRD's choose hcp, against the cube, the truncated octahedron and controls) the proposition is not supported: choice, direction and position all fail. A shape's own lattice points lie exactly on the poles of its canonical form, the extended face planes, but that is bisector geometry (the neighbour across a face is mirrored in it), not the form's values; the controls (icosahedron, dodecahedron, perturbed RD) show the same signatures. New finding, outside the plan: far away, the canonical form of every zonotope falls off like r−6 (RD, cube, truncated octahedron, hexagonal prism, and also the rhombic triacontahedron, which does not fill space), the TRD like r−5, every other shape tested like r−4: a zonotope law, not a tiling law; exact at the tested directions, general proof open. Lead only (not tested): the pole structure itself, with neighbours sitting on the field lines of the face planes

15. Near, but never on?The special points of a line (its roundest point, its design station) sit close to the turn. Exactly on it when the solid is its own dual partner (Theorem A) or when the turning solid's symmetry silences the tone (Theorem B: on the 4D cube line the turning solid is the 24-cell). Elsewhere they miss by a computable amount, and the nearness drifts away in higher dimensions. A partial law: "never on" is false in general, and nearness is not a law.study 2026-11-13-near-but-never-on (Theorems A, B; unpublished); Geometry Gives Laws, Theorem 2; #94New chapter from the study.
