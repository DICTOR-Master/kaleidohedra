Plate 1. The c-tile

The tile of a cubic layer, from the cubic stacking ABC…, at λ = 1/10 (as Figure 14.1) · Chapter 14

  • One ball's share of its layer, made of four pieces: the top half of the octahedral piece below (dark violet), the two tetrahedral pieces (gold, meeting at the ball, black dot) and the bottom half of the octahedral piece above (light violet).
  • In every close-packed stacking, at every weight of the walk −11/24 ≤ λ ≤ 3/8, the tile of every c-layer is carried onto this tile by one motion, all four pieces at once (the Two-Tile Theorem).
  • The two octahedral halves are set apart, because the layers above and below sit on different positions. The ball is a centre of symmetry of the cubic stacking and swaps the two tetrahedral pieces and the two halves.
  • Above a c-tile the next layer takes the third position.

Plate 2. The h-tile

The tile of a hexagonal layer, from the hexagonal stacking AB…, at λ = 1/10 (as Figure 14.1) · Chapter 14

  • The same four kinds of piece as the c-tile: two octahedral halves (violet) and two tetrahedral pieces (gold); black dot, the layer's ball.
  • The two halves lie on one vertical line, because the layers above and below sit on the same position; that is the whole difference between the two tiles. The layer plane is a mirror of the hexagonal stacking.
  • Any tile sits on any tile: the joint between two layers, the section of an octahedral piece through its hole, has one shape for both tiles. Every string of c- and h-tiles is a stacking, and every stacking is such a string.
  • The tiles meet on flat planes for λ ≤ −7/24 and interlock above; at λ = 1/10 the tetrahedral pieces reach a full layer up and down. Above an h-tile the next layer returns to the position of the layer below.

Plate 3. HexOct-K: a layer that counts

Keyed HexOct tiles (DICTO's name), as Figure 14.3; tiles drawn as hexagons at their balls · Chapter 14, Section 8

  • Four keyed tiles, each holding a bit (gold 1, pale 0): the lower axis face reads the bit of the tile below in the layer, the right face takes a carry, the left face passes it on (new bit = old bit xor carry, carry out = old bit and carry).
  • With row 0 all zeros and a carry of 1 entering every row at the right, an exhaustive search finds exactly one filling (3 × 8, 4 × 16 and 5 × 20 windows); its rows are 0, 1, 2, … 15 in binary.
  • With keys on the four axis faces a HexOct-K layer is a Wang tiling of the square grid, so it can carry out any computation. Where the tiles interlock, each touches the layer below and the layer above at exactly three positions along the common diagonal (192 of 192 cases): the stack runs a cellular automaton, one step per layer, with the stacking itself as the clock.

Plate 4. 3C, the cubic stacking

ABC… · all layers c · h = 0 · six layers, each a 2 × 2 patch of its tile, λ = 1/10 · Chapters 1, 11, 14

  • A string of c-tiles only (gold; darker the tetrahedral pieces, lighter the octahedral halves). Its pieces are those of the octet of #51.
  • One of the two pure stackings from which, by the Rule of Halves, every piece of every stacking is borrowed, whole or in half.
  • BaRuO3 takes 3C at 18 GPa, the last step of the walk down h under pressure, 9R → 4H → 6H → 3C (Chapter 11).

Plate 5. 2H, the hexagonal stacking

AB… · all layers h · h = 1 · six layers, each a 2 × 2 patch of its tile, λ = 1/10 · Chapters 1, 11, 14

  • A string of h-tiles only (blue; darker the tetrahedral pieces, lighter the octahedral halves). Its pieces are those of the twinned octet of #56.
  • The other pure stacking of the Rule of Halves; with 3C it fixes the two ends of every straight line of the Hexagonality Law.
  • In hexagonal perovskites the face-sharing octahedra of 2H form endless chains (Chapter 11).

Plate 6. 4H

ABCB… (the double-hexagonal stacking, written ABAC in Chapter 1) · types h c h c · h = 1/2 · eight layers, λ = 1/10 · Chapters 1, 5, 8, 11

  • h- and c-tiles in turn (blue and gold). By the Rule of Halves every octahedral piece here is a Janus c/h piece, a cubic half and a hexagonal half.
  • Chucky, Fang and the Triskelion Tetrahedron (Plates 11 to 13) were found on this stacking (Chapter 1).
  • In the proof that the Swap Rule gives the Hexagonality Law, 4H is the test case: its four gaps give T = 2F(h, c) + 2F(c, h), which the Swap Rule makes 2F(c, c) + 2F(h, h) (Chapter 5).
  • β-cerium is double-hexagonal (h = 1/2), 1.2% larger per atom than cubic γ-cerium (Chapter 8); BaRuO3 takes 4H at 3 GPa (Chapter 11).

Plate 7. 6H

ABCACB… · types h c c h c c · h = 1/3 · six layers, λ = 1/10 · Chapters 1, 7, 8, 11

  • One h-tile after every two c-tiles. The six-layer stacking on which the walk first mixed the cubic and hexagonal pieces (Chapter 1).
  • The commonest polytype of silicon carbide; its hexagonality 1/3 is the ground-state filling of the supersymmetric lattice fermions of Fendley, Schoutens and de Boer (Chapter 7).
  • BaRuO3 takes 6H at 5 GPa (Chapter 11).

Plate 8. 9R, the samarium stacking

ABACACBCB… · types h h c, three times · h = 2/3 · nine layers, λ = 1/10 · Chapters 1, 11

  • Two h-tiles to every c-tile. The nine-layer samarium stacking added no piece beyond those of the mixed stackings before it, which pointed to the local Rule of Halves (Chapter 1).
  • BaRuO3 is 9R at ambient pressure (octahedra joined corner, face, face, repeated); its face-sharing octahedra form trimers (Chapter 11).

Plate 9. The Fibonacci stacking

16 layers ABACBCACBABCACBC (as Figure 7.1) · types in the order of the Fibonacci word, chcchchcchcch… · λ = 1/10 · Chapter 7

  • The types follow the substitution c → ch, h → c. In the never-repeating stacking the share of hexagonal layers is h = 1/φ² = (3 − √5)/2, and no two h-tiles are neighbours.
  • Of the four kinds of piece between neighbouring layers only three occur, cubic–cubic, cubic–hexagonal and hexagonal–cubic, in the proportions 1/φ³ : 1/φ² : 1/φ²; every kind of piece averages exactly V(cc) + (V(hh) − V(cc))/φ² per layer.

Plate 10. A random stacking

12 layers ACACBABABACB, drawn by coin toss · types c h h c c h h h h c c c · λ = 1/10 · Chapter 6

  • Each new layer takes either of the two positions not used by the layer below, with equal chance; the draw was repeated until the twelve layers close into a repeat, so that the stack can be computed as a crystal. This draw has 6 hexagonal layers of 12.
  • With equal chances (α = ½, completely random stacking) the expected hexagonality is h = 1/2. Wherever the Swap Rule holds, the totals of a random stacking are fixed by its share of hexagonal layers alone.
  • Whatever the order, the string is made of the same two tiles.

Plate 11. Chucky

DICTO's name · the octahedral piece at λ = −3/8 with a hexagonal layer above and a cubic layer below (ABAC) · Chapter 1

  • 19 corners; 3 triangles, 3 squares, 6 pentagons and one nonagon.
  • A Janus c/h piece: hexagonal top half (blue), cubic bottom half (gold). Volume 383√2/768, exactly the mean of the cubic rhombic dodecahedron √2/2 and the hexagonal piece 191√2/384.
  • With the cubic layer above and the hexagonal one below, the stacking gives its mirror image.

Plate 12. Fang

DICTO's name · the octahedral piece at λ = 5/24 with a hexagonal layer above and a cubic layer below (ABAC) · Chapter 1

  • 7 corners; 4 triangles and 3 squares.
  • A Janus c/h piece: hexagonal top half (blue), cubic bottom half (gold). Volume 5√2/324, exactly the mean of the cubic octahedron √2/81 and the hexagonal rhombohedron √2/54.
  • Chucky and Fang are the named members of the Janus c/h pieces; two further families, of 9 and of 27 corners, complete it.

Plate 13. Triskelion Tetrahedron (TT)

DICTO's name · the tetrahedral piece of ABAC at λ = 5/24 · Chapter 1

  • 16 corners; 4 triangles and 6 hexagons. Volume 79√2/324.
  • By the Rule of Halves it is the cubic tetrahedral piece at that weight, a member of the sliding family of Samson in #51; the name marks that member.
  • In every tile each tetrahedral piece is the cubic or the hexagonal tetrahedral piece named by its nearest layer (gold in Plates 1 and 2, there at λ = 1/10); TT is the cubic one at λ = 5/24.
