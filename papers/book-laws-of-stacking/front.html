Opening

Stack oranges in a crate and each new layer sits in the hollows of the one below. There are two ways to place it: straight above the layer two steps down, or in the third position, the one neither of the last two layers used. Repeat the first choice and the stack is hexagonal; repeat the second and it is cubic. Every mixture of the two is also a perfect packing, and nature uses many of them: silicon carbide, cobalt, ice, the oxides of batteries and the magnets of new electronics all come in such stackings.

This book follows three laws found in this one setting, in the order they were found, the two pieces they give, and the theorem that holds the three laws together. The Rule of Halves (Chapter 1): the gaps between the balls of any stacking are built from the gaps of the two pure stackings, whole or in halves. The Swap Rule (Chapters 3 and 4): when the layers are squashed or stretched, the two mixed pieces always add up to the two pure ones. The Hexagonality Law (Chapters 5 to 7): every count and every volume of the pieces is a straight line in one number, the share h of hexagonal layers. The second part tests the last law on real materials, from silicon carbide to spin ice, and measures for each how far it stands from the straight line. The third part, HexOct, turns back from the laws to the pieces: the space of every layer is one tile, and there are only two tiles, one cubic and one hexagonal, that stack in any order (Chapter 14). The fourth part, the Window Law (Chapter 15), holds the three laws in one theorem: whatever a stacking fixes through a window of g neighbouring layers averages to a linear function of the shares of its c/h words of length g. The Hexagonality Law is the window of one layer, and the Swap Rule is exactly what brings a count of two layers down to it. The fifth part ends the HexOct walk with Narcissus and Covenant (Chapter 16), and the sixth gathers further results: each ball's own cell, the rhombic dodecahedron or its turned twin (Chapter 17), and the interlocking of the HexOct layers, the spectrum of the Fibonacci stacking and the pieces of rock salt (Chapter 18).

The Hexagonality Law, defined

DICTO's Hexagonality Law is a law of structural crystallography: in every close-packed crystal structure, however its layers are stacked, every count of the structure (its pieces, holes, bonds and swaps) is a straight line in the structure's hexagonality, the crystallographers' share of hexagonal layers.

Its reach, stated exactly (Chapter 15). The counts of the law are totals per layer of what the layers hold: every count read from one layer, and every count of two neighbouring layers that obeys the Swap Rule. The pieces, holes, bonds and swaps of this book are of that kind, since each is borrowed, whole or in half, from one layer's pure stacking (the Rule of Halves) or changes only by swapping (the Swap Rule). A count that sorts sites by what two neighbouring layers do together, and breaks the Swap Rule, such as the octahedra of a perovskite with exactly 0, 1 or 2 shared faces, is not of this kind: the Window Law gives it exactly, as a plane in h and the share phh of neighbouring hexagonal pairs, with the Swap defect as the coefficient of phh.

How the rules fit together.

The Window Law always holds, for every window g: a count read from g neighbouring layers is a linear function of the shares of the c/h words of length g. The Rule of Halves and the Swap Rule decide which window a count needs. When a count needs only one layer, or two layers and obeys the Swap Rule, the Window Law reduces to the Hexagonality Law (g = 1), the straight line in hexagonality. Pair counts that break the Swap Rule are planes in (h, phh).

What this book will show

By the end of the book seven things will have been demonstrated, each with proof or with the published data that tests it.

(1) Finitely many pieces. However equal balls are stacked in close-packed layers, the space between and around them is shared out into pieces of finitely many kinds, every one borrowed, whole or in half, from the two pure stackings, cubic and hexagonal (the Rule of Halves, Chapter 1, and its pyrochlore form, Chapter 2).

(2) Change by swapping. When the layers are squashed, stretched or restacked, the pieces change only by swapping: the two mixed pieces always add up to the two pure ones (the Swap Rule, Chapters 3 and 4, proved in four classical crystal families).

(3) One straight line. For every piece fixed by its two neighbouring layers, the Swap Rule is equivalent to the Hexagonality Law: every count and every volume of the pieces is a straight line in the share h of hexagonal layers, for random stackings, faulted ones and even the never-repeating Fibonacci stacking (Chapters 5 to 7).

(4) Exact in real materials. The counts that decide real chemistry and physics obey the law exactly in real crystals: where hydrogen can sit in a metal, how many faces the octahedra of an oxide share, how many bonds of diamond, ice or a semiconductor are eclipsed, how many kagome planes pass through each atom of a magnet (Chapters 10 to 13).

(5) A measure of every material. Where a property is not a pure count, its distance from the straight line is one number for each material, the share of interactions that reach beyond the next layer. It sorts the materials from tin, on the line, to silicon carbide, far from it, and it marks where the law stops (Chapters 8 to 13).

(6) Two pieces, HexOct. Cut the space of a stacking layer by layer and every layer's share is one of only two tiles, the c-tile and the h-tile, placed by one motion; any tile sits on any tile, so every stacking, periodic or not, is a string of the two, and every string is a stacking (Chapter 14). With keys on their faces the tiles can carry out any computation.

(7) One frame, the Window Law. Every count read from g neighbouring layers is a linear function of the shares of the c/h words of length g, which have exactly 2g−1 free values. At one layer this is the Hexagonality Law; at two layers exactly one more number enters, the share phh of neighbouring hexagonal pairs, with the Swap defect as its coefficient, so a count of two layers is a straight line in h exactly when it obeys the Swap Rule. It is tested with exact counts on perovskites, Frank–Kasper phases and the approximants of quasicrystals, where the approximants carry zero defect on the chamber that holds the quasicrystal (Chapter 15).

The work began with building, in PET bottles and printed pieces, and grew through three open apps (Kaleidohedra, Rhombiverse and Polyhedraverse) into the papers gathered here. Each chapter is one paper, kept word for word in its theorems and proofs; repeated introductions are cut, and each chapter names the paper and DICTO DOI it comes from. The sources checked for every chapter are listed together at the end.
