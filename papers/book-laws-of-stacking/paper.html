Opening

Stack oranges in a crate and each new layer sits in the hollows of the one below. There are two ways to place it: straight above the layer two steps down, or in the third position, the one neither of the last two layers used. Repeat the first choice and the stack is hexagonal; repeat the second and it is cubic. Every mixture of the two is also a perfect packing, and nature uses many of them: silicon carbide, cobalt, ice, the oxides of batteries and the magnets of new electronics all come in such stackings.

This book follows three laws found in this one setting, in the order they were found, the two pieces they give, and the theorem that holds the three laws together. The Rule of Halves (Chapter 1): the gaps between the balls of any stacking are built from the gaps of the two pure stackings, whole or in halves. The Swap Rule (Chapters 3 and 4): when the layers are squashed or stretched, the two mixed pieces always add up to the two pure ones. The Hexagonality Law (Chapters 5 to 7): every count and every volume of the pieces is a straight line in one number, the share h of hexagonal layers. The second part tests the last law on real materials, from silicon carbide to spin ice, and measures for each how far it stands from the straight line. The third part, HexOct, turns back from the laws to the pieces: the space of every layer is one tile, and there are only two tiles, one cubic and one hexagonal, that stack in any order (Chapter 14). The fourth part, the Window Law (Chapter 15), holds the three laws in one theorem: whatever a stacking fixes through a window of g neighbouring layers averages to a linear function of the shares of its c/h words of length g. The Hexagonality Law is the window of one layer, and the Swap Rule is exactly what brings a count of two layers down to it.

The Hexagonality Law, defined

DICTO's Hexagonality Law is a law of structural crystallography: in every close-packed crystal structure, however its layers are stacked, every count of the structure (its pieces, holes, bonds and swaps) is a straight line in the structure's hexagonality, the crystallographers' share of hexagonal layers.

Its reach, stated exactly (Chapter 15). The counts of the law are totals per layer of what the layers hold: every count read from one layer, and every count of two neighbouring layers that obeys the Swap Rule. The pieces, holes, bonds and swaps of this book are of that kind, since each is borrowed, whole or in half, from one layer's pure stacking (the Rule of Halves) or changes only by swapping (the Swap Rule). A count that sorts sites by what two neighbouring layers do together, and breaks the Swap Rule, such as the octahedra of a perovskite with exactly 0, 1 or 2 shared faces, is not of this kind: the Window Law gives it exactly, as a plane in h and the share phh of neighbouring hexagonal pairs, with the Swap defect as the coefficient of phh.

How the rules fit together.

The Window Law always holds, for every window g: a count read from g neighbouring layers is a linear function of the shares of the c/h words of length g. The Rule of Halves and the Swap Rule decide which window a count needs. When a count needs only one layer, or two layers and obeys the Swap Rule, the Window Law reduces to the Hexagonality Law (g = 1), the straight line in hexagonality. Pair counts that break the Swap Rule are planes in (h, phh).

What this book will show

By the end of the book seven things will have been demonstrated, each with proof or with the published data that tests it.

(1) Finitely many pieces. However equal balls are stacked in close-packed layers, the space between and around them is shared out into pieces of finitely many kinds, every one borrowed, whole or in half, from the two pure stackings, cubic and hexagonal (the Rule of Halves, Chapter 1, and its pyrochlore form, Chapter 2).

(2) Change by swapping. When the layers are squashed, stretched or restacked, the pieces change only by swapping: the two mixed pieces always add up to the two pure ones (the Swap Rule, Chapters 3 and 4, proved in four classical crystal families).

(3) One straight line. For every piece fixed by its two neighbouring layers, the Swap Rule is equivalent to the Hexagonality Law: every count and every volume of the pieces is a straight line in the share h of hexagonal layers, for random stackings, faulted ones and even the never-repeating Fibonacci stacking (Chapters 5 to 7).

(4) Exact in real materials. The counts that decide real chemistry and physics obey the law exactly in real crystals: where hydrogen can sit in a metal, how many faces the octahedra of an oxide share, how many bonds of diamond, ice or a semiconductor are eclipsed, how many kagome planes pass through each atom of a magnet (Chapters 10 to 13).

(5) A measure of every material. Where a property is not a pure count, its distance from the straight line is one number for each material, the share of interactions that reach beyond the next layer. It sorts the materials from tin, on the line, to silicon carbide, far from it, and it marks where the law stops (Chapters 8 to 13).

(6) Two pieces, HexOct. Cut the space of a stacking layer by layer and every layer's share is one of only two tiles, the c-tile and the h-tile, placed by one motion; any tile sits on any tile, so every stacking, periodic or not, is a string of the two, and every string is a stacking (Chapter 14). With keys on their faces the tiles can carry out any computation.

(7) One frame, the Window Law. Every count read from g neighbouring layers is a linear function of the shares of the c/h words of length g, which have exactly 2g−1 free values. At one layer this is the Hexagonality Law; at two layers exactly one more number enters, the share phh of neighbouring hexagonal pairs, with the Swap defect as its coefficient, so a count of two layers is a straight line in h exactly when it obeys the Swap Rule. It is tested with exact counts on perovskites, Frank–Kasper phases and the approximants of quasicrystals, where the approximants carry zero defect on the chamber that holds the quasicrystal (Chapter 15).

Prologue. From the Octet to Every Stacking: the Web of Bonds

From paper #58, DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23665944

How cutting gaps in the right place, and watching which pieces touch, led to one rule for every way of stacking balls

Pile up oranges in a shop and there are small gaps between them. Those gaps come in two shapes, and the way the layers of oranges are stacked decides how the gaps are arranged. Over ten papers we asked a simple question again and again: if the gaps and the oranges' own spaces are shared out as solid pieces, what shapes do the pieces take, and which pieces touch which? The answer ends in one rule, the rule of halves: however the layers are stacked, every piece is borrowed, whole or in half, from just two ways of stacking.

The core statement

Plato built his elements from solids, Democritus from indivisible atoms, and some of today's physics pictures space itself as made of small polyhedral grains. The old question behind them is how far space can be shared out into pieces of a few fixed kinds. For stacked balls there is now an exact answer:

However layers of equal balls are stacked, the space between and around them is shared out into pieces of only finitely many kinds, and every one of them is borrowed, whole or in half, from just two pure orders, the cubic and the hexagonal. Endless variety of order, from a finite set of quanta.

This is geometry, proved exactly in paper #57; the link to atoms and to physics is one of lineage and analogy, not a claim about nature.

1. Pieces that fill space

DICTO came to the division of space through his hands, cutting and rebuilding PET-bottle caps into DICTOspheres. He narrowed the range to three spheres, each building its own crystal: the dodecahedron builds icosahedra and tetrahedra, the cuboctahedron builds cubes and rhombic dodecahedra, and the rhombic dodecahedron builds truncated octahedra and the octet. In Deltaverse he let deltahedra find their own lattice, where Rhombiverse had laid the lattice down first; in Polyhedraverse he cut rhombic dodecahedra in half and joined the halves into new solids, among them a slightly irregular truncated octahedron that still filled space, a forerunner of the golden split, and turned those cuts into the RHOMBIS puzzle. The apps became his playground, a place to pick up and turn solids freely; there he filled the gaps of dodecahedral and icosahedral structures with bidiminished and tridiminished icosahedra. He even planned spheres made in two halves, to bridge five-fold and four-fold symmetry. Cutting into halves and filling what is left run through every paper in this series; the rule of halves is where they meet.

Some shapes fill space on their own, like bricks or cubes. Most do not. DICTO's first question was whether any mix of the five Platonic solids (the tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron and icosahedron) can be made to fill space. Paper #47 showed that every mix can, all 31 of them, as long as a small set of seven connector pieces is allowed to plug the gaps.

The icosahedron, the twenty-sided die, is the most stubborn. Put icosahedra in a square grid and two kinds of gap are left over: a squashed icosahedron in the middle, which DICTO called the Unborn, and thin wedges between them, the AXE. The AXE sticks out wherever it is glued. In paper #48, the Golden Split, we cut each AXE into four small pyramids from one inside point and handed each pyramid to the neighbour it touches. At exactly one point, the golden point, nothing sticks out any more: the icosahedra and the Unborns both become neat, convex solids that fill space together. DICTO named them the Icosaxes and the Unborn Axes.

Paper #51, the Delian Bond, did the same with the oldest mixed filling of all: the octet, where tetrahedra and octahedra fill space together. Slide one flat cut through every place where a tetrahedron meets an octahedron, and give each side to its own piece. As the cut slides, one kind grows and the other shrinks, but the two always fill space. At one end the pieces are Kepler's rhombic dodecahedra, at the other end cubes. Halfway, at one exact place, the two pieces weigh the same: the small one has doubled and the large one halved. Doubling is the ancient Delian problem, doubling a cube, and DICTO named this equal pair Samson and Delilah.

2. The web of bonds

Next we stopped looking only at the pieces and looked at who touches whom. Draw a line, a bond, between the centres of every two pieces that share a face. The lines make a web. Paper #52 showed that both of DICTO's families above are really one fixed crowd of points of two kinds, with one number slid between the kinds, like turning a dial. Each piece is simply the region that belongs to its point. As the dial turns, the pieces change shape, but the web changes in only two ways. The bond between a piece of one kind and a piece of the other never breaks. The bonds between pieces of the same kind hand over (one pair of pieces stops touching as another pair starts): in the golden family slowly and smoothly, in the Delian family all at once, at the octet.

The dial has a physical meaning: it is the size of a ball. The golden family is the arrangement of atoms in caesium chloride, the Delian family that of fluorite, and the range where the balls can touch ends exactly at the limits Linus Pauling gave for these crystals.

DICTO guessed that these two ways of handing over would turn up elsewhere. Paper #53 turned the same dial on ten more crystal arrangements and recorded every change exactly. The guess holds: the all-at-once hand-over appears in the square grid with its centres, in the honeycomb and in rock salt; the smooth hand-over appears in zinc blende, in the gaps of the hexagonal stacking, and in two famous metal alloy structures, A15 and C15.

3. Every stacking: the rule of halves

Real crystals rarely stack in only one pure way. They mix: ABAC, ABCACB, and endless others. An early signpost was paper #40, where two short rules placed DICTO's Ogdoecontahedron and its family into the stack of tetrahedra and octahedra, layered in any order, even in a pattern that never repeats.

Paper #57 turned the dial on every stacking at once and found a rule simple enough to say in two sentences. A four-faced (tetrahedral) gap always takes the piece it would have in the pure stacking of the layer it sits closest to. An eight-faced (octahedral) gap sits halfway between two layers, so its piece is two halves: the top half borrowed from the layer above, the bottom half from the layer below. That is all. So every stacking, however long or strange, is built from the pieces of the two pure stackings, and there are only finitely many kinds of piece. Where a cubic layer meets a hexagonal one, pieces appear that are half of one and half of the other, which DICTO named the Janus pieces (after the Roman god who looks both ways), Chucky and Fang.

Chapter 1. The Rule of Halves: the Holes of Every Close-Packed Stacking

From paper #57, DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23647898

Theorem: in every close-packed stacking, along the whole weight walk, each tetrahedral hole's piece is the piece of the cubic or the hexagonal stacking named by its nearest layer, and each octahedral hole's piece is a top half and a bottom half, each the half of the cubic or the hexagonal piece named by the layer on that side; so the pieces of all stackings are finite in kind and an octahedral piece's volume is always the mean of its two parents' volumes.

Stack layers of balls and the gaps between them are small octahedra and tetrahedra. Stack them in the cubic way (ABCABC…) or the hexagonal way (ABAB…) and these gaps are arranged differently; crystals also use countless mixed stackings, such as ABAC or ABCACB. In papers #51, #53 and #56 we slid one number, a weight, between the two kinds of gap and followed how space is shared out among them: the web of bonds. Here we follow it on every stacking at once, and find a simple rule. A tetrahedral gap always takes the piece it would have in the pure stacking of the layer it sits closest to. An octahedral gap sits half-way between two layers, and its piece is two halves: the top half is the top half of the cubic or the hexagonal piece, as the layer above decides, and the bottom half likewise from the layer below. So every stacking, of any length or pattern, is built from the two pure ones, and the kinds of piece are finite: we list them all, with exact volumes. Where a cubic layer meets a hexagonal one, octahedral pieces appear that are half cubic and half hexagonal, which DICTO calls the Janus c/h pieces; two of them he named Chucky and Fang; and its volume is exactly the mean of its two parents'. The proof is exact, with one bound done by hand. The rule was not found in the sources checked; its classical counterpart for the balls themselves, that each ball's surroundings are set by its own layer, is the reason the h/c notation of crystallography exists.

1. Where it started

Paper #56 [1] followed the weight walk of #53 [2] on the hexagonal stacking and found DICTO's Sonsam and Lahdeli, Ian Dury and Blockhead, the hexagonal twins of the cubic pieces of #51 [3]. DICTO then proposed following the web of bonds onto the other close-packed stackings. On the double-hexagonal stacking ABAC and the six-layer ABCACB the walk mixed the cubic and hexagonal pieces and added two pieces neither had, which DICTO named Chucky and Fang; the nine-layer samarium stacking ABACACBCB added nothing new. That pointed to a local rule, which we prove here.

2. The set-up

Each gap gets a share of space; one weight decides how big a share the tetrahedral gaps get; slide it and the shares change shape.

Unit balls sit in close-packed layers (triangular lattices of spacing 1) at heights kd, d = √(2/3); layer k sits on one of the three positions A, B, C, and no two consecutive layers share a position. Layer k is h if layers k − 1 and k + 1 sit on the same position and c otherwise (Jagodzinski's notation [4]). In the gap between layer k (position X) and layer k + 1 (position Y) there are three holes (Figure 1.1): an octahedral hole over the third position Z at height (k + ½)d, a tetrahedral hole over X at height (k + ¾)d and one over Y at height (k + ¼)d. The holes are the sites of a power diagram [5]: each site p, with weight wp, owns the points x for which |x − p|² − wp is smallest; octahedral sites have weight 0 and tetrahedral sites weight λ. The pieces are convex and fill space; two pieces sharing a face are bonded. The walk is λ sliding over −11/24 ≤ λ ≤ 3/8, the range in which, in at least one of the two pure stackings, both kinds of piece are present (the hexagonal tetrahedral pieces shrink to points at −11/24, the cubic ones at −3/8, the octahedral pieces of both at 3/8). On each open stretch of λ between the finitely many stops, where some piece changes its faces, every corner of every piece moves on a straight line (Lemma 1). The pure stackings are the cubic ABC… (all layers c; the octet of #51) and the hexagonal AB… (all layers h; the twinned octet of #56).

Definitions. The walk: for each λ in the range we set wtet = λ, woct = 0 and take the power diagram. A piece: the cell of one hole. A stop: a λ at which some piece gains or loses a face; a stretch: an open interval of λ between consecutive stops. A window: the six layers k − 2, …, k + 3 round the gap of layers k and k + 1, up to relabelling A, B, C; it is fixed by the h/c types of layers k − 1, …, k + 2, so there are 16. The top and bottom halves of an octahedral piece: its parts above and below the horizontal plane through its hole.

3. Main theorem

Theorem (the rule of halves). For every close-packed stacking and every λ with −11/24 ≤ λ ≤ 3/8:
  1. the piece of a tetrahedral hole is congruent to a tetrahedral piece of the pure stacking (cubic if c, hexagonal if h) of the type of its nearest layer: the hole over X, at height (k + ¾)d, is nearest to layer k + 1, the hole over Y to layer k;
  2. the piece of an octahedral hole, cut by the horizontal plane through the hole, has a top half congruent to the top half of an octahedral piece of the pure stacking of the type of layer k + 1, and a bottom half congruent to the bottom half of an octahedral piece of the pure stacking of the type of layer k.
Corollaries. (i) Up to congruence there are finitely many pieces in all close-packed stackings together: 21 combinatorial kinds, listed in the Appendix with exact volumes. (ii) The volume of an octahedral piece is the mean of the volumes of its cubic and hexagonal parents (or equal to the parent's, when both layers have the same type).

The two pure stackings are the only ingredients; every other stacking just decides, layer by layer, which ingredient each half comes from.

4. Proof

Lemma 1 (corners move on straight lines). A corner of a piece is the common point of three dividing planes 2(qi − p)·x = |qi|² − |p|² − wqi + wp. Their normals do not depend on λ and their right-hand sides are affine in λ; on a stretch the same three planes define the corner, and their normals are linearly independent (otherwise they would not meet in a point), so the corner is M⁻¹b(λ), with M the fixed matrix of the three normals and b(λ) affine: affine in λ. At a stop the set of planes through a corner may change, which is why the stretches are treated one by one. □

Step 1: six layers decide. The pieces of the holes of gap k depend only on the layers k − 2, …, k + 3. (i) Exact computation. Up to relabelling A, B, C, these six layers are fixed by the types of the four inner layers k − 1, …, k + 2: 16 windows. For each we built a periodic stacking containing the six layers and computed its power diagram exactly, in rational arithmetic, over the whole walk (the census engine of #53 [2], which also checks on every stretch that its finite list of sites suffices and that the pieces' volumes add up to the cell's). In every window, on every stretch, every corner of every piece of gap k lies within squared distance ½ of its hole (176 cases; squared distance along a straight path is convex in λ, so the stretch ends suffice). (ii) By hand. A hole three or more gaps away is at least 2.5d higher or lower than every hole of gap k, so |q − p| ≥ 2.5d, and |wp − wq| ≤ 11/24. Its dividing plane lies at distance (|q − p|² + wp − wq)/(2|q − p|) ≥ ((2.5d)² − 11/24)/(5d) = (89/24)/(5d) from p (the function t ↦ (t² − c)/(2t) increases), whose square is (7921/576)/(50/3) = 23763/28800 > ½. So for every λ no such plane meets the ball of squared radius ½ about p. (iii) Convexity. Let C be the piece of p computed from the holes of gaps k − 2 to k + 2 only. In any stacking with the same six layers the piece of p is C cut by the planes of farther holes; these miss the ball, and by (i) the piece in the test stacking lies inside the ball; a convex body inside the ball whose boundary there is made of C's planes only is C itself. So all such stackings give the same piece. This holds for every stacking, periodic or not, since any stacking shows one of the 16 windows round each of its gaps. □

Step 2: the 16 windows. For each window, stretch and hole of gap k we searched the 24 rigid motions that fix the hole's vertical axis and map the triangular layers to themselves (the 12 symmetries of the triangular lattice about the axis, with or without the reflection z ↦ −z; they serve only to exhibit congruence). For tetrahedral holes, one such motion carries the corner functions (Lemma 1) of the piece exactly onto those of the stated pure piece: all cases. For octahedral holes, one motion carries the corner functions above, on and below the mid-plane, and the points where edges cross it (exact rational functions of λ), onto those of the stated pure halves: all 160 halves. Equal corner functions give equal pieces for every λ in the stretch. Together: 176 of 176 pieces, 0 failures; no stretch needed to be split where a corner crosses the mid-plane.

Step 3: stops. At a stop each piece is the limit of the pieces on the two adjacent stretches (the engine checks that the limit is the piece computed directly at the stop), so the congruences hold there too. □

The corollaries. (i) follows because the windows are finitely many and so are their stretches. (ii): each pure octahedral piece has a centre of symmetry at its hole (the octet's octahedral holes are centres of inversion, and so are the octahedral holes of hexagonal close packing), and inversion through the hole swaps the two halves, so each half has exactly half the parent's volume; by the theorem the top half of a mixed piece is congruent to the top half of one parent and its bottom half to the bottom half of the other, so its volume is half the one plus half the other. In particular, exactly: Fang = 5√2/324, the mean of the cubic octahedron √2/81 and the hexagonal rhombohedron √2/54; Chucky = 383√2/768, the mean of the cubic rhombic dodecahedron √2/2 and the hexagonal piece 191√2/384.

Limits. The theorem covers −11/24 ≤ λ ≤ 3/8. At the ends of the range, and at −3/8 for the cubic tetrahedral pieces, some pieces shrink to points or edges; there the statement holds with those pieces read as points, as limits of the stretches. Steps 1(i) and 2 are exact computations; Step 1(ii) and (iii) and Lemma 1 are by hand; no floating-point arithmetic enters the proof.

Computational methods. Python with exact rationals (fractions, sympy), using the census engine of #53 [2]: for a periodic stacking it lists the holes within a fixed distance, computes each piece exactly at a rational λ, finds the stretch round it exactly, and expresses every corner as an affine function of λ, with checks that the site list suffices and that the volumes add up to the cell. All scripts and outputs are in the study folder [S]; a fresh copy of the repository on a second computer reproduced every result of Steps 1 and 2 and the exact volumes identically.

5. The pieces

Chucky (DICTO's name): the octahedral piece at λ = −3/8 where a hexagonal layer lies above and a cubic layer below; 19 corners, with 3 triangles, 3 squares, 6 pentagons and one nonagon; volume 383√2/768. Fang (DICTO's name): the octahedral piece at λ = 5/24 in the same position; 7 corners, with 4 triangles and 3 squares; volume 5√2/324. Both belong to longer families of mixed pieces along their stretches (Appendix), and the stacking with the cubic layer above and the hexagonal one below gives their mirror images. DICTO calls all the octahedral pieces with one cubic and one hexagonal half the Janus c/h pieces (Janus, the two-faced god of doorways: each looks into one stacking above and the other below); Chucky and Fang are its named members, and two further families, of 9 and of 27 corners, complete it. Triskelion Tetrahedron (TT, DICTO's name): the tetrahedral piece of ABAC at λ = 5/24 with 16 corners (4 triangles, 6 hexagons). By the theorem it is the cubic piece there, a member of the sliding family of Samson in #51 [3]; the name marks that member; volume 79√2/324. The other pieces of the mixed stackings are the cubic and hexagonal pieces of #51 and #56; the two further Janus c/h families live on whole stretches and meet no stop.

6. What is known, and what was not found

Known and credited. Close-packed stackings and their polytypes, and the h/c notation, whose purpose is that each ball's own surroundings are set by its layer's type [4]; our theorem is the counterpart for the holes, along the whole walk. Chapter 2. DICTO Janus c/h Pyrochlore

From paper #60, DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23678750

The rule of halves of #57 carried to the pyrochlore network, its layers stacked in any order

The pyrochlore network is made of small tetrahedra that touch only at their corners. It hides inside every diamond-like crystal: take the middle of every bond, and the four middles round each atom make one of these tetrahedra. Diamond-like crystals can stack their layers in many orders, so the pyrochlore network can too. We share out all of space round it by one weight, as in #57, and prove the same rule: every piece is borrowed, whole or in half, from the pure cubic or the pure hexagonal stacking.

1. The idea

DICTO's idea (27 October 2026): "close packing halves rule pyrochlore network" — the rule of halves of #57 (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23647898) on the pyrochlore network, its layers (kagome and triangular, seen down a three-fold axis) stacked in any order. The series paper #58 (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23665944) tells the road to #57.

2. Main theorem

Theorem. In every close-packed stacking, and for every weight λ from −11/36 to 1/4, divide space by weight among the balls and diamond atoms (weight λ) and the two empty holes of each gap (weight 0). Then every piece is a piece of a pure stacking: a ball's piece is the ball piece of its own layer's pure stacking (cubic if the layer is c, hexagonal if h); a diamond atom's piece that of its nearest layer; and each hole piece, cut at its gap's mid-height, is a bottom half from the layer below and a top half from the layer above.

Where a cubic layer meets a hexagonal one, the hole pieces are therefore Janus c/h pieces, as in #57: a cubic half glued to a hexagonal half (in 4H, octahedral pieces of volume 0.346 and 0.325, beside 0.339 in the cubic and 0.332 in the hexagonal stacking). The tetrahedra of the network — the pieces of the balls and diamond atoms — are always whole, each from one layer; the Janus pieces are the network's open spaces.

2a. Proof

The proof follows #57 and uses its exact engine unchanged (rational arithmetic throughout).

Locality. Near any gap, a stacking is fixed by the cubic or hexagonal type of the four layers round it: sixteen windows. Every corner of every piece of the central gap lies within squared distance 11/32 of its site (exact, at the ends of every stretch; convexity). A site whose letter the window does not fix is at least 2¼ layer spacings away in height, and the weights differ by at most 11/36, so its dividing plane stays at squared distance at least ((27/8 − 11/36)/(2·√(27/8)))² ≈ 0.698 from the site: it cannot touch the piece. So each piece is the same in every stacking that shares its window.

Pieces along every stretch. On each stretch of the walk (stops −11/36, −1/4, −3/20, 0, 1/8, 1/6, 1/4) every corner of every piece moves on a straight line in λ. For each of the sixteen windows and each stretch, one symmetry of the layer carries the corners of each whole piece (ball, diamond atom) onto those of its pure piece, and the corners above, on and below the mid-plane together with the cut points (exact rational functions of λ) of each half (octahedral and Y holes) onto those of its pure half: 384 of 384. As an independent check, the pieces are congruent to their pure pieces at twelve exact weights covering every stretch: 768 of 768.

3. Construction

Balls of spacing 1 in close-packed layers √(2/3) apart, stacked by a word in A, B, C. The diamond-type crystal (as silicon carbide) is the balls plus #57's tetrahedral hole "over X at 3/4" above each ball. The middles of its bonds form the pyrochlore network: round each atom four middles make a regular tetrahedron of edge 1/2, and every corner belongs to exactly two tetrahedra (checked for 3C, 2H, 4H and 6H). Each gap holds four sites: the ball S, the diamond atom C, the octahedral hole O and the other tetrahedral hole Y; the two empty holes are exactly the network's open spaces.

4. The weight walk

Computed exactly with #57's census engine, unchanged (rational arithmetic; every built-in check passed).

Cubic (3C). Stops at −3/8 (the atoms' pieces vanish), −1/4 (atoms regular tetrahedra, holes truncated tetrahedra of volume 23√2/96: the classical tetrahedral–truncated tetrahedral honeycomb), +1/4 (the roles swap: atoms truncated tetrahedra, holes tetrahedra), and +3/8 (the holes' pieces vanish).

Hexagonal (2H). Stops at −11/36, −1/4, −3/20, 0, 1/8, 1/6 and 1/4. At −1/4 the diamond atoms' pieces are still regular tetrahedra but the balls' pieces have ten corners: the regular pyrochlore tetrahedra are exact pieces only on cubic layers.

Chapter 3. The Swap Rule

From paper #61, DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23682596

Ganesha, the Janus tetrahedra of squashed stackings, and the border at true close packing

Abstract. Stack layers of balls, cubic or hexagonal in any order, and share the space between the balls' holes by weight: each hole gets a piece. Paper #57 showed that at true close packing an octahedral piece is two halves, each borrowed from a pure stacking, while a tetrahedral piece is borrowed whole from its nearest layer. Here the layers are squashed or stretched. We prove that for every layer spacing from half the close-packed squash upwards, every hole piece, tetrahedral ones included, is a top half from the pure stacking of the layer above and a bottom half from the pure stacking of the layer below. Two consequences follow. The Swap Rule: the two mixed pieces always add up to the two pure ones. And a sharp border: the tetrahedral pieces are whole exactly when the layers are at least as far apart as in true close packing; squash them even slightly and each mixed tetrahedral piece gets its head from one side and its body from the other. DICTO calls these pieces Ganesha. Below half the squash the halves stop being pure, yet the Swap Rule for volumes still holds, down to a quarter of the squash exactly. The rule was found by DICTO's idea of looking for simple rules in pictures of computed values; a map of eleven spacings shows where each rule holds.

1. The idea

DICTO's idea (27 October 2026): the rule of halves of #57 (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23647898) was spotted in a picture of computed values; other such pictures may hide other simple rules (halves, thirds, ...). A rule finder was built to look for them (Splash Rules, study 2026-10-27-splash-rules). Its first run, on the #57 walk with the layer spacing changed, found a rule nobody had looked for: when the layers are squashed, the mixed tetrahedral pieces stop being whole, yet their volumes still add up exactly. DICTO named the rule the Swap Rule and the new pieces Ganesha, after the god whose head was swapped.

2. Set-up

Balls of spacing 1 lie in triangular layers at positions A, B, C, stacked by a word with no letter twice in a row; the layer spacing is d, with d² = s·⅔ (s = 1 is true close packing). Each gap between a layer X and the next layer Y holds three holes per cell: an octahedral hole over the third position at mid-gap, a tetrahedral hole over X at ¾ of the gap and one under Y at ¼. As in #57 the octahedral holes get weight 0 and the tetrahedral holes weight λ, and space is divided by power distance (a Laguerre or power diagram); each hole's share is its piece. A layer is c (cubic) if its two neighbours differ, h (hexagonal) if they are equal; the pure stackings are ABC (all c) and AB (all h).

3. Main theorem

Theorem. Let s ≥ ½. Put λ₋(c) = −¼ − s/8, λ₋(h) = −⅓ − s/8 and U(s) = min(⅓ + s/24, 3s/8). In every close-packed stacking, periodic or not, take any hole piece of a gap whose lower layer has type t and upper layer type t′, at any λ with max(λ₋(t), λ₋(t′)) ≤ λ ≤ U(s). Cut it by the horizontal mid-plane of its gap. The part below is congruent to the lower half of the piece of the same kind in the pure stacking of type t, and the part above to the upper half of that piece in the pure stacking of type t′. This holds for all three kinds of hole piece.

The weight range contains the whole walk of the cubic stacking for every s ≥ ½, and the whole walk of every stacking for s ≥ 1.

Corollary 1 (the Swap Rule). Write V(t|t′) for the volume of a piece over a t layer and under a t′ layer. Then, for all three kinds of piece,
V(c|h) + V(h|c) = V(c|c) + V(h|h).
Proof: V(t|t′) = L(t) + R(t′) with L and R the pure lower and upper half volumes; both sides equal L(c) + L(h) + R(c) + R(h).

Corollary 2 (the border at true close packing). For s ≥ 1 the far half of a tetrahedral piece is the same over a c layer as over an h layer, so the piece is borrowed whole from its nearest layer, as in #57. For every s with ½ ≤ s < 1 there is a weight in the cubic walk where the far half over a c layer is a proper part of the far half over an h layer, with strictly smaller volume: the mixed tetrahedral pieces are then Janus c/h pieces in their own right. These are the Ganesha pieces.

Corollary 3 (4 : 5 : 6). At true close packing, for 5/24 < λ < 3/8 the three octahedral pieces (cubic, Janus, hexagonal) have volumes in the exact ratio 4 : 5 : 6 and vanish together at λ = 3/8. Near the end of the walk each octahedral piece is cut out by its tetrahedral neighbours only. In the cubic stacking it has 8 of them, one on each face, and is a regular octahedron |x| + |y| + |z| ≤ 1 (volume 4/3 at that scale); in the hexagonal stacking the octahedral holes sit face to face along the stacking axis, only 6 tetrahedral neighbours remain, and the piece is the rhombohedron |x + y − z|, |x − y + z|, |−x + y + z| ≤ 1 (volume 2). Hence 4 : 6, and the Janus piece, half of each, gives 5.

4. The map

The map was made by the rule finder: each piece is labelled by the layer pattern around it, the shortest label that fixes its exact volume is found, and each volume is fitted exactly as a combination of the pure ones. At every spacing tested from 15/16 down to ¼ the Swap Rule appears, so it switches on as soon as the layers come closer than true close packing; the theorem proves this for s ≥ ½.

5. Proof

One polyhedron for everything. Use lateral coordinates x, y, the height ζ in layer spacings and q = d² = 2s/3, and put u = q·ζ. Then every power-distance comparison between two holes becomes a linear inequality in the five variables (q, λ, x, y, u). So a piece, its cut at the mid-plane (u ≤ q/2) and the whole range of spacings and weights (q ≥ ⅓, λ₋ ≤ λ ≤ U, all linear in q) form one convex polyhedron in five dimensions.

Locality, for all spacings at once. The lower half of a piece is fixed by the holes of its own gap and of the gap below, which depend only on the type t of the lower layer. That no other hole of any stacking cuts into it is a linear statement on the polyhedron, proved by one exact Farkas certificate per outside hole: 1070 certificates, all checked in exact rational arithmetic, covering every s ≥ ½ (s → ∞ included) and every λ in range. Lateral and vertical domination arguments reduce the outside holes to finite lists. The upper half follows by the mirror ζ → 1 − ζ.

Range of the walk. Summing bisector inequalities against neighbours whose weighted offsets cancel shows when a piece must be empty; this gives the bounds λ₋ and U (four cases, symbolic in q and λ).

Border. For q ≥ ⅔ (s ≥ 1), 38 certificates show that the holes of the gap below never reach a tetrahedral piece's far half, so it is the same for c and h. For ½ ≤ s < 1, six holes present only below a c layer cut the h far half (the cutting function is 1/12 or 1/6 at s = ½, 0 at s = 1, and concave), twelve holes present only below an h layer do not cut the c far half, and interior points are exhibited: the c far half is a proper part of the h far half.

Sharpness. At s = ½ exactly, the octahedral hole of the next gap first reaches past the mid-plane (the cutting function is 0 there and positive just below). At s = 2/5 the exact engine finds halves that are not the pure ones, and above U(s) (s = ¾, λ = 5/16) the halves rule and the Swap Rule both fail. As an independent check, the engine of #57, rebuilding whole stackings from scratch, agrees with the theorem in 4992 of 4992 exact comparisons (six stackings, s = ½, 5/8, ¾, 1, 3/2, 2).

6. Below half the squash

Below s = ½ the halves are no longer pure, yet the Swap Rule for volumes still holds.

Theorem 2. For ¼ ≤ s < ½ and every λ up to 3s/8, in every close-packed stacking, each hole piece has volume V(t|t′) = A(t) + B(t′): one term fixed by the lower layer's type, one by the upper layer's. Hence the Swap Rule V(c|h) + V(h|c) = V(c|c) + V(h|h) holds for every s ≥ ¼. The bound ¼ is exact.

Why. Below s = ½ the octahedral hole of the next gap reaches across the mid-plane and carves a fixed piece D out of the lower part of this gap's octahedral and tetrahedral pieces, but only when the layer it shares is cubic. D lies against the mid-plane, out of reach of every hole of the gap below that differs between a cubic and a hexagonal layer, so it is the same piece whatever the far layer is. The lower part of a piece is therefore the pure lower half minus D when the upper layer is c; by the mirror, the upper part is the pure upper half minus a second fixed piece when the lower layer is c. Each correction is one constant per cubic layer, so the volume stays a sum of one share per layer. The halves fail only because D sits on one side of the mid-plane: in the pure cubic stacking every piece loses it, in a mixed one only some do. In exact numbers, at s = 2/5 and λ = −29/100 the octahedral pieces move δ = 2/3125 across the mid-plane inside the same piece, and the two mixed tetrahedral pieces carry +7/37500 and −7/37500, which cancel in the sum.

Proof. The same five-variable polyhedron as in §5, now for q from 1/6 to ⅓: 1706 exact Farkas certificates show that no other hole cuts a lower part and that, on each carved region, every hole that differs between c and h is inactive. At s = ¼ exactly, every hole two gaps away stops cutting (13 critical cases; the cutting function is 0 at q = 1/6 and changes sign there). At s = 1/5 the exact engine shows octahedral volumes depending on all four layers round the gap and tetrahedral ones on three, so the two-type rule fails. At s = 1/6 the pieces reach two gaps and the layer-sum rules of Figure 3.2 take over.

7. Prior art and credit

Power diagrams are affine in the weights, and absorbing the layer spacing into the weights (u = q·ζ) is that standard fact used once more; Farkas certificates are textbook linear programming. The rule of halves and the Janus c/h pieces Chucky and Fang are DICTO's #57. No source checked states a halves rule or a Swap Rule for hole pieces, or treats the layer spacing as a parameter of the hole power diagram.

8. Conclusion

True close packing is a border. At or above it, the holes of any stacking are shared out by the rule of halves of #57, and the tetrahedral pieces are whole. Below it, down to half the squash, every hole piece is a top from one pure stacking and a bottom from the other; the mixed tetrahedra become Ganesha pieces, and the two mixed pieces always add up to the two pure ones. Further down, to a quarter of the squash, the halves give way but the sums do not. However the layers are stacked, the space is still built from the halves of two pure orders. Chapter 15 gives the rule its exact weight: a count fixed by two neighbouring layers is a straight line in the hexagonality exactly when it obeys the Swap Rule, and where it does not, the failure F(c, c) + F(h, h) − F(c, h) − F(h, c), the Swap defect, is the coefficient of the one further number the count needs.

The Swap Rule is not a property of one packing. 2. Stackings, layers and pieces

Close-packed layers of equal balls sit in three positions A, B, C, and each new layer sits on the one below in one of the two other positions, so a stacking is a word such as ABC (cubic, 3C), AB (hexagonal, 2H) or ABCB (4H). Each layer is cubic (c) when its two neighbours differ and hexagonal (h) when they are the same: Jagodzinski's notation. In a stacking with period n, nc layers are cubic and nh hexagonal; the hexagonality is nh/n. Every gap between two layers holds a ball site at its foot, two tetrahedral sites and one octahedral site. A crystal family fills some of these sites with atoms, and space is shared out among all sites by weight, in a power diagram, as in #57 and #64: atoms of one kind have weight λ (zinc blende's second atom λ + μ), empty holes weight 0. Each site's cell is a piece, and its role is its kind of site (ball, octahedral, one of the tetrahedral sites; for the Laves phases the six sites of their net, #64). Everything is exact in rational arithmetic, with volumes as polynomials in λ.

3. The Hexagonality Law

Theorem (Hexagonality Law). Let a role have, in the gap between layers of types x (below) and y (above), a piece of volume F(x, y), the same in every stacking. Then the following are equivalent:
(i) the Swap Rule F(c, h) + F(h, c) = F(c, c) + F(h, h);
(ii) in every stacking, the total volume of the role over one period is T = nc·F(c, c) + nh·F(h, h).
So each layer holds, in every stacking, exactly what it holds in the pure stacking of its own type, and the total per layer is the straight line F(c, c) + (nh/n)·(F(h, h) − F(c, c)) in the hexagonality.

Proof. (i) ⇒ (ii). Put g(c) = 0, g(h) = F(c, h) − F(c, c) and f(x) = F(x, c). Then F(x, y) = f(x) + g(y) for all four pairs: for (x, c) by definition, for (c, h) by definition, and for (h, h) by the Swap Rule, since f(h) + g(h) = F(h, c) + F(c, h) − F(c, c) = F(h, h). Summing over the n gaps of one period, gap k between layers k and k + 1, gives T = Σk f(tk) + Σk g(tk+1) = Σk (f + g)(tk), because the period closes up. As (f + g)(c) = F(c, c) and (f + g)(h) = F(h, h), T = ncF(c, c) + nhF(h, h). (ii) ⇒ (i). The stacking ABCB (4H) has layer types alternating h, c, so its four gaps are (h, c), (c, h), (h, c), (c, h) and T = 2F(h, c) + 2F(c, h); (ii) gives T = 2F(c, c) + 2F(h, h). ∎

Two kinds of layer. In cadmium chloride and cadmium iodide the octahedral sites are filled in every other gap only, so the layers come in two kinds, alternating. The same proof, with f and g allowed to depend on the kind, gives T = f(c)nc′ + f(h)nh′ + g(c)nc″ + g(h)nh″, where ′ and ″ count the layers of each kind: the law holds for the hexagonality of each kind of layer separately. A single hexagonality is not enough there: it fails exactly for the stackings ABCB and ABCBAB, the two in which the two kinds of layer have different hexagonalities.

4. Physics

In the physics of stacking variants (polytypes), a property of a crystal is written as a sum over its layers, with terms for each layer alone and for pairs of layers further apart: the axial next-nearest-neighbour Ising (ANNNI) picture of Heine and coworkers for silicon carbide (Cheng, Needs and Heine, 1988). In that language the Hexagonality Law says that for the volume of every piece the pair terms are exactly zero: each layer contributes alone. Straight-line laws in the hexagonality are classical in measurement. The indirect band gap of the silicon carbide polytypes rises with the hexagonality, roughly in a straight line (Choyke, Hamilton and Patrick, 1964), and basal lattice constants of metallic polytypes correlate linearly with it; later work finds these laws approximate. The Hexagonality Law is an exact geometric law of the same form, for the space each atom and hole occupies. We make no claim that it explains the measured laws; it is a candidate for an exact part of them. One further echo, of form only: causal dynamical triangulations build spacetime in slabs between slices, from simplices typed by how they meet the slice above and the slice below, as our pieces are typed by the layers above and below their gap.

5. Conclusion

Space in flat stackings is shared out into a few kinds of pieces built from the tetrahedron and the octahedron, whole or in halves, and when a stacking changes, the pieces change by swapping, never by appearing from nothing. For the stackings the last clause is now a theorem: the Swap Rule is exactly the statement that every layer keeps, in every stacking, what it holds in its own pure stacking, so every kind of piece totals a straight line in the hexagonality. It holds in every crystal family we have computed, and in cadmium iodide for each kind of layer. Chapter 15 sets this theorem in a wider frame, the Window Law: whatever a stacking fixes through g neighbouring layers averages to a linear function of the shares of its c/h words of length g, and the Swap Rule is exactly what brings a two-layer count down to one layer.

Abstract. 2. Setting

As in #57, #64 and #66: close-packed layers in positions A, B, C; a layer is cubic (c) when its two neighbours differ and hexagonal (h) when they are the same (Jagodzinski); the sites of each gap (a ball site, two tetrahedral sites, an octahedral site) share space by weight in a power diagram, atoms with weight λ, empty holes 0. 3. Any stacking

Theorem 1. Let a role satisfy the Swap Rule. For any sequence of N consecutive layers of any stacking, periodic or not, the total volume T of that role's pieces in the N − 1 gaps between them satisfies |T − (ncF(c, c) + nhF(h, h))| ≤ 2·max|f| + 2·max|g|, where nc, nh count the cubic and hexagonal layers. Hence, in any stacking whose share of hexagonal layers tends to h, the average volume per layer tends to F(c, c) + h·(F(h, h) − F(c, c)), whatever the order of the layers and whatever the correlations between them.

Proof. With F(x, y) = f(x) + g(y), the gaps between layers 1, …, N give T = Σk<N f(tk) + Σk>1 g(tk) = Σk (f + g)(tk) − g(t1) − f(tN), and (f + g)(c) = F(c, c), (f + g)(h) = F(h, h). The two end terms are bounded; dividing by N gives the limit. ∎

So the total needs only h. The split of the pieces among the four kinds cc, ch, hc and hh does depend on how neighbouring layers are correlated, and the Markov descriptions of disordered stackings (Hart, Hansen and Kuhs, 2021) give it; the totals are independent of all of that.

Where h is measured. In crystals of nearly hard colloidal spheres the layers are often randomly stacked (random hexagonal close packing): each new layer takes either of the two positions not used by the layer below, with probability α for the cubic choice; α = ½ is completely random stacking, and then h = ½ (Pusey et al., 1989; later work discusses whether faulted-twinned cubic crystals are the stable form, Kegel and Dhont, 2000, and how such crystals age, Martelozzo et al., 2002). In ice frozen from supercooled water the layers are cubic and hexagonal in random order, stacking-disordered ice Isd (Malkin et al., 2012), whose cubicity 1 − h is measured by diffraction: 73.3% in the most cubic bulk sample (Malkin et al., 2015), 78% in 20 nm particles (Amaya et al., 2017). 5. Hard-core fermions

Read a hexagonal layer as a particle on a ring of sites, one site per layer. A stacking in which no two hexagonal layers touch, the Fibonacci stacking among them, is then a configuration of particles with hard cores: never two on neighbouring sites. These are exactly the configurations of the supersymmetric lattice fermions of Fendley, Schoutens and de Boer (2003), whose Hamiltonian is H = {Q+, Q−} with fermions that may not sit next to each other. On the periodic chain of N sites their ground states have int((N + 1)/3) fermions, a filling of 1/3, two of them when N is a multiple of 3. We counted, for every N from 2 to 15, the hard-core configurations by particle number: they agree with theirs (for N = 6: 1, 6, 9 and 2 with 0 to 3 particles, total 18), and so does their Witten index, 2(−1)p for N = 3p and (−1)p otherwise.

A crystal adds two conditions, because a periodic stacking must close: the step signs close only for an even number of hexagonal layers, so stackings lie in the sector of even particle number, and the layer positions close only when the steps add up to a multiple of 3. For N = 6 the closing configurations are the cubic stacking (no particle) and the three positions of 6H (two particles, h c c h c c); and 6H, the commonest polytype of silicon carbide, has hexagonality 1/3, the ground-state filling of the supersymmetric chain, in the same particle-number sector. The two pictures differ in their physics: the supersymmetric fermions hop and repel, while by the Hexagonality Law the volume each hexagonal layer carries does not depend on the others. We record the correspondence of configurations and counts only; the Fibonacci stacking's density 1/φ² ≈ 0.382 lies between the ground-state filling 1/3 and the hard-core limit 1/2.

6. Conclusion

The Fibonacci stacking is the never-repeating crystal of close-packed layers in its most ordered form. Its share of hexagonal layers is 1/φ², its hexagonal layers never touch, and its pieces come in the golden proportions 1/φ³ : 1/φ² : 1/φ². By the Hexagonality Law the golden ratio then enters every average piece volume exactly. One kit of pieces, borrowed from the two repeating stackings, builds the never-repeating one: the bridge between the two orders that DICTO set out to find, here at the level of the pieces themselves. Its hexagonal layers never touch, so its share phh of neighbouring hexagonal pairs is zero, and every count of two neighbouring layers, Swap Rule or not, is read on a straight line in h; Chapter 15 carries this to the repeating approximants of quasicrystals, window after window.

Silicon carbide, silicon, germanium and cerium against the straight line

Abstract. The Hexagonality Law (#66) says that in ideal stackings every kind of piece totals a straight line in the share h of hexagonal layers. DICTO proposed to test it against real crystals. In the language physicists use for stackings, the axial Ising (ANNNI) model, we prove that a property is a straight line in h exactly when its couplings between layers two and three apart vanish, J2 = J3 = 0, and that its stacking faults then stand intrinsic : extrinsic : twin = 2 : 2 : 1, the ratios of #67. We then read published data. The computed volumes of the silicon carbide polytypes lie on the line within the precision of the published numbers; the measured ones scatter between laboratories by more than the whole effect. 1. From the ideal to the real

The Hexagonality Law was proved for the pieces of ideal stackings, where space is shared by weight among atoms and holes at fixed positions (#66), and #67 drew its consequences for random stackings and faults. DICTO proposed the next step: to look at real crystals. Real crystals relax: their layers move apart or together a little, and their atoms shift. What matters is how close real stackings come to the straight line, and what the departures mean. We use only published numbers, and say for each how precise it is.

2. The straight line in the physicists' language

A stacking is a sequence of steps σi = ±1 from layer to layer (Hägg); a layer is cubic when the steps on either side agree and hexagonal when they differ, so the hexagonality is h = (1 − ⟨σiσi+1⟩)/2. In the axial next-nearest-neighbour Ising (ANNNI) model of Heine and coworkers a property X of a stacking, per atom pair and relative to the cubic stacking 3C, is written with couplings J1, J2, J3 between layers one, two and three apart. With the convention of Keller et al. (2023), checked against their energies: X(2H) = 2(J1 + J3), X(4H) = J1 + 2J2 + J3, X(6H) = (2/3)(J1 + 2J2 + 3J3).

Proposition. (i) A property X of the polytypes 3C, 6H, 4H and 2H is a straight line in h, X = s·h, exactly when J2 = J3 = 0 (and then J1 = s/2). (ii) For such a property the stacking faults of a cubic crystal stand X(ISF) : X(ESF) : X(TSF) = 2 : 2 : 1, where, in the same model, X(ISF) = 4(J1 + J2 + J3), X(ESF) = 4(J1 + 2J2 + 2J3) and X(TSF) = 2(J1 + 2J2 + 3J3).

Proof. (i) The three equations have the matrix with rows (2, 0, 2), (1, 2, 1), (2/3, 4/3, 2), of determinant 16/3 ≠ 0, so the couplings are fixed by the three values. X = s·h gives X(2H) = s, X(4H) = s/2, X(6H) = s/3, which J1 = s/2, J2 = J3 = 0 satisfies, and by uniqueness only it. Conversely J2 = J3 = 0 gives 2J1, J1, (2/3)J1, which is s·h with s = 2J1. (ii) With J2 = J3 = 0 the three fault values are 4J1, 4J1 and 2J1. ∎

So the ideal pieces of #66, which obey the law, have only a nearest coupling, and #67's fault ratios (a twin turns one layer hexagonal, an intrinsic and an extrinsic fault two each) are this 2 : 2 : 1. In a real crystal, J2 and J3 measure how far a property bends away from the line.

5. Band gaps

The band gap of the silicon carbide polytypes rises with the hexagonality (Choyke, Hamilton and Patrick, 1964): about 2.36 eV (3C), 3.02 (6H), 3.23 (4H) and 3.33 (2H). 6. A metal: cerium

At room temperature cerium exists both as cubic γ-cerium (fcc, a = 5.1612 Å, h = 0) and as double-hexagonal β-cerium (dhcp, ABAC, a = 3.6811 Å, c = 11.857 Å, h = 1/2). With two stackings a line cannot be tested, but the size of the change from cubic to half hexagonal is thirty times that of silicon carbide or silicon.

7. Conclusion

In the language of stacking physics the Hexagonality Law is the statement that a property has only a nearest coupling between layers, and its fault ratios are then exactly 2 : 2 : 1. Real crystals follow the straight line to within their data in silicon carbide and to about 0.04% in silicon and germanium, whose small bend is a relaxation coupling of the size of the nearest one; germanium's fault energies are close to 2 : 2 : 1, silicon's are not; band gaps bend much more. The law's straight line is the ideal part of every stacking, and the departures of real crystals from it are what they add. In the terms of Chapter 15, with J3 = 0 a property is the plane X = (2J1 + 4J2)h − 4J2phh in h and the share phh of neighbouring hexagonal pairs: J2 is the Swap defect (Δ = −4J2), and J3 needs a window of three layers.

Entropy and pressure

Abstract. The Hexagonality Law (#66) says that in ideal stackings every kind of piece is a straight line in the share h of hexagonal layers; in the physicists' layer model this is a property with only a nearest term (#68). Alloys and hydrogen

Abstract. Hydrogen is the enemy of titanium implants and of the zirconium tubes that hold nuclear fuel: it gathers in the metal and turns it brittle. We show that the places hydrogen can sit follow the Hexagonality Law (#66) exactly. 1. Why alloys

Papers #67–#70 tested the Hexagonality Law on stackings of single elements and simple compounds. Engineering metals are alloys, and their most important stacking events are faults: the turning of a few layers from hexagonal to cubic or back. DICTO proposed to test the law there, starting from the titanium alloys Ti-6Al-4V (aircraft, implants, printed parts) and Ti-Nb-Zr (low-stiffness implants), and to include their spherical powders. We added four families in which cubic against hexagonal stacking is the main story: the long-period stacking (LPSO) magnesium alloys, Fe–Mn and high-entropy alloys, cobalt alloys, and the zirconium alloys of nuclear fuel cladding. A powder bed or a printed part is made of grains; inside each close-packed grain the results below apply unchanged.

2. Hydrogen sites

Take balls of diameter a in close-packed layers A, B, C, any stacking, and let h be the share of hexagonal layers (a layer is hexagonal when the layers above and below it sit in the same position). Every stacking has 2 tetrahedral and 1 octahedral hole per ball (#66).

Theorem. Per ball, in any close-packed stacking: (i) the pairs of tetrahedral holes sharing a face number h; (ii) the pairs of octahedral holes sharing a face number h. Consequently (iii) if two hydrogen atoms cannot occupy one face-sharing tetrahedral pair, at most 2 − h tetrahedral sites per ball can be filled, and (iv) a hydride MHx with its hydrogen in tetrahedral sites needs local hexagonality h ≤ 2 − x.

Proof. (i) A tetrahedral hole has its base on a triangle of one layer and its apex on a ball of the next. Two tetrahedra can share a face only across a layer i, on the same triangle, one with its apex in layer i − 1 and one in layer i + 1. For each ball of layer i there is exactly one triangle of i that lies under a ball of i + 1, and its partner below exists exactly when layer i − 1 sits where i + 1 sits, that is when layer i is hexagonal. So each hexagonal layer gives one pair per ball and each cubic layer none: h pairs per ball. (ii) The octahedral holes between two layers sit over the third position, the one neither layer uses. The holes between i − 1 and i and between i and i + 1 share a face of layer i exactly when they sit over the same position, that is when i − 1 and i + 1 coincide: again one pair per ball of each hexagonal layer. (iii) Each pair loses one of its two sites, and the pairs are disjoint (each tetrahedron has one base layer), so 2 − h remain. (iv) follows. ∎

We checked (i) and (ii) on explicit coordinates for six stackings (3C, 2H, 4H, 6H and the 10H and 14H of §3), periodic in all three directions; all counts are exact. The same run finds the tetrahedral pairs that share only an edge (centres 0.707a apart) at 6 − 3h per ball, also a straight line (computed, not proved here).

A face-sharing tetrahedral pair has its centres 0.408a apart: 1.20 Å in α-titanium (a = 2.95 Å), 1.32 Å in α-zirconium (a = 3.23 Å), far inside the 2.1 Å that hydrogen atoms in metals keep apart (Switendick's criterion), so (iii) applies to both. The edge-sharing pairs are 2.28 Å apart in zirconium (allowed) and 2.09 Å in titanium (at the limit; no claim made). 1. Why oxides

Papers #67–#71 tested the Hexagonality Law on elements, simple compounds and alloys: there the atoms themselves are the close-packed balls. In most oxides the oxygen ions are the close-packed layers and the metal ions sit in the holes between them, mostly in octahedra. Whether two neighbouring octahedra share a corner, an edge or a face then depends only on the stacking of the oxygen layers, and Pauling's third rule says the face-sharing ones cost energy, because their metal ions come close. DICTO proposed that three-dimensional stacking rules (the Hexagonality Law, the Rule of Halves, the Swap Rule) can stand in for rules of thumb used in chemical modelling. Here we test the first of them on two families where the stacking is the main story: the hexagonal perovskites, and the layered oxides that hold the lithium in rechargeable batteries.

2. The count

Let h be the share of hexagonal layers in the stacking of the close-packed oxygen (or AO3) layers: a layer is hexagonal when the layers above and below it sit in the same position, A, B or C. An octahedral hole between two layers sits on the third position, the one neither layer uses.

Theorem. (i) In a perovskite ABO3 built of close-packed AO3 layers with B in the octahedra formed by oxygen alone, each B octahedron shares a face with 2h neighbours and a corner with 6(1 − h) neighbours, for any stacking. (ii) In a layered oxide LiMO2 (oxygen layers in pairs around the M layers, lithium in the gaps), the face-sharing lithium–metal pairs number 2h per lithium site; a gap bounded by two cubic layers (O3 type) has none, a gap bounded by two hexagonal layers (O1 type) has two, a mixed gap (O2 type) has one.

Proof. Take one oxygen layer and the octahedra just above and just below it. Each sits on the position missing from its own two layers. If the layer is hexagonal, the layers above and below it are on the same position, so the two octahedra are on the same position too and share the three oxygen atoms of the layer between them: a face. If the layer is cubic, the two octahedra are on different positions and meet in an edge (layered oxides) or, in the perovskite, where only one hole in four is filled, in single corners with three neighbours. Each B octahedron of a perovskite has one layer above and one below; each lithium gap has one bounding layer on each side. Averaging over the stacking gives 2h and 6(1 − h). ∎ The counts were checked from explicit coordinates for 3C, 2H, 4H, 6H and 9R perovskites and for the O3, O1, O2 and H1-3 layered stackings (study files perovskite_counts.py, cathodes.py).

4. Lithium cobalt oxide on charge

LiCoO2, the cathode of most phone batteries, has cubic O3 stacking (h = 0): every lithium octahedron shares only edges with the cobalt octahedra. On charge the lithium leaves. Motohashi et al. measured the full series: O3 down to x = 0.35, then the H1-3 phase at x = 0.12, then CoO2 with O1 stacking (h = 1) at x = 0. H1-3 has alternating O3-type and O1-type gaps (h = 1/2), and the lithium sits only in the O3-type gaps (Van der Ven et al. predicted this staging; Motohashi et al. confirm the structure). By Theorem (ii) the face-sharing count per gap runs 0, 2, 0, 2, …: H1-3 puts all its face-sharing into the gaps it leaves empty.

Proposition (lithium bound). If lithium occupies only gaps in which it shares no face with a metal octahedron, then x ≤ 1 − hgap, where hgap is the share of O1-type gaps. All stable phases in Motohashi et al.'s series obey it: O3 (hgap = 0, x ≤ 1), H1-3 (1/2, x = 0.12), O1 (1, x = 0).

This is the oxide counterpart of the hydride bound of #71 (h ≤ 2 − x): in both, the number of guest atoms is limited by the faces the stacking creates. One known structure breaks it: O2-LiCoO2, in which every gap is mixed and every lithium shares one face with cobalt. It is made only by ion exchange from a sodium cobalt oxide (Delmas, Braconnier and Hagenmuller) and is metastable (Carlier et al.). A face-sharing pair therefore costs energy rather than being forbidden, as Pauling's rule says; the law counts the pairs exactly.

The layer spacing tests the law as a number. Motohashi et al. give 4.81 Å per CoO2 layer for O3 at x = 0.35–0.50 and 4.24 Å for O1. The law line at h = 1/2 gives 4.525 Å; H1-3 measures 4.52 Å (c/6 = 4.522 Å), within 0.11%. Spacings in staged intercalation compounds are known to add layer by layer (as in graphite intercalation), so this agreement is expected once H1-3 is read as half O3 and half O1; what the law adds is the face count that tells which half holds the lithium.

5. Where the law stops

(1) The BaMnO3 energies are far from the straight line (Section 3), although the volumes are close. (2) The sizes of face-sharing clusters (dimers in 4H, trimers in 9R, endless chains in 2H) are not linear in h; they depend on runs of hexagonal layers and drive the magnetism Jin et al. report. (3) The layer spacing of LixCoO2 also depends on x at fixed stacking (4.68 Å at x = 1 against 4.81 Å at x = 0.35), so the law describes the stacking part only. (4) In nickel-rich cathodes the H2 → H3 change keeps O3 stacking (h = 0 throughout); its large shrinkage of the layer spacing is outside the law.

6. Conclusion

Pauling's third rule and the tolerance factor become one count: the faces shared by oxygen octahedra are 2h per octahedron in perovskites and 2h per lithium site in layered cathodes, for any stacking. Measured volumes of BaRuO3 lie on the law line, pressure walks them down h, and charged LiCoO2 keeps its lithium where the count gives zero, x ≤ 1 − h. Chapter 15 extends the count: per octahedron, face-sharing clusters number 1 − h and A neighbours 8 − 2h in every stacking, while the octahedra with exactly 0, 1 or 2 shared faces break the Swap Rule and are planes in h and phh.

The stacking notation for hexagonal perovskites (h and c layers, nH and nR) goes back to Katz, Ward and co-workers (Donohue, Katz and Ward); the O/P notation for layered oxides is Delmas, Fouassier and Hagenmuller's; H1-3 as an O1/O3 hybrid with lithium in every other gap is Van der Ven et al.'s prediction and Motohashi et al.'s measurement; that face-sharing costs energy is Pauling's third rule. Chapter 12. The Hexagonality Law in Real Materials V

From paper #73, DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23728921

Ice, diamond and nanowires

Abstract. Ice, diamond and the semiconductor nanowires of new lasers and solar cells are all built from the same piece: a tetrahedron of bonds, in close-packed layers that stack cubic or hexagonal. We test the Hexagonality Law (#66) on them. The geometry is exact: in any stacking, the eclipsed bonds (whose neighbours face each other) number h/2 per atom, where h is the share of hexagonal layers. For six III-V semiconductors in four stackings each, computed by Belabbes et al., the conduction-band offset lies on the law's straight line within 4% of its full change and the crystal-field splitting within 7%. The polytype energies of the tetrahedral elements rank them by their distance from the law: tin 0.02, germanium 0.08, diamond 0.15, silicon 0.32, and silicon carbide 1.03; silicon carbide, the best-known material with many stacking forms, is the one furthest from the law. For ice, Tonauer et al. measured −37.7 J/mol for pure cubic ice turning hexagonal; the law predicts that the stacking-disordered ice of clouds releases 37.7 J/mol times its cubicity, and that its excess vapour pressure scales the same way. This closes the series on real materials.

1. One piece, three materials

In diamond each carbon atom bonds to four others at the corners of a tetrahedron. Silicon, germanium and grey tin do the same; in gallium arsenide, indium phosphide and their relatives the two kinds of atom alternate; in ice the oxygen atoms of water hold the same network through hydrogen bonds. In all of them the atoms form close-packed double layers, and the double layers stack cubic (zinc blende, diamond, ice Ic) or hexagonal (wurtzite, lonsdaleite, ice Ih), or any mixture. Grown as thin wires, GaAs and InP switch between the two, and the stacking can be set layer by layer during growth (Algra et al.; Caroff et al.); a few cubic layers in a hexagonal wire act as a quantum well (Spirkoska et al.). DICTO chose these three families as the last test of the law in real materials.

2. The count

Each double layer has its lower atoms on one of the positions A, B, C and its upper atoms straight above them, joined by a bond along the stacking axis. The upper atom bonds to three atoms of the next double layer, and the lower atom to three of the previous one. A layer is hexagonal when the layers on either side of it sit on the same position.

Theorem. In a tetrahedral network on any close-packed stacking, per atom: (i) the eclipsed bonds number h/2 (all other bonds are staggered); (ii) the eclipsed third-neighbour pairs number 3h/2, at 5/3 of the bond length (staggered third neighbours lie at 1.915 and 2.517 bond lengths).

Proof. Only the bond along the axis can be eclipsed; the three slanted bonds of every atom are staggered in all stackings. The axial bond of double layer k joins a lower atom whose other bonds point down to position Lk−1 and an upper atom whose other bonds point up to position Lk+1. The two sets of three bonds face each other (eclipsed) exactly when Lk−1 = Lk+1, that is when layer k is hexagonal, and then each of the three pairs of end atoms is a short third-neighbour pair. There is one axial bond per two atoms, so the eclipsed bonds are h/2 per atom and the short pairs 3h/2. ∎ Both counts were checked from explicit coordinates on 3C, 2H, 4H, 6H, 9R and 15R (tetra_counts.py).

The eclipsed bond is the only geometric difference between cubic and hexagonal stacking up to third neighbours, so any property that is a sum of local bond terms is a straight line in h. That is the Hexagonality Law for these materials. How far real properties stay on it is a question of data.

5. Ice

Ice formed in clouds and from deeply supercooled water (Murray, Knopf and Bertram) is neither cubic nor hexagonal but stacking-disordered (ice Isd, Malkin et al.; Hansen, Koza and Kuhs), and stacking disorder takes part in how ice nucleates (Lupi et al.); its make-up is given by the cubicity, the share of cubic layers, which is 1 − h. Tonauer, Yamashita, del Rosso, Celli and Loerting measured the enthalpy of pure cubic ice turning hexagonal, using the fault-free cubic ice made only recently (del Rosso et al.; Komatsu et al.): −37.7 ± 2.3 J/mol, at 226 K. Earlier values for stacking-disordered samples scatter from less than −10 to about −160 J/mol, partly because the samples differed in cubicity. Tonauer et al. note that ice with many hexagonal layers must release less heat and have a smaller excess vapour pressure.

Prediction. To the extent that the law holds for ice, stacking-disordered ice of cubicity Φ releases 37.7 Φ J/mol on turning hexagonal, and its excess vapour pressure over hexagonal ice is Φ times that of cubic ice. The eclipsed hydrogen bonds number (1 − Φ)/2 per molecule.

The prediction is testable with samples whose cubicity is measured by diffraction; we found no published set that pairs cubicity with enthalpy. Its weight is in the clouds: the vapour pressure decides whether cubic-rich ice crystals grow at the expense of hexagonal ones, and measured vapour pressures of cubic-rich ice (Murray and Bertram) differ more than the calorimetry allows, as Tonauer et al. point out.

6. Where the law stops

(1) Silicon carbide, and to a lesser degree silicon, are far from the law in energy (Section 4). (2) The volumes of silicon and germanium polytypes change by only 0.1–0.2% across the stackings and do not follow the line. (3) Valence-band offsets of InP and GaSb depart by up to 27% of the full change. (4) The ice prediction is untested. (5) Nanowire surfaces, strain and the electric fields of hexagonal segments are outside the bulk numbers used here.

7. Conclusion

The tetrahedral networks carry the exact count of the law: eclipsed bonds h/2 per atom in every stacking. Their electronic properties come close to the line, the conduction-band offset of nanowire semiconductors closest. Their energies sort the elements from tin, on the law, to silicon carbide, far from it, and the distance tells how many stackings compete. For ice the law turns a qualitative remark into a number to measure. In the terms of Chapter 15, the J2 part of that distance is a plane in h and the share phh of neighbouring hexagonal pairs, and only J3 needs a window of three layers. The series on real materials (Chapters 8 to 12) was followed by a companion on magnets, Chapter 13.

Chapter 13. Spin Ice and Kagome Magnets on Every Stacking

From paper #74, DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23730421

Loops, kagome planes and the Hexagonality Law

Abstract. Two families of magnets are built from corner-sharing triangles and tetrahedra: spin ice, whose magnetic atoms sit on the pyrochlore network of DICTO Janus c/h Pyrochlore (#60), and the kagome antiferromagnets Mn3X, whose manganese atoms form kagome nets. Both are close-packed layers, so both can stack cubic, hexagonal or any mixture. We count exactly what changes with the share h of hexagonal layers. On the pyrochlore network of any stacking, three quarters of the sites lie in kagome layers and one quarter in triangular layers; per tetrahedron the boat-shaped six-loops number 3h/2 and the chair-shaped 2 − 3h/2. Yet the closed loops of the network do not see the stacking at all: on five stackings the numbers of closed non-backtracking loops of every length from 6 to 34 bonds are identical, while the shells of neighbours differ by up to 5%. We call this the Loop Equality (computed, conjecture for all lengths). It gives a counting reason why the frozen-in entropy of spin ice and of water ice is the same for cubic and hexagonal stacking, as recent tensor-network work finds, and predicts the same value for every stacking, the disordered ice of clouds included. In the kagome magnets each manganese atom lies on exactly 1 + 3(1 − h) kagome planes: four in cubic Mn3Ir, kagome in all directions, one in hexagonal Mn3Sn, kagome in layers only.

1. Triangles, tetrahedra and stacking

In spin ice (Dy2Ti2O7, Ho2Ti2O7) the magnetic atoms sit at the corners of tetrahedra that touch only at corners: the pyrochlore network. Each tetrahedron must have two spins pointing in and two out, the same rule as the hydrogen atoms of water ice, and like ice the material keeps a frozen-in entropy at the lowest temperatures (Bramwell and Gingras); its defects behave as magnetic monopoles (Castelnovo, Moessner and Sondhi). In a field along the stacking axis the spins of the triangular layers are held and the kagome layers carry the disorder (Isakov et al.); ice on a single kagome layer has its own exact entropy (Lin and Tang). The pyrochlore network is the network of bond midpoints of a diamond-like crystal (#60), so it follows the stacking of the diamond layers: cubic, hexagonal or any mixture, as in #73. In the kagome antiferromagnets Mn3X the manganese atoms make kagome nets of corner-sharing triangles inside close-packed layers, the same AX3 layers as the hexagonal perovskites of #72; Mn3Ir and Mn3Pt stack them cubic, Mn3Sn and Mn3Ge hexagonal. Their spins on the kagome triangles give a large anomalous Hall effect without net magnetisation, found at room temperature in Mn3Sn (Nakatsuji, Kiyohara and Higo; reviewed by Šmejkal et al.). DICTO chose these two families as the last materials study.

2. The pyrochlore network on any stacking

Theorem 1. On the pyrochlore network of any close-packed stacking with hexagonality h: (i) there are two sites per atom of the parent network, one quarter in triangular layers and three quarters in kagome layers; (ii) per tetrahedron, h/2 of the corner-sharing pairs are eclipsed (mirror images across the shared corner) and the rest staggered (inversion images).

Proof. Each site is the midpoint of one bond; there are two bonds per atom. The bond along the stacking axis, one per two atoms, has its midpoint in a triangular layer; the three slanted bonds have theirs in kagome layers. Two tetrahedra sharing a corner are the tetrahedra of the two atoms of one bond; they are eclipsed exactly when the bond is eclipsed, which by #73 happens for h/2 bonds per atom. ∎

The loops of the network change shape with the stacking. Counted from explicit coordinates on 3C, 2H, 4H, 6H and 9R, each tetrahedron has 3h/2 boat-shaped six-loops and 2 − 3h/2 chair-shaped ones, two in all, and three eight-loops, the same in every stacking.

3. The Loop Equality

A closed non-backtracking loop is a walk along the network that returns to its start and never turns straight back. These loops carry the corrections to Pauling's estimate of the ice-rule entropy. We counted them on 3C, 2H, 4H, 6H and 9R, from one column of sites in a cell 36 by 36 sites wide and at least 36 layers high, so that no loop up to length 34 can wrap round the cell, with every bond checked against its length.

Loop Equality (computed; conjecture for all lengths). On every close-packed stacking, the number of closed non-backtracking loops of each length per site is the same. Computed for all even lengths from 6 to 34 on the five stackings: 24, 48, 480, 3432, 25704, 194256, 1509072, 11820000, …, 27416593190640, identical to the last digit.

The networks themselves differ. The numbers of atoms 3, 4, 5 and 6 bonds away are 24, 42, 64, 92 for the cubic stacking and 25, 44, 67, 96 for the hexagonal, the known values for diamond and lonsdaleite, with 4H in between (Figure 13.1a). The shells see the stacking from three bonds on; the loops do not see it up to 34.

Entropy. Pauling's estimate gives the same residual entropy, (1/2) ln(3/2) per spin in spin ice, for every stacking. Onsager proved that the hexagonal value is at least the cubic one; Nagle's series found them equal within its precision, and Xu, Lin and Zhang's tensor-network computation finds w = 1.5074584 for both, as numerical evidence that they are equal. The Loop Equality gives a counting reason: the loops that correct Pauling's estimate are the same on both, through loops of 34 bonds. It also extends the statement from the two pure stackings to all of them: to the extent that the entropy is set by these loops, spin ice and water ice have the same frozen-in entropy in every stacking, stacking-disordered ice included. In the language of the Hexagonality Law, this is the law with zero slope.

4. Kagome magnets

In Mn3X each close-packed layer holds manganese on a kagome net and X on the remaining quarter of the sites, and the layers stack so that no two X atoms touch. We counted from explicit coordinates on 3C, 2H, 4H, 6H and 9R. Many things do not change: each manganese has 8 manganese and 4 X neighbours, 2 triangles of manganese in its layer and 6 tilted ones, and one third of an octahedral hole surrounded by manganese only. What changes is how the tilted triangles join.

Theorem 2. In an A3B close-packed stacking with hexagonality h, each A atom lies on 1 + 3(1 − h) kagome planes: always its own layer, and three tilted planes if its layer is cubic, none if it is hexagonal.

Proof. A kagome plane through the atom needs two triangles of the plane meeting at the atom, one on each side. For a tilted plane, one triangle reaches into the layer below and the other into the layer above, and the two lie in one plane exactly when the atoms above are the images of the atoms below under inversion through the atom. That is so when the layers below and above sit on different positions, a cubic layer, for each of the three tilted directions; when they sit on the same position, a hexagonal layer, the images are mirror images and no tilted pair is coplanar. Averaging over the layers gives 1 + 3(1 − h). ∎ The count was checked on all five stackings (1 + 3(1 − h) = 4, 3, 2.5, 2, 1 for 3C, 6H, 4H, 9R, 2H).

So the law counts the dimension of the kagome network. In cubic Mn3Ir every manganese lies on four kagome planes, one in each of the four (111) directions, as Chen, Niu and MacDonald describe; in hexagonal Mn3Sn on one, the basal plane. The intermediate stackings, such as the 4H (D024) type, would have kagome planes in between, 2.5 per atom on average: half the atoms with four, half with one. The Swap Rule of #61 and #64 applies to these layers unchanged, since they are the close-packed AX3 layers treated there and in #72; we do not test it again.

5. Where the results stop

(1) The Loop Equality is computed through length 34 on five stackings; we have no proof for all lengths and all stackings. (2) The step from equal loops to equal entropy is not exact: the corrections to Pauling's estimate come from clusters of loops, not only from loop counts; the agreement with the tensor-network values supports it but does not prove it. (3) Theorem 2 counts geometry only; which kagome planes carry the spin order and the Hall effect depends on the magnetic structure, and for Mn3Sn the basal-plane Hall response is forbidden by symmetry (Liu et al.).

6. Conclusion

The Hexagonality Law reaches magnets in two ways. The kagome planes through each manganese atom of the Mn3X magnets fall on its straight line, 1 + 3(1 − h), from three-dimensional kagome in Mn3Ir to two-dimensional in Mn3Sn. The loops of the pyrochlore network do not depend on the stacking at all through length 34, so the frozen-in entropy of spin ice and ice is the same for every stacking: the law with zero slope. This chapter closes the series on single materials; Part IV returns to the perovskites and adds the Frank–Kasper phases and the approximants of quasicrystals, under one theorem (Chapter 15).

Chapter 14. HexOct: Two Tiles for Every Stacking

Every close-packed stacking is a string of two layer tiles, the c-tile and the h-tile

The Law gave the pieces,
The pieces prove the law.

DICTO

Abstract. In the Rule of Halves (#57) every hole of every close-packed stacking gets a piece, and each piece is made from the pieces of the two pure stackings, cubic and hexagonal. Here we cut space into layer tiles: the tile of a layer is the share of space of that layer, made of four pieces, the top half of the octahedral piece below it, the two tetrahedral pieces next to it and the bottom half of the octahedral piece above it. We prove, exactly and along the whole weight walk of #57, that there are only two tiles. Every layer tile is carried onto the c-tile of the cubic stacking or the h-tile of the hexagonal stacking by one motion, all four pieces at once, and which of the two is decided by the layer's own type. Any tile sits on any tile, since the joint between two layers has the same shape for both types. So every close-packed stacking, periodic or not, is a string of two tiles, and a string of two tiles in any order is a stacking. The tiles meet on flat planes for weights up to λ = −7/24 and interlock above it, their tetrahedral pieces reaching into the neighbouring layer. At each weight all stackings together use five whole shapes. Version 2 adds HexOct-K, keyed HexOct tiles: with keys on the axis faces of one layer the tiles form a Wang tiling of the square grid, so HexOct-K layers can compute; a layer of four keyed tiles counts in binary. Where the tiles interlock, every tile touches the layer below and the layer above at exactly three positions along the layers' common diagonal, in every stacking (192 of 192 cases), so HexOct-K tiles run any elementary cellular automaton with one step per layer: the stacking itself is the clock, and with Rule 110 the stack is a universal computer.

Stack layers of balls and the gaps between them can be shared out among the holes. Paper #57 found that every piece is built from two kinds of half. Here the halves are packed into layers, and only two kinds of layer come out, one for each way a layer can sit between its neighbours. Pick any sequence of the two and you have built a crystal stacking; the pieces fit at every joint, endlessly.

1. Where it started

The Euclid–Kepler–Pacioli cell (#8 [9]) holds all five Platonic solids in one unit, and copies of the unit join across their faces so that the cycle of solids runs on from cell to cell without end. DICTO proposed that the pieces of the Rule of Halves (#57) behave in the same way: that they fill one unit and join across units, cycling without end. The natural unit is one layer of balls with its share of the holes on either side. This chapter proves that the idea holds exactly, and finds the shape of the two units.

2. The set-up

As in #57: unit balls sit in close-packed layers (triangular lattices of spacing 1) at heights kd, d = √(2/3); layer k sits on one of the three positions A, B, C, with no two consecutive layers on the same position. Layer k is h if layers k − 1 and k + 1 sit on the same position and c otherwise. Between layers k and k + 1 lie an octahedral hole at height (k + ½)d and two tetrahedral holes, at (k + ¼)d and (k + ¾)d. One weight λ, given to the tetrahedral holes (the octahedral holes have weight 0), decides the power diagram of the holes, whose cells are the pieces; the walk is −11/24 ≤ λ ≤ 3/8. By #57, a tetrahedral piece is the cubic or hexagonal tetrahedral piece named by its nearest layer, and an octahedral piece cut by the horizontal plane through its hole has its bottom half named by the layer below and its top half by the layer above.

Definition. The tile of layer L, for one ball of the layer, is made of four pieces: the top half of the octahedral piece of the gap below (hole at (L − ½)d), the tetrahedral pieces at (L − ¼)d and (L + ¼)d, and the bottom half of the octahedral piece of the gap above (hole at (L + ½)d). The c-tile and h-tile are the tiles of the pure cubic stacking ABC… and the pure hexagonal stacking AB…. Every piece and half of the diagram belongs to exactly one tile, so the tiles of all balls of all layers fill space without overlap.

3. Main theorem

Theorem (two tiles). For every close-packed stacking and every λ with −11/24 ≤ λ ≤ 3/8, the tile of each layer L is carried onto the c-tile, if L is c, or onto the h-tile, if L is h, by one motion that maps the layer to itself: a symmetry of the triangular lattice about the vertical axis through the ball, followed by a translation. The same motion carries all four pieces.

Corollaries. (i) Any tile sits on any tile. The face where two tiles meet is the section of an octahedral piece by the horizontal plane through its hole; for every λ this section has one shape, shared by the c- and h-tiles. Every sequence of c- and h-tiles is therefore a stacking, and every stacking is such a sequence. (ii) Flat or interlocking. For λ ≤ −7/24 every tile lies between the planes through the octahedral holes above and below, and the tiles meet on flat planes; for λ > −7/24 the tetrahedral pieces cross these planes and the tiles interlock. The value −7/24 is the same for both tiles and is not a stop of the walk. (iii) Five shapes. At each λ all close-packed stackings together use five whole pieces: the c- and h-tetrahedral pieces and three octahedral pieces, with two c-halves, two h-halves, or one of each.

The Rule of Halves matched each piece separately, each with its own motion. The theorem here is stronger: one motion places the whole tile, so the tile is a rigid unit that can be picked up and stacked.

4. Proof

Step 1: seven layers decide. The two octahedral halves and two tetrahedral pieces of tile L belong to the gaps L − 1 and L. By Step 1 of #57 the pieces of a gap depend only on the six layers around it, so the tile depends only on the layers L − 3, …, L + 3. Up to relabelling A, B, C these are fixed by the types of the five inner layers L − 2, …, L + 2: 32 contexts. For each we took a periodic stacking containing the seven layers; the 32 stackings are listed in prove.out [S].

Step 2: the stretches. For each context we found exactly, with the census engine of #53 and #57, every λ at which one of the four cells of the tile, or of the pure tile of the same type, changes its faces. Between two such breakpoints every corner of every cell is an affine function of λ, and the corners of a half, where an edge crosses the cutting plane, are rational functions of λ. On each sub-interval we found the motion at one sample λ, applied it to the tile, and compared the result with the pure tile as rational functions of λ (computer algebra, exact): they are identical, so the tiles are equal on the whole sub-interval. All 32 contexts, every sub-interval: equal.

Step 3: the breakpoints. At each breakpoint the tile was computed directly, in rational arithmetic, and the same comparison holds. □

Corollary (i). In a stacking with a c-layer below an h-layer, the octahedral piece between them has a c-half below and an h-half above. Its section by the plane through its hole is the top face of the c-half and the bottom face of the h-half at once. By the theorem every c-tile's top face is a copy of this c-face and every h-tile's bottom face a copy of this h-face, and they are the same shape. The same holds the other way up, and for c on c and h on h trivially. Corollary (ii). On each sub-interval the heights of the tetrahedral corners are affine in λ; solving where they meet ±½ (in layer units) gives λ = −7/24 in all 32 contexts, and no other crossing. Corollary (iii). In a c-layer the ball is a centre of symmetry of the cubic stacking, which swaps the two tetrahedral pieces of the tile and its two octahedral halves; in an h-layer the layer plane is a mirror of the hexagonal stacking, which does the same. So each tile holds one tetrahedral shape and one half shape, and the octahedral pieces are made of two halves of these four kinds: c + c, h + h or c + h (the mixed pieces of #57, Chucky and Fang among them). □

5. The two tiles

A tile is one ball's share of its layer; side by side, copies of a tile fill the layer as a slab whose upper and lower faces are flat for λ ≤ −7/24 and toothed above. At λ = 1/10 the tetrahedral pieces reach a full layer up and down, to the height of the next layer's balls, so each layer keys into the next (Figure 14.2).

So there is a cubic tile and a hexagonal tile, and either fits on the top or bottom face of either. Sideways a layer is made of one tile only, since all its balls have the same type. The type of a tile does not limit what goes above it; it decides where the next layer sits: above a c-tile the next layer takes the third position, above an h-tile it returns to the position of the layer below.

Known and credited. Close-packed stackings, polytypes and the h/c notation [4]; power diagrams [5]; the pieces and the Rule of Halves [1] on the walk of #53 [2], with the twinned octet pieces of #56 [3]. Order–disorder (OD) theory, from Dornberger-Schiff, describes polytypic crystals as stacks of layers of one or more kinds, any two neighbouring layer pairs being geometrically equivalent (the vicinity condition) [6]: the general frame in which a stacking is a string of layers. Tilings of space by regular tetrahedra and octahedra, including non-periodic ones obtained by sliding along planes, are enumerated by Gabbrielli, Jiao and Torquato [7]; the unit cell of the octet as a rhombohedron of two tetrahedra and one octahedron, and its layered sections, are in Lalvani [8]. The Euclid–Kepler–Pacioli cell network is DICTO's [9].

Stated here. The layer tiles built from the power-diagram pieces of the holes; the proof that there are only two, placed each by one motion, for all stackings and the whole walk; the shared joint; the switch from flat to interlocking at λ = −7/24; the five shapes. We found no earlier statement of tiles of this kind.

7. Where the results stop

(1) The theorem covers the walk of #57, −11/24 ≤ λ ≤ 3/8, with one weight for all tetrahedral holes; at the ends some pieces shrink to points and the tiles are read as limits. (2) With different weights for the two kinds of tetrahedral hole the tiles may split into more kinds; not tested. (3) The tiles are geometric; which of them a real crystal prefers is the subject of the Hexagonality Law papers, not of this one.

8. HexOct-K

DICTO proposed that data could be built into HexOct configurations, the ways the tiles may connect setting the limits, as the basis of a new kind of programming language. On their own the two tiles carry one free choice per layer: any tile sits on any tile, so a stack is a string of c and h, a perfect store of bits that can never refuse a tile. To compute, a set of tiles must be able to refuse. That is what keys do: marks on the faces where tiles meet, with a tile allowed only where every key matches its neighbour's. DICTO names keyed HexOct tiles HexOct-K.

Where keys can go. We measured, for every tile, which neighbouring tiles share a face with it and how much face area each, in six stackings covering both tile types and mixed neighbourhoods (ABC, AB, ABAC both layers, ABABC, ABACBC) and five weights across the walk (λ = −2/5, −1/3, 0, 1/10, 1/4): points sampled on every face of every piece of the tile, pushed a hair outward, and looked up in the pieces of every tile within two layers; no face area was left unmatched. Three facts hold in every case. (i) Within its own layer every tile shares faces with its four neighbours along the lattice axes, (±1, 0) and (0, ±1), each face of area at least 0.28 (ball spacing 1), and with up to four more. (ii) Where the tiles meet on flat planes (λ ≤ −7/24), each tile touches exactly one tile above and one below, through the whole joint of area √3/2: the stack falls into columns. (iii) Where they interlock, each tile touches four to six tiles in the layer above and in the layer below.

Keys on the joints alone count, but cannot compute. With keys only on the joints between layers, a column of tiles is a string read once from bottom to top, each tile checking only the one below. Such a set of tiles accepts exactly the strings a finite machine with a fixed memory can recognise (a regular language): it can enforce patterns such as "no two h-tiles in a row", but it cannot count without bound or compute.

Keys on the side faces compute anything. Put keys on the four axis faces of the tiles of one layer, the other side faces left blank (one key that always matches). The layer is then a Wang tiling of the square grid: square tiles with marked edges, placed without turning, edges matching [10]. Wang tilings can carry out any computation, the rows of a tiling being the successive steps of a Turing machine; and no general method can decide whether a given set of Wang tiles fills the plane [11, 12]. Both statements therefore hold for HexOct-K layers, at every weight of the walk, for c-tiles and h-tiles alike.

A layer that counts. The smallest working example uses four keyed tiles (Figure 14.3). An exhaustive search over windows of 3 × 8, 4 × 16 and 5 × 20 tiles found exactly one filling in each, and in each the rows count up in binary. Where the tiles meet on flat planes, the same four tiles can count up the stack instead, one number per layer, with the carry passing along the layer and each number handed to the layer above through the single joint (fact ii): the stacking itself becomes the clock.

The stacking as the clock. Where the tiles interlock (λ > −7/24), measure the lattice offsets of the touching tiles along the layers' common diagonal, s = du − dv; every layer shift, B = (1/3, 1/3) or C = (2/3, 2/3), lies along (1, 1), so s does not change from layer to layer. In all 32 five-layer contexts at six weights across the interlocking range (λ = −1/4, −1/6, 0, 1/10, 1/4, 1/3; 192 cases, face areas sampled as above, none unmatched), every tile touches the tiles of the layer below, and of the layer above, at exactly three positions, s − 1, s and s + 1, and at no others [S3]. Give each tile one state, written on the faces it shares with tiles above and read on the faces it shares with tiles below, the faces within a layer blank, and take every layer constant along the diagonal: a set of keyed tiles with one tile for each triple of states below then runs an elementary cellular automaton, one step per layer, forced layer by layer, whatever the order of c- and h-tiles. Rule 110 is universal [19], so a HexOct-K stack can compute anything, with the stacking itself as the clock. Each tile is decided by its own layer, so the tiles are the Window Law's window of one layer (Chapter 15): every count of a stacking read tile by tile is a straight line in h. What is known, and what was not found. Wang tiles and their computing power are classical [10–12]; tiles that compute by assembling themselves were made from DNA [13], and three-dimensional tile assembly can simulate Turing machines [14]; Wang cubes have been used to build aperiodic material samples [15], and triangular and hexagonal tiles have been studied for self-assembly [16]. Space-time diagrams of cellular automata are tilings of this kind, and Wang cubes have been made aperiodic (K. Culik II and J. Kari, Journal of Universal Computer Science 1 (1995) 675–686). Sources checked: these works; web searches for cellular automata as layered three-dimensional tilings and for close-packed stacking layers as computation (one study models the change of a stacking itself by cellular automaton rule 232: D. P. Varn and J. P. Crutchfield, arXiv cond-mat/0307296); web searches for three-dimensional polyhedral tiles with matching rules and computation, and for close-packed stacking layers as tiles with keys or computation; and DICTO's reference library (7,730 passages) for Wang tiles and self-assembly, which returned the aperiodic monotile [17] and interlocking tetrahedron–octahedron blocks [18]. We found no use of close-packed stacking layers, or of tiles built from their holes, as a medium for computation. The computing power here is Wang's; what HexOct adds is the geometry that makes it available: two tiles that fit every stacking, with axis faces of positive area in every layer, and one-to-one joints that make the stacking a clock.

Status. HexOct-K is a first study (2026-10-29, [S2]): the contact measurements are sampled, not exact; the counting layer is checked exhaustively on the windows above. The stacking-as-clock contacts are measured in the same way, in all 192 cases [S3]; the cellular automaton follows from them by construction.

9. Conclusion

Every close-packed stacking, at every weight, is a string of two tiles, and any string of the two tiles is a stacking. Each tile is one rigid unit of four pieces, decided by its own layer alone; the tiles meet on flat planes up to λ = −7/24 and interlock beyond it. As DICTO proposed, the pieces fill one unit and join across units without end. Keyed, as HexOct-K, the tiles compute: within a layer as a Wang tiling, and, where they interlock, up the stack, one cellular-automaton step per layer, with the stacking itself as the clock.

The Hexagonality Law beyond one layer: one elementary theorem for every count of a two-letter stacking, tested on perovskites, Frank–Kasper phases and quasicrystal approximants

Abstract. DICTO's Hexagonality Law says that in every close-packed crystal structure, however its layers are stacked, every count of its pieces, holes, bonds and swaps is a straight line in its hexagonality h, the share of hexagonal layers. We prove the law that contains it and marks its exact reach: every count read from one layer, and every pair count that obeys the Swap Rule. For a layer sequence over two letters, c and h, any quantity fixed by g consecutive layers has a mean that is a linear function of the shares of the c/h words of length g, which have exactly 2g−1 free values; the mean drops to a window of g − 1 layers exactly when certain Swap defects vanish. At g = 1 this is the Hexagonality Law. At g = 2 exactly one new number enters, the share phh of neighbouring hexagonal pairs, and its coefficient is Δ = F(cc) + F(hh) − F(ch) − F(hc), the Swap defect: DICTO's Swap Rule is exactly the condition for a pair count to stay a straight line in h. The proof is elementary, and the frame is classical in substance. We test it on three kinds of crystal, with exact counts throughout. In the hexagonal perovskites (all 505 polytypes up to twelve layers and 50 random stackings) 16 of 36 counts are lines in h, 9 are planes in (h, phh) with coefficient Δ, and 11 need longer windows; new exact counts include face-sharing clusters per B = 1 − h, A neighbours per B = 8 − 2h, the same holes in every gap, and the B–B distance shells. In the Frank–Kasper phases (18 real phases and 181 layered stackings) the classical identities and the 1974 Yarmolyuk–Kripyakevich line hold, and three hypothetical structures leave that line through a single defect, the same shape; the layered families carry Swap defects in the pattern +1, −2, +1 and cannot see the c/h letter of their Laves slabs. In the periodic approximants of quasicrystals one number, the share of one tile, orders everything: on the chamber that holds the quasicrystal the defect is zero and the quasicrystal lies on every line; beyond its walls the defect appears, in one dimension as a kink of twice the Swap defect, in the Ammann–Beenker tiling as a forbidden vertex star growing as a square. Approximants carry zero defect window after window, up to a length that grows with their period; zero defect at window 2 alone does not single them out. This is the bridge to DICTO's aim.

1. Introduction

Look at a stack of layers through a window one layer tall, and every count you can read there is a straight line in one number, the share of hexagonal layers. That is DICTO's Hexagonality Law. Open the window to two layers and one more number comes into view, the share of hexagonal pairs; how strongly a count sees it is the Swap Rule's defect. Open it further and more numbers come in, a window at a time. In the approximants of a quasicrystal the second number is held at zero, and the straight lines run all the way to the never-repeating limit.

DICTO's Hexagonality Law is a law of structural crystallography: in every close-packed crystal structure, however its layers are stacked, every count of the structure (its pieces, holes, bonds and swaps) is a straight line in the structure's hexagonality, the crystallographers' share of hexagonal layers. It grew out of DICTO's Rule of Halves (#57), which builds every hole piece of every stacking from the pure cubic and hexagonal ones, and his Swap Rule (#61, #64): the two mixed pieces add up to the two pure ones. The Focus (#66) proved the law from the Swap Rule wherever a piece is fixed by its two neighbouring layers; The Hexagonality Law and the Fibonacci Stacking (#69) carried it to a never-repeating stacking, and The Hexagonality Law in Real Materials IV (#72) to the perovskite oxides. This book gathers the whole line of work, and this chapter closes it.

DICTO's Splash Rules (27 October) look for one simple rule behind a whole family of exactly computed values. Three outside runs on 31 October, on perovskite polytypes, Frank–Kasper phases and quasicrystal approximants, found the same rule behind every local rule in all three. This chapter states it as a theorem and proves it (Section 2), draws its corollaries (Section 3), and reports the three test grounds with their exact results and their status, known or new (Sections 4–6). Section 7 sets out what is known and what is new, Section 8 concludes.

2. The Window Law

A stacking is a sequence of letters c and h, one per layer; a layer is h when its two neighbours sit on the same position (A, B or C) and c otherwise, which is Jagodzinski's notation [1]. Every such sequence is the layer sequence of a stacking: each new position is either the one two layers down (h) or the third one (c). We work with periodic stackings w = w1 … wn, indices taken mod n; everything holds also for any infinite sequence in which the shares below exist, such as the Fibonacci stacking. The share fu of a word u of length g is the number of positions i at which wi … wi+g−1 = u, divided by n. Thus h = fh, and phh = fhh is the share of neighbouring hh pairs.

A quantity is fixed by g consecutive layers if it attaches a number Fi to every position i (a layer, a gap, a formula unit) and there is a table F on the words of length g and an offset k such that Fi = F(wi+k … wi+k+g−1). Its mean is F̄ = (1/n) Σ Fi. For a word v of length g − 2 the Swap defect of F at v is

Δv(F) = F(cvc) + F(hvh) − F(cvh) − F(hvc);

at g = 2 the word v is empty and Δ = F(cc) + F(hh) − F(ch) − F(hc), the quantity whose vanishing is DICTO's Swap Rule.

Main Theorem (the Window Law). Let F be a quantity fixed by g consecutive layers of a two-letter stacking.

(a) F̄ = Σ|u| = g F(u)·fu: the mean is a linear function of the shares of the c/h words of length g.

(b) These shares obey Σ fu = 1 and, for every word v of length g − 1, fcv + fhv = fvc + fvh. They leave exactly 2g−1 free values, and no other linear relation holds for all stackings; so the linear function in (a) is unique once these free values are chosen.

(c) For g ≥ 2, F̄ is a linear function of the shares of the words of length g − 1 for every stacking if and only if every Swap defect Δv(F), |v| = g − 2, is zero.

(d) For g = 2, with h and phh as free values,

F̄ = F(cc) + [F(ch) + F(hc) − 2F(cc)]·h + Δ·phh,   Δ = F(cc) + F(hh) − F(ch) − F(hc),

so F̄ is a straight line in h for every stacking if and only if Δ = 0, the Swap Rule.

Proof. (a) Group the positions i by the word they read: the word u occurs n·fu times and contributes F(u) each time.

(b) Each occurrence of a word v of length g − 1 is followed by exactly one letter and preceded by exactly one letter, and in a periodic word every occurrence of a word of length g contains one occurrence of its first g − 1 letters and one of its last g − 1. So fvc + fvh = fv = fcv + fhv (Kirchhoff's law for factor frequencies). There are 2g shares and 2g−1 balance relations; the balance relations are the node equations of the de Bruijn graph B(2, g − 1) (nodes: the 2g−1 words of length g − 1; arrows: the words of length g), which is strongly connected, so its incidence matrix has rank 2g−1 − 1: the relations add up to 0 = 0 and are otherwise independent. Σ fu = 1 is independent of them, since every combination of balance relations vanishes on the indicator of a cycle while the sum of shares does not. Together they have rank 2g−1, leaving 2g − 2g−1 = 2g−1 free values. To see that no further relation holds, read the words of length g as the arrows of the de Bruijn graph (from their first g − 1 letters to their last g − 1). The non-negative solutions of the relations are the circulations of total 1 on this graph; each is a mixture of simple cycles (the classical decomposition of a flow), and a simple cycle is the share vector of the periodic word that reads it. The de Bruijn word of order g has every share equal to 2−g, a solution with no zero entry, so the solutions form a polytope of full dimension 2g−1 spanned by share vectors of stackings.

(c) If every Δv is zero, set A(xv) = F(xvc) and B(vy) = F(cvy) − F(cvc) for letters x, y. Then A(xv) + B(vy) − F(xvy) = F(xvc) + F(cvy) − F(cvc) − F(xvy), which is zero when x or y is c and equals −Δv = 0 when x = y = h. So F(u) = A(first g − 1 letters of u) + B(last g − 1 letters), and by the balance relations F̄ = Σ|w| = g−1 [A(w) + B(w)]·fw. Conversely, suppose F̄ = Σw L(w)·fw for every stacking (a constant term is L summed against Σ fw = 1). Then G(u) = F(u) − L(first g − 1 letters of u) has Σ G(u) fu = 0 for every stacking, hence by (b) along every direction inside the polytope. For a fixed v the vector δv with +1 on cvc and hvh and −1 on cvh and hvc keeps every balance relation (each of the four end words cv, hv, vc, vh meets one +1 and one −1) and has total 0, so it is such a direction. Therefore 0 = Σ G·δv = Δv(F) − [L(cv) + L(hv) − L(cv) − L(hv)] = Δv(F). The 2g−2 defects are a complete set of invariants, so the condition is minimal: the functions of g letters form a space of dimension 2g, the sums of a function of the first g − 1 and a function of the last g − 1 letters a subspace of dimension 2g−1 + 2g−1 − 2g−2 (the overlap is the functions of the middle g − 2 letters), so the quotient has dimension 2g−2; the Δv, one for each v, vanish on that subspace and are independent, since each lives on its own four words.

(d) The balance relation at g = 2 gives fch = fhc, and h = fhc + fhh, so fcc = 1 − 2h + phh, fch = fhc = h − phh, fhh = phh. Put these into (a) and multiply out. The last claim is (c) with v empty; two real stackings show the "only if" directly: 4H (hc) and 12R (hhcc) both have h = 1/2, with phh = 0 and 1/4, so their means differ by Δ/4. ∎

The theorem is elementary: (a) is a regrouping, (b) is Kirchhoff's law for factor frequencies, (c) is the statement that a two-by-two table is a sum of a row function and a column function exactly when its interaction term vanishes. The frame is classical in substance: factor frequencies and their balance relations are standard for Sturmian and other words (Dekking [2]; Berthé 1996 [3]; Balková and Pelantová [4]), criterion (c) is the cohomology of locally constant functions on the full shift, and in crystallography the theory of stacking disorder (Hendricks and Teller 1942 [5], Jagodzinski 1949 [1]) and the J1–J2 energies of polytype models (the ANNNI model [6]) express quantities through the correlations of a few neighbouring layers. The link to the stacking Hamiltonian is exact. Put s = −1 for c and s = +1 for h and write a pair quantity as F(x, y) = α + β sx + γ sy + δ sxsy; then Δ = 4δ, and (d) reads F̄ = F0 + (β + γ)⟨s⟩ + δ⟨sisi+1⟩ with ⟨s⟩ = 2h − 1 and ⟨sisi+1⟩ = 1 − 4h + 4phh. So the Swap defect is four times the nearest-neighbour coupling J1 of the stacking Hamiltonian written in c/h spins (in the Hägg spins σ of the ANNNI polytype model, si = −σiσi+1, so the same coupling is the J2 term σiσi+2; Cheng, Needs and Heine 1988 [7]; Price and Yeomans [8]). For general g the Δv are the couplings that contain both end spins of the window. The Swap defect is therefore classical as an object; it is new as a name and as the bridge to DICTO's rules. What the theorem adds to DICTO's line of work is the exact form: one law, read one window at a time, with the Swap defect as the coefficient of the one new number at window 2.

3. Corollaries

Corollary 1 (the Hexagonality Law, g = 1). A quantity fixed by one layer has mean F(c) + [F(h) − F(c)]·h: a straight line in the hexagonality, through its values in the pure cubic and the pure hexagonal stacking.

Corollary 2 (the Swap Rule as the test). A quantity fixed by two neighbouring layers is a straight line in h on all stackings if and only if it obeys the Swap Rule, F(ch) + F(hc) = F(cc) + F(hh). If it fails, it is a plane in (h, phh) with slope Δ in phh, and no other number enters.

Corollary 3 (defects add). Δv is linear in F. If counts F1, …, Fk by kind add up to a count that is a line in h (or a constant), their defects add up to zero. The counts of gaps of the three classes cc, mixed (ch or hc) and hh have defects +1, −2, +1.

Corollary 4 (same h, different stacking). Two stackings with the same h differ in a window-2 count by exactly Δ·(phh − p′hh). At a given h the second number runs over max(0, 2h − 1) ≤ phh < h for every mixed periodic stacking (a stacking of period n with both letters has phh ≤ h − 1/n; the upper edge phh = h is reached only by the pure stackings and approached by long blocks of h), so a count with Δ ≠ 0 fills a band of width up to |Δ|·(h − max(0, 2h − 1)) above each h.

Corollary 5 (the lower edge). The lower edge phh = max(0, 2h − 1) of that band is reached exactly by the stackings with no hh pair (h ≤ 1/2) or no cc pair (h ≥ 1/2). On it every window-2 count is a straight line in h on each side of h = 1/2, and the slope jumps there by exactly 2Δ.

Proof. Corollaries 1, 2 and 4 are parts (a) and (d). Corollary 3: Δ is a fixed combination of the values F(cc), F(ch), F(hc), F(hh), so it adds; the count of cc gaps has table (1, 0, 0, 0), the mixed gaps (0, 1, 1, 0), the hh gaps (0, 0, 0, 1). For the band: fcc = 1 − 2h + phh ≥ 0 and fch = h − phh ≥ 0. Corollary 5: phh = 0 means fhh = 0, and phh = 2h − 1 means fcc = 0; the slope in h of F̄ changes by Δ·2 where phh turns from 0 to 2h − 1. ∎

Corollary 1 is the Hexagonality Law for every count read from one layer. Corollary 2 marks its exact reach among pair counts: The Focus (#66) proved the direction "Swap Rule ⇒ line" for pieces and showed the two equivalent there; here the statement is for every pair count, with the defect Δ as the exact coefficient of the one number that enters when the rule fails; a pair count that breaks the Swap Rule needs one more number, and only one. Corollary 5 is the door to the approximants (Section 6): the balanced words of one-dimensional quasicrystal theory lie on this lower edge.

How DICTO's rules fit together

The Window Law does not compete with DICTO's earlier rules; it is the frame in which each of them has its place. Ordered from the general to the particular:

  1. The Window Law (the Main Theorem) holds for every quantity of every c/h stacking, with no geometry assumed: a count read from g layers is a line in the shares of the words of length g. It never fails; it only says how many numbers a count needs.
  2. The Rule of Halves (#57) is geometry: every piece between two layers is made of a half borrowed from the lower layer's pure stacking and a half from the upper one's. So a count of halves is read from one layer (window 1), and a count of whole pieces from two (window 2).
  3. The Swap Rule (#61) is geometry too: pieces change only by swapping halves, which makes the Swap defect Δ of every piece count zero. By Corollary 2 this is exactly what brings the window-2 piece counts down to window 1.
  4. The Hexagonality Law (HexOct) is then the result: every count of halves, and every piece count that obeys the Swap Rule, is a straight line in hexagonality (Corollary 1).

So nothing takes precedence over anything else in the sense of overruling it: the Window Law is always true, and the Rule of Halves and the Swap Rule are the geometric facts that decide which window a count lives in. Where they hold, the Hexagonality Law follows; where a count escapes them (the perovskite counts of B with 0, 1 or 2 shared faces, the B–B shells), the Window Law says exactly which extra number it needs. This ordering is our reading of DICTO's rules.

4. Test ground 1: perovskite polytypes

We model X and A as equal balls (X–X = 1), A on a 2 × 2 sublattice of each layer. 5. Test ground 2: Frank–Kasper phases

A Frank–Kasper (tetrahedrally close-packed) structure is cut by its atoms into tetrahedra only. Each atom has Z = 12, 14, 15 or 16 neighbours (its Delaunay neighbours), every bond is shared by 5 or 6 tetrahedra, and Z = 12 + (number of six-fold bonds at the atom). The six-fold bonds form Frank and Kasper's major skeleton [14, 15], Nelson's disclination network [16]. We read the topology of 18 real phases from published coordinates (AFLOW prototype encyclopedia [17] and the Crystallography Open Database [18]) by a periodic Delaunay step, and checked every site: closed triangulated shell with Euler number 2, folds 5 or 6, Z = 12 + #six-fold bonds, no two six-fold neighbours adjacent in a shell. Every count after that is an integer and every test exact. Four candidates fail the check and are not tetrahedrally close-packed: χ (α-Mn) and Mg17Al12 (bonds shared by 4 tetrahedra), Mg23Al30 and Mg21Zn25. Two layered families give the spread: square–triangle rows (Sullivan [19]; Sadoc and Mosseri 2016 [20]; S = A15, T = Z, ST = H, STT = J; all 62 words checked, all primitive words up to length 7 and 30 random words of 20 rows) and kagome slabs (Frank and Kasper 1959 [15]; a step ± is a Laves slab of 6 atoms, a step 0 a Z slab of 7 atoms; + = C15, +− = C14, 0 = Z, 0+ = μ, 0+0− = K7Cs6; all 119 words checked, all words up to length 6 and 40 random words of 14 slabs). In all, 199 structures.

The identities (classical; exact on all 199). Per atom, with ρ = six-fold bonds per atom: Z̄ = 12 + 2ρ, bonds = 6 + ρ, five-fold bonds = 6, tetrahedra = 5 + ρ, triangles = 10 + 2ρ. A tetrahedron has 0, 1 or 2 six-fold edges, and two only when they are opposite.

An atom has 12 five-fold bonds whatever its Z, and its shell has 2Z − 4 triangles, which gives the identities; two six-fold edges at one corner would make adjacent six-fold vertices in that corner's shell. So one number, the disclination density ρ, fixes every topological count per atom, by identity. This is Frank and Kasper's [14, 15] and Nelson's [16] picture, not new.

6. Test ground 3: quasicrystal approximants

All three families are cut-and-project sets: lattice points of Zn in a strip around a space E are kept and projected. The quasicrystal takes an irrational E; an approximant replaces the irrational number by a fraction, and the pattern repeats. We used the canonical strip and a generic offset and counted everything per period, exactly: in one dimension the words sn = ⌊(n+1)a + r⌋ − ⌊na + r⌋ (S for 1, L for 0, a = share of S) for all 1101 fractions 0 < a = p/q < 1 with q ≤ 60 and windows g = 1 to 9, and the Fibonacci chain a = 1/φ² in exact arithmetic in Q(√5); in two dimensions the Ammann–Beenker (AB) square approximants, √2/2 replaced by t = a/b, all 246 fractions 1/2 ≤ t ≤ 1 with b ≤ 40 and the Pell ones up to 99/140, the limit in Q(√2); in three dimensions the cubic approximants of the 3D Penrose tiling, φ replaced by Fk+1/Fk from 1/1 to 55/34. Two facts hold throughout and were checked on every run: the number of tiles of each kind per period is the absolute value of a minor of a basis of the period lattice (its Plücker coordinate), and vertices equal tiles in number.

One dimension. The S/L words are two-letter stackings, and the Window Law applies as it stands, with S in the place of h. For every window length g and every word w of length g, the share of w in the approximant p/q is a straight line in a on each interval between neighbouring fractions with denominator ≤ g, bending only at those fractions; on the interval containing 1/φ² the Fibonacci chain lies on the same line; there are at most three distinct shares per length and g + 1 words of each length g. This is classical (Morse and Hedlund 1940 [24]; Berthé 1996 [3]; the three-distance theorem) and was checked on all 1101 approximants for g ≤ 9. Two readings are ours. First, the approximants lie on the lower edge of the Window-Law plane (Corollary 5): pSS = max(0, 2a − 1), the smallest share of SS pairs any word with this a can have. Of the 747 primitive periodic words up to 12 letters, 157 lie on this edge (every word with no SS when a ≤ 1/2, or no LL when a ≥ 1/2) and 590 above it; of the 157, only 47 are balanced, and these are the approximants (Christoffel words). Zero defect at window 2 is therefore necessary, not sufficient: what singles out the approximants is that they stay on the quasicrystal's chamber line window after window, up to a length that grows with the period (below). So every pair count of an approximant is a line in a on each side of 1/2, and its kink at 1/2 is exactly 2Δ: twice the Swap defect. 8. Conclusion

What the law is. The Window Law is a general frame, and an elementary one: whatever a stacking's layers fix through a window of g layers averages to a linear function of the shares of the words of length g, and the window narrows exactly where the Swap defects vanish. DICTO's Hexagonality Law is its first step, the window of one layer, and DICTO's Swap Rule is the condition for a pair count to stay on it. Nothing in three kinds of crystal needed more than this frame.

What it adds. The Swap-defect reading, carried across three kinds of crystal: the coefficient of the one new number at window 2 is the Swap defect; the same pattern +1, −2, +1 marks the kinds of B in a perovskite and the kinds of bond and tetrahedron in the layered Frank–Kasper phases; the Yarmolyuk–Kripyakevich line is left only through one defect, in the same shape; and in the approximants the defect is a kink of twice the Swap defect in one dimension and a forbidden star with a fixed transfer vector in two. Alongside, specific new exact counts: clusters per B = 1 − h, A per B = 8 − 2h, the stacking-blind holes and the B–B shells of the perovskites; the window-2 catalogue and the stacking-blindness of the Frank–Kasper families; the Ammann–Beenker chamber laws, walls and defect; the cubic-cell tile formulas of the 3D approximants.

The bridge. DICTO's aim is a bridge between periodic and aperiodic order. The Window Law gives one plank of it: the periodic approximants of a quasicrystal carry zero defect on the chamber that holds the quasicrystal, window after window up to a length that grows with their period (zero defect at window 2 alone is shared by 110 other words up to 12 letters, whose shares part from the approximant's of the same share a at some longer window, since a periodic word is fixed by its factors), so every count of every approximant there lies on one straight line in the share of one tile, and the never-repeating limit lies on the same line. The defect does not shrink towards the limit; inside the chamber it is already zero, and what shrinks is only the distance along the line, the phason strain.

Part V. The end of the walk

Chapter 16. Narcissus and Covenant: One Half, Two Ways

From paper #98, DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24005929

Also a chapter of HexOct: The Hexagonality Law. Theorem: the HexOct h-tile's piece at the end of the walk, Narcissus, is its own mirror image and fills space alone; two of them joined on their hexagons make the twisted cube when put on straight and Covenant, a parallelogram dodecahedron that fills space by the fcc lattice, when turned 60°; and the Dehn invariants of all HexOct pieces are exact linear functions of the stretch.

Abstract. At the end of the HexOct walk (#75, λ ≥ 3/8) the octahedral pieces vanish and each layer tile is two copies of one piece: a cube in a c layer, and in an h layer a ten-cornered piece we call Narcissus. Narcissus is its own mirror image (three mirror planes), so copies of one shape fill space. Two copies joined on their hexagonal faces give two solids. Put on straight they make the twisted cube of #86; turned by 60° they make Covenant, a convex dodecahedron of twelve parallelograms, which is a parallelohedron and fills space by translation along the fcc lattice. The same law of two ways, straight or twisted, one free choice per layer, holds for the classical half of the rhombic dodecahedron (whose two ways are the RD and the TRD), as it holds for the octet pyramids in #92. We also give the Dehn invariants of every HexOct piece exactly: 36(5/24 − λ) ⊗ β for the h pieces (zero from λ = 5/24 on) and 24(3/8 − λ) ⊗ β for the c pieces, with β = arccos(1/√3).

Cut a solid in half, and you can put the halves back two ways: as they were, or with one turned. For most solids the turned version has a dent. For a few it does not, and for fewer still it fills space. This chapter is about the few.

1. Main theorem

Theorem. Let N (Narcissus) be the tetrahedral piece of the HexOct h-tile for λ ≥ 3/8, and C the cube of edge 1/√2 (ball spacing 1). (a) N has the symmetry C3v; in particular it is its own mirror image. The h-tile is two copies of N, so copies of N alone fill space. (b) Two copies of N joined on their hexagonal faces, the upper one turned over: put on straight they form the twisted cube of #86 (11 corners, 6 squares and 6 triangles); turned by 60° about the hexagon's centre they form Covenant, a convex dodecahedron with 12 parallelogram faces and 14 corners. Covenant is the zonohedron of three orthogonal segments of length 1/√2 and one along their body diagonal of a third of its length; it fills space by translation along the fcc lattice, one Covenant per ball. (c) Every stacking of c and h layers is filled by copies of N (h layers) and C (c layers). (d) The Dehn invariant of every piece of the HexOct tiles is exactly (s ⊗ β) with β = arccos(1/√3), where s = 36(5/24 − λ) for the h pieces with λ < 5/24, s = 0 for the h pieces with λ ≥ 5/24, and s = 24(3/8 − λ) for the c pieces (tetrahedral pieces with the opposite sign of the octahedral ones).

One self-mirroring piece, joined to itself two ways, gives two different space-fillers; and the stretch at which the HexOct pieces could start to fill space on their own is fixed by an exact straight line.

2. Narcissus

At λ = 3/8 the octahedral pieces of #75 shrink to nothing; for every larger λ the h-tile is the same two pieces (the walk saturates). Their corners are: a regular hexagon of side 1/√3 at the height of the next layer (three ball centres of that layer and three holes between them), three corners half a layer lower under the three holes, and one corner on the ball below the hexagon's centre. The faces are one hexagon, three quadrilaterals and six triangles; the dihedral angles are 90°, 120° and 135° only; the volume is √2/4 and the Mahler product exactly 34/3 (rational affine image). Three mirror planes contain its axis; the two copies in an h-tile are the same piece, one turned over. Copies of one piece therefore fill space (Theorem (a)). Within HexOct, Narcissus fills only the h layers: in a c layer the piece is the cube (Theorem (c), checked over all 32 five-layer contexts at λ = 3/8 and 2/5). The every-stacking statement is #86's, read with its tiles halved: two Narcissus make the twisted cube and two cubes the brick of #86's pair A.

3. Covenant

Turned by 60°, the union of two Narcissus is convex and centrally symmetric, and all twelve faces are parallelograms. Its edges run in four directions: three mutually orthogonal of length 1/√2 (the small fcc cube's edges) and one along the cube's body diagonal of a third of the diagonal's length. Every convex zonohedron of the five parallelohedron types fills space by translation (Fedorov; see Grünbaum [G]); Covenant's six face-to-face translations all have length 1 and generate the fcc lattice (twelve nearest neighbours at 1, six at √2). In the family of zonohedra of a cube's three edges and c times its body diagonal, c = 0 is the cube, c = 1 the rhombic dodecahedron; Covenant is c = 1/3. Its faces are not rhombi (edges 1/√2 and 1/√6), so it is none of the isozonohedra Grünbaum lists. Caution: in Covenant's tiling only the lower Narcissus of each pair has its corner on a ball; it is an fcc fundamental domain, not a ball-by-ball c-layer cell.

4. The same law for the rhombic dodecahedron

Cut across its three-fold axis through the centre, the rhombic dodecahedron falls into two congruent halves with a regular hexagon as cut, and the trapezo-rhombic dodecahedron (TRD) is made of the very same half: put back straight, the halves make the RD; one turned by 60°, the TRD (classical: Coxeter; the TRD is the RD with one half turned). Read as a standing option, the twist is a free choice per layer and never per cell: twisting one cell's half in a packing leaves gaps and overlaps, and a whole layer twists only if the layers above re-seat over the other holes; with each ball's halves turned to face its actual neighbours, every stacking tested (fcc, hcp, ABCB, ABCACB and the Fibonacci approximant ABACBCAC) is filled with no overlap. This is the law of #92 (one straight-or-twisted choice per layer, for the octet pyramids) for a second set of pieces. In Polyhedraverse, the half wears a two-colour seam: alternating colours across the seam mean the RD, matching colours the TRD (Figure 16.3).

Which solids can be halved and turned. A band (a prism) put between the two halves keeps every turn convex exactly when the central cut equals the solid's shadow along the axis. The RD satisfies this on its 2-, 3- and 4-fold axes; the truncated octahedron and the cube only on their 2- and 4-fold axes (cut/shadow 0.84 and 0.75 on the 3-fold axis, which is why the truncated octahedron's turned halves are dented). Only a hexagonal cut gives a genuine choice in a tiling, so the banded RD (half, hexagonal prism, half; straight or turned) is a family of space-fillers for every band height and every stacking. A leaning band dents at every lean; leaning a whole layer (a shear) keeps the tiling. Among the classical solids only the cuboctahedron (to J27) and the RD (to the TRD) turn convexly. The Dehn invariant is additive, so every rejoining of two Dehn-zero halves is Dehn-zero; what decides whether a turned solid can tile is the local count of dihedral angles round an edge. The turned B3 permutohedron, for instance, is Dehn-zero but fails that count (a 135° edge with no 90° partner). Halves of different solids with the same cut also rejoin: Narcissus and the RD half share their hexagon, and both of their joins (straight and turned) have only 90°, 120° and 135° dihedrals, so they pass both tests — candidates still to be decided.

5. Exact Dehn invariants of the HexOct pieces

Every dihedral angle of every HexOct piece is a rational multiple of π plus a multiple of β = arccos(1/√3): 70.53° = π − 2β, 109.47° = 2β, 125.26° = π − β, 144.74° = π/2 + β, and 90°, 120°, 135°. Squared edge lengths are rational in lattice coordinates, so each length is r√d with r rational and d squarefree. The Dehn invariant is therefore (Σ ce re√de) ⊗ β with integer ce, and is zero exactly when each rational sum Σ ce re over one d vanishes. Computed in exact fractions over the whole walk, only d = 1 occurs, and the sums are the straight lines of Theorem (d): 36(5/24 − λ) for the h pieces below 5/24 and 0 from 5/24 on; 24(3/8 − λ) for the c pieces. The octahedral and tetrahedral pieces of each tile carry opposite values, so every whole tile has Dehn invariant zero, as it must: Dehn's invariant [De01] decides scissors congruence with the cube together with volume [Sy65], and a polyhedron that tiles space has invariant zero [Deb80; LM95]. The two special stretches of HexOct are the two zeros: 5/24, where the h pieces could begin to fill space alone, and 3/8, where the c pieces become cubes and the h pieces Narcissus.

6. What is known and what is new

Known: the rhombic dodecahedron as a space-filler [Ke19], the RD/TRD relation and the Voronoi cells of the close packings [Co73; CBG08], the ortho/gyro operation [Jo66], the space-filling of every parallelohedron-type zonohedron [Fe85; Gr10; GS87], convex space-fillers in general [GS87; Se81; Go78; Go82], the stackings filled by #86's pair A, and the per-layer law for the octet pyramids (#92). Known in type (prior-art search, 5 November 2026): every convex zonohedron with four zones is combinatorially Kepler's rhombic dodecahedron and a parallelohedron (Fedorov 1885; Grünbaum 2010), so Covenant belongs to a classical type; in its family (the cube's three edge directions plus a fourth along a body diagonal, at a fraction c of the diagonal) the fraction c is an affine invariant, c = 1 being Kepler's dodecahedron and c = 1/3 Covenant. Narcissus, half of Covenant cut through its hexagonal waist, has ten faces (one hexagon, three quadrilaterals, six triangles), ten corners and eighteen edges, polyhedron formula 1036-64 in Goldberg's notation. Its combinatorial type is Goldberg's space-filler 10-XII (1982), the type whose hexagon is ringed by six triangles, which he builds by joining two opposite-hand heptahedra 7-XXIX at their lateral faces (7-XXIX: one of eight heptahedra cut from a regular triangular prism, Goldberg 1978). The other type with the same formula, 10-XIII (half of the pencil cube), rings its hexagon with triangles and quadrilaterals and is not this one. The type is therefore known as a space-filler. Narcissus also has the form of Goldberg's construction: cut on any of its three mirror planes, it splits into two mirror-image heptahedra, each of volume √2/8 (computed). Whether these halves are his 7-XXIX, and so whether Narcissus is metrically a member of his 10-XII class, needs the 1978 paper, which is not freely available and not yet read. Half of the rhombic dodecahedron is a different type (thirteen corners, one hexagon and nine quadrilaterals). New within the sources checked: Narcissus as this particular metric heptahedron, self-mirroring and filling space alone; its two joins, the twisted cube and Covenant as the c = 1/3 member of the four-zone family; the "cut equals shadow" criterion for banded turns; and the exact Dehn lines of the HexOct pieces. Open: whether the h-piece fills space alone for 5/24 ≤ λ < 3/8, where both necessary conditions (Dehn zero, the edge-angle count) hold.

Epilogue. What has been shown

Conclusion

The book set out to show that the endless variety of close-packed stackings is built from a few pieces, and that one rule governs how those pieces change. It has. The space of any stacking divides into finitely many kinds of piece, each borrowed whole or in half from the cubic or the hexagonal stacking (Chapter 1). When a stacking changes, pieces are never created from nothing; they swap, and the two mixed pieces add up to the two pure ones (Chapters 3 and 4). For every piece fixed by its two neighbouring layers, that swap is the same statement as a straight line: every layer keeps what it holds in its own pure stacking, so every count and volume is linear in the hexagonality h (Chapter 5), for random, faulted and never-repeating stackings alike (Chapters 6 and 7). Finite pieces, one rule of exchange, and from them every stacking: endless variety of order from a finite set of quanta.

The second part carried the law into matter. Where the property is a count fixed by geometry, real crystals follow the law exactly: hydrogen sites in titanium and zirconium (2 − h), shared faces in perovskites and battery cathodes (2h), eclipsed bonds in diamond, ice and semiconductors (h/2), kagome planes in magnets (1 + 3(1 − h)). Where the property depends on energy, the law is the first term, and the distance from it is one number per material, the reach of interactions beyond the next layer: tin, germanium, diamond, cobalt and iron lie close to the line; silicon further; silicon carbide, the best-known polytypic material, furthest. In spin ice and water ice the loops that set the frozen-in entropy do not see the stacking at all through every length computed, the law with zero slope (Chapter 13). Geometry fixes what every stacking must contain; the materials choose among the stackings by how far their forces reach.

The third part returned to the pieces. The Rule of Halves gave them; the two HexOct tiles, the c-tile and the h-tile, are what every layer of every stacking is made of, and they fit in any order (Chapter 14). The law gave the pieces, and the pieces prove the law: a string of the two tiles is a stacking, and each of the laws above is a statement about which tile each layer holds.

The fourth part set the laws in one frame. The Window Law (Chapter 15) holds for every count of every c/h stacking, with no geometry assumed: a count read from g neighbouring layers is a linear function of the shares of the words of g layers. The Hexagonality Law is its first case, the window of one layer, and it covers exactly what DICTO's definition names, the counts of pieces, holes, bonds and swaps, read from one layer or from two neighbouring layers in a way that obeys the Swap Rule. At two layers one more number enters, the share phh of neighbouring hexagonal pairs, and its coefficient is the Swap defect; so the Swap Rule is exactly the condition for a pair count to stay on the straight line, and a pair count beyond it is a plane in (h, phh). In the physicists' layer model of the second part, the Swap defect is the coupling J2, and J3 is the first coupling that needs three layers: the distance of each material from the line, measured in Chapters 8 to 12, is read window by window. The same frame holds in the perovskites (16 of 36 counts on the line, 9 planes, 11 needing longer windows), in the Frank–Kasper phases, which leave the Yarmolyuk–Kripyakevich line only through one defect, and in the approximants of quasicrystals, which carry zero defect on the chamber that holds the quasicrystal, so that the never-repeating limit lies on the same straight lines. That is one plank of the bridge between periodic and aperiodic order that the Fibonacci stacking began (Chapter 7).

How the rules fit together

The Window Law always holds, for every window g: a count read from g neighbouring layers is a linear function of the shares of the c/h words of length g. The Rule of Halves and the Swap Rule decide which window a count needs. When a count needs only one layer, or two layers and obeys the Swap Rule, the Window Law reduces to the Hexagonality Law (g = 1), the straight line in hexagonality. Pair counts that break the Swap Rule are planes in (h, phh).

About the author

DICTO is an artist in Osaka, Japan. His background is architectural sculpture, stonecarving and Buddhist statuary. He trained as an architectural stonecarver at the City and Guilds of London Art School (1998–2001), worked at St James's Palace, and recarved the Roman lettering of the Civil Service Rifles War Memorial at Somerset House, London, for its rededication in 2002. Since 2009 he has worked in Japan in the sculpture and restoration of Buddhist statues, among them a statue at Takaosan Jingo-ji, Kyoto, traditionally attributed to Kōbō Daishi Kūkai.

He builds geometric sculpture from recycled PET bottles with two modular systems of his own, B1NR and DICTOspheres, joined without glue. He holds six Japanese design registrations; five rest on spherical projections of the Platonic solids, the five forms behind his name: Dodecahedron, Icosahedron, Cube, Tetrahedron, Octahedron. His sculptures have been shown in Kyoto at the Kyoto Municipal Museum of Art (Interdependent Origination III, 2025), the Insho Domoto Museum of Fine Arts, the Kyoto Botanical Garden, the Kansho Exhibition and the Kyoto Takasegawa Art Festival.

The geometry behind the sculptures led to seventy-five papers, gathered in DICTO: Collected Geometric Works, and to four books: this one, The Golden Cycle, Alicia in Wonderland: The Red & White Queens, and Quanta of Space: A Manifesto (all 2026). Every paper and book is published openly with its own DICTO DOI at Zenodo. More at https://dictospheres.com.
