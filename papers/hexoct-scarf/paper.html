The HexOct scarf

A knitting pattern: DICTO's Hexagonality Law in two colours

DICTO · pattern of 31 October 2026 · for knitters in the family · CC BY-SA 4.0
The idea. Balls packed as tightly as possible lie in flat layers, and each layer sits on the one below in one of two ways: c (cubic) or h (hexagonal). Every such crystal is a sequence of c and h layers. Knit each layer as a band of rows, gold for c and blue for h, and the scarf is the stacking. The share of blue bands is the crystal's hexagonality; DICTO's Hexagonality Law says every count in the crystal is a straight line in that one number.

Materials and stitches

  • Two colours of the same yarn (double knitting / DK is easy): gold (c) and blue (h); any two strong colours work.
  • Needles to suit the yarn (about 4 mm for DK). Cast on about 40 stitches for a scarf 18–20 cm wide.
  • Garter stitch (knit every row) lies flat and needs no edging. One layer = 10 rows (5 ridges), about 2.5 cm; knit a small swatch first and adjust.
  • Change colour at the start of a right-side row; carry the unused colour up the side when the same colour returns within 2 bands, otherwise cut and weave in.

Making the law visible

  • A family of scarves: 3C, 6H, Fibonacci, 4H and 2H have hexagonality 0, 1/3, 0.382, 1/2 and 1. Laid side by side in that order they show the one number that orders every close-packed crystal.
  • The Fibonacci scarf never repeats: build it by the rule c → ch, h → c, starting from c. Two blue bands never touch, and the share of blue bands approaches 1/φ² (φ the golden ratio), as DICTO's paper #69 shows.
  • Your own stacking: any sequence of c and h is a possible crystal. Count the blue bands, divide by all the bands: that is its hexagonality.
