The Golden Ladder starts at one small triangle of mirrors. Why there, and why should anyone see that as a first step? This lesson climbs to it from nothing: from a point, through the kinds of triangle, the floor, the mirror and the fold, to the ball and its three mirror triangles. Every step has a mnemonic, a bridge to the step above and a bridge to the step below, so the ladder can be climbed both ways.

How to use this lesson

Read one step at a time. Say the mnemonic aloud, then cover the page and say the bridges in your own words: up, what this step makes possible; down, what it rests on. Answer the questions before turning to the answers at the end. Coming down, read the steps in reverse and use only the downward bridges.

Step 1 · A place, a path, a turn

A point marks a place and has no size. Two points fix exactly one straight line between them. Where two lines meet they make an angle, and an angle is best seen as a turn: a full turn is 360°, half a turn is 180° and makes a straight line, a quarter turn is 90°, the right angle, the corner of a sheet of paper.

Mnemonic Place, path, turn. A point is where; a line is between; an angle is how far you turn.

Bridge up ↑ Three points not on one line fix a triangle: three places, three paths, three turns.

Bridge down ↓ This is the ground floor.

Questions. (a) How many straight lines pass through two given points? (b) How many pass through one point? (c) What angle is a straight line?

Step 2 · How many triangles? How many kinds?

Any three points not on one line make a triangle, so there are infinitely many triangles. But many of them are the same triangle at a different size. Enlarge a triangle, as a photocopier does, and its angles do not change; triangles with the same angles are similar, the same shape. So the better question is how many shapes there are.

Cut the three corners off a paper triangle and lay them side by side: they always make a straight line (Figure 2). The angles of every flat triangle add up to 180°, half a turn. So once two angles are chosen the third is fixed, and a triangle's shape is given by just two numbers. Those two numbers can be anything that adds up to less than 180°, so there are infinitely many shapes as well; but now they can be laid out, named and compared.

Mnemonic Three corners make one straight line.

Bridge up ↑ Two angles fix a shape, so shapes can be sorted into kinds: step 3.

Bridge down ↓ The straight line is the half turn of step 1.

Questions. (a) Can a triangle have two right angles? (b) Two triangles have angles 50° and 60°. What is the third angle of each, and are they the same shape? (c) Why does size not matter for shape?

Step 3 · The kinds, and five special shapes

By their angles, triangles are acute (every angle under 90°), right (one angle exactly 90°) or obtuse (one angle over 90°). By their sides they are equilateral (three equal sides, so three angles of 60°), isosceles (two equal sides and two equal angles) or scalene (all different).

Five shapes carry this whole book (Figure 1). The equilateral triangle. Half a square, cut along its diagonal: its long side is √2 ≈ 1.414 times its short ones. Half an equilateral triangle, angles 90°, 60°, 30°: its sides are 1, √3 ≈ 1.732 and 2. And two shapes cut from the regular pentagon, whose diagonal is φ ≈ 1.618 times its side, the golden ratio φ = (1 + √5)/2: the golden triangle (36°, 72°, 72°) and the golden gnomon (108°, 36°, 36°). In each, long side to short side is φ.

Mnemonic Halve a square, find √2; halve a triangle, find √3; cut a pentagon, find φ.

Bridge up ↑ These numbers, 1, √2, √3 and φ, come back at the top of this rung as the numbers of the mirror triangles.

Bridge down ↓ Each special shape is one choice of two angles from step 2.

Questions. (a) Is the half-square triangle isosceles? (b) Which special triangle has an obtuse angle? (c) A pentagon has side 1. How long is its diagonal?

Step 4 · Round a point: tiling the floor

Lay tiles round a point with no gaps and no overlaps, and their angles at the point must add up to a full turn, 360°. Every triangle tiles the floor: two copies make a parallelogram, and parallelograms fill the floor in rows. Regular tiles are pickier. A regular polygon's corner is 60° (triangle), 90° (square), 108° (pentagon) or 120° (hexagon). Six triangles, four squares or three hexagons make exactly 360°, but three pentagons make 324° and leave a gap of 36°, and four would overlap (Figure 3).

Mnemonic Six, four, three: the floor is complete. The pentagon leaves thirty-six.

Bridge up ↑ The gap is not a failure. Close it by folding, and the floor rises into a solid corner: step 6.

Bridge down ↓ The full turn of step 1, and the angle sum of step 2 (six triangles of 60°).

Questions. (a) Why does a regular pentagon not tile the floor? (b) How many squares meet at a corner of a chessboard? (c) Does a scalene triangle tile the floor?

Step 5 · Mirrors: halve the turn, double the beads

One mirror makes one copy. Two mirrors hinged at an angle make many: if the angle is 180°/n, a single bead between them becomes a ring of 2n beads. At 90° you see 4, at 60° you see 6 (Figure 4), at 45° 8, at 36° 10. This is the kaleidoscope. The angle must be 180°/n exactly, or the copies do not close up into a ring.

Now put three mirrors round a triangle on the floor. The copies tile the floor only if every corner is a mirror angle 180°/n; with the 180° sum of step 2 that leaves exactly three triangles: 60°-60°-60°, 90°-45°-45° and 90°-60°-30°. Three of the special shapes of step 3 are the three flat kaleidoscopes.

Mnemonic Halve the turn, double the beads. A mirror angle of 180°/n gives 2n copies.

Bridge up ↑ Three mirrors meeting in space, like the corner of a box, make not a floor but a whole solid: steps 7 and 8.

Bridge down ↓ The full and half turns of step 1; the special triangles of step 3.

Questions. (a) Two mirrors at 30°: how many beads? (b) Why must a mirror angle be 180°/n? (c) Which flat mirror triangle is half an equilateral triangle?

Step 6 · Folding the floor: five solids, and no more

If the faces round a point add up to less than 360°, the gap can be closed by folding, and the corner rises out of the floor: a solid corner. Exactly 360° stays flat; more cannot fold into a corner that points outwards. With regular faces, at least three at each corner, there are exactly five ways (Figure 5), and each makes one Platonic solid. In DICTO order: the Dodecahedron (3 pentagons), the Icosahedron (5 triangles), the Cube (3 squares), the Tetrahedron (3 triangles) and the Octahedron (4 triangles). Euclid closes his Elements with this proof (Book XIII).

Count the gaps over a whole solid: dodecahedron 20 corners × 36°, icosahedron 12 × 60°, cube 8 × 90°, tetrahedron 4 × 180°, octahedron 6 × 120°. Every one comes to 720°, two full turns (Descartes).

Mnemonic D·I·C·T·O: Dodeca, Icosa, Cube, Tetra, Octa. And: every solid misses two full turns.

Bridge up ↑ Blow a solid up onto a sphere round it; its faces become curved triangles, and the 720° becomes the sphere itself: step 7.

Bridge down ↓ The gap of step 4 (the pentagon's 36°) is exactly the dodecahedron's corner.

Questions. (a) Why can six triangles not make a solid corner? (b) Why are there no Platonic solids with hexagon faces? (c) How many corners has the icosahedron, and what is its gap at each?

Step 7 · Triangles on a ball

Draw a triangle on a ball with arcs of great circles, the straightest lines on a sphere. Its angles add up to more than 180°, and the extra, the excess, measures its area. Cut an orange into eighths: each piece has three right angles, 270°, an excess of 90°, and 8 × 90° = 720° covers the whole ball. The two full turns of step 6 are the whole sphere, and any set of equal triangles that covers it has excesses adding up to 720°.

Mnemonic Flat: three corners make a line. Round: more than a line, and the extra is the area.

Bridge up ↑ Now let the triangles on the ball be mirrors: step 8.

Bridge down ↓ The 180° of step 2, and the 720° of step 6.

Questions. (a) A triangle on a ball has angles 90°, 90° and 60°. What is its excess? (b) How many such triangles cover the ball? (c) Can a triangle on a ball have three right angles?

Step 8 · The triangle of mirrors: the first rung

Every Platonic solid has mirror planes through its centre: cut along one and the halves are mirror images. These planes cut the sphere round the solid into small identical triangles, and every one has the angles 90°, 60° and 180°/p: p = 3 for the tetrahedron, p = 4 for the cube and octahedron, p = 5 for the dodecahedron and icosahedron. Each is a kaleidoscope of three mirrors (step 5): one point inside it, reflected again and again, makes the corners of a whole solid.

Its excess is 90° + 60° + 180°/p − 180°. For p = 3 that is 30°, so 720°/30° = 24 triangles; for p = 4, 15° and 48; for p = 5, 6° and 120. For p = 6 the excess is 0: the triangle is the flat 90°-60°-30° of steps 3 and 5, and it tiles the floor of close-packed balls instead of the sphere. For p = 7 or more the excess is negative and no triangle closes on a ball. That is why there are three families and five solids.

Each triangle carries one number, 2 cos(180°/p): 1 for p = 3, √2 for p = 4, φ for p = 5, √3 for the flat p = 6, the numbers of the special triangles of step 3. Everything golden in this book lives with φ, in the p = 5 triangle. That is the first rung: Chapter 1 of The Golden Ladder, "One Triangle, Three Numbers", begins here.

Mnemonic Three, four, five curl into a ball; six lies flat. One, root two, phi; root three for the floor.

Bridge up ↑ Chapter 1 of The Golden Ladder: where a point sits in this triangle decides the solid, and the golden cuts are points on its sides.

Bridge down ↓ Its corners are the mirror pairs of step 5 (90°, 60°, 180°/p); its excess is the area of step 7; its three families are the five solids of step 6.

Questions. (a) What are the angles of the cube's mirror triangle? (b) Why is there no mirror triangle with p = 7 on the sphere? (c) Which number belongs to the dodecahedron's triangle?

Answers

Step 1. (a) One. (b) Infinitely many. (c) 180°, half a turn.

Step 2. (a) No: two right angles already make 180°, leaving nothing for the third. (b) 70° for both; yes, the same shape (similar). (c) Enlarging changes the sides but not the angles, and the angles fix the shape.

Step 3. (a) Yes: two equal sides, and angles 45° and 45°. (b) The golden gnomon (108°). (c) φ ≈ 1.618.

Step 4. (a) 108° does not divide 360°: three leave 36°, four overlap by 72°. (b) Four. (c) Yes: every triangle tiles the floor.

Step 5. (a) 12 (30° = 180°/6, so 2 × 6). (b) Otherwise the copies do not close up into a ring. (c) 90°-60°-30°.

Step 6. (a) Six times 60° is 360°: the corner stays flat. (b) Three hexagons already make 360°: flat, no gap to fold. (c) 12 corners, a gap of 60° at each (12 × 60° = 720°).

Step 7. (a) 60° (240° − 180°). (b) 720°/60° = 12. (c) Yes: the orange eighth, 90°-90°-90°.

Step 8. (a) 90°, 60°, 45°. (b) 90° + 60° + 180°/7 is less than 180°: the excess is negative. (c) φ, the golden ratio.
