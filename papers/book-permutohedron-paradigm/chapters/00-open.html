Ring three bells, one after another: 1, 2, 3. Ring them again, but let two neighbours swap places: 2, 1, 3. Keep going, one swap of neighbours at a time, and after six rows you are back at 1, 2, 3, having rung every order of the three bells once. Now write each row as a point, with the bells' places as its coordinates, and join two rows when one swap turns one into the other. The six points are the corners of a hexagon, and each of its six sides is one swap. This book follows that single move. With four values the swaps build Kelvin's cell, the shape soap foam strives for. Inside living tissue, the same move is a cell trading one neighbour for another, the scutoid. One dimension up, DICTO found that every solid of the right kind, swept by a moving slice, is a whole tissue of such swaps, counted exactly. And at the top of the climb, every solid in the book turns out to be one family, joined by swaps. Then the book comes back down, to the bells.

Write the values 1, 2, 3 in every order and join two orders when one becomes the other by swapping two neighbouring values: the six orders are the corners of a hexagon. With 1, 2, 3, 4 they are the 24 corners of the truncated octahedron, Kelvin's cell; with 1, …, 5, the 120 corners of a four-dimensional solid. That solid of all orders is the permutohedron. It is a classical object, named by Guilbaud and Rosenstiehl in 1963 and studied by Schoute half a century before them; what is new in this book is the use DICTO makes of it. Seen through the permutohedron, much of his earlier work turns out to be one story: the truncated triangle is the permutohedron of three values, Kelvin's cell and the whole golden-bond slider are permutohedra of four values, and the 4D Vacuus is held together by permutohedra of four values at the heart of the permutohedron of five. "Now we have the permutohedron paradigm, everything earlier reconfigures slightly" (DICTO, 11 November 2026; spelling tidied).

One word. DICTO's papers and the chapters first drafted for this book call the move an exchange of neighbours; the series to which the book belongs calls it a swap. In this book the two words are one word: an exchange is a swap, and every edge of every permutohedron is one.

The arc: one move from beginning to end

One move runs through every chapter: two neighbouring values change places. It is every edge of every permutohedron, the step of a slider, the trade of neighbours inside a scutoid, a contact gained and a contact lost in a 4D tissue, a wall of the Swap Rule, the edge direction that every member of the family keeps, and the stroke of a band of ringers.

Beginning: one swap. One swap of neighbouring values, repeated, draws the hexagon, and every edge of every permutohedron is a swap (Chapter {{ch:three}}). The same move builds Kelvin's cell, which fills space alone (Chapter {{ch:kelvin}}), the slider of solids that fill space in pairs, with its silent golden station (Chapter {{ch:slider}}), and the bonds between two kinds of site (Chapter {{ch:bonds}}), until, in a build of DICTO's, the edges stop being swaps (Chapter {{ch:rd}}).

Middle: the climb into the cells. In a slice a swap is a scutoid, a cell that trades one neighbour for another (Chapter {{ch:scut3}}). One dimension up, the permutohedron of five values is the web of swaps, with its Vacuus and the mirrors of the Queens (Chapter {{ch:web}}). The centre of the book: swept by a moving slice, it is a tissue of cells that are born, swap neighbours and die, counted by the Eulerian numbers (Chapter {{ch:centre1}}), and so is every simple 4-polytope, its swaps counted by its h-vector and balanced between the Queens, 601 each way (Chapter {{ch:centre2}}). Where many neighbours swap at once the tissue makes rosettes, which balance exactly with symmetry and fail to without it (Chapter {{ch:rosettes}}). The walls of the Swap Rule of close packing are 4D scutoids (Chapter {{ch:walls}}). Then the swaps are counted in every dimension (Chapter {{ch:everyn}}) and the golden slice is cut (Chapter {{ch:golden}}).

Summit: one family. Slide the walls of the permutohedron without turning them and every edge stays parallel to a swap: every solid of the climb lies in one family, from Kelvin's cell, which fills space, to the associahedron, which hands out a law of particle physics (Chapter {{ch:family}}).

End: the bells. Ringers may only swap neighbours. Three bells ring the hexagon of this first page, four ring Kelvin's cell, five ring the 4D permutohedron of the centre (Chapter {{ch:bells}}). The book ends where it began, now heard.

Where the paradigm came from

The DICTO 1:2 Truncated Octahedron grew from two lines of DICTO's work. The first began in Polyhedraverse, where rhombic dodecahedra cut in half and rejoined gave a slightly irregular truncated octahedron that still filled space (#58). #48 then placed truncated octahedra in a family T(x), each filling space with its partner T(1/x), from Kelvin's cell at x = 1 to the golden pair, the Icosaxes and the Unborn Axes, at x = τ; the DICTO 1:2 is its member x = 1/2. #52 read the family as one weight sliding between the two kinds of site of the caesium-chloride network, and #93 proved that each pair on the walk fills space in that one way only. The hexagons of the DICTO 1:2 are truncated triangles, the face that #88 found running through the golden cuts: there their sides are in the ratio φ, here in the ratio 2; and #88 and #89 found the golden pairs as slices of four-dimensional solids. The second line is the Vacuus: #44 cut the Platonic solids from their centres into Aristotle's pyramids, and #87 kept only the walls between those pyramids, one fin on every edge, so that the octahedron's Vacuus is three squares through its centre and the cuboctahedron's four hexagons. #105 joined the two: one dimension up, the walls become three-dimensional central slices of the 4D permutohedron, and the five at its heart are DICTO 1:2 Truncated Octahedra, the stop x = 1/2 of the slider of #48. Read back through the permutohedron, the path has one thread, the swap of neighbours, and this book follows it.

What this book shows

(1) The swap builds solids. The truncated triangle is the permutohedron of three values: for values a < b < c the six orders span a hexagon with every angle 120° and sides alternating √2(b − a) and √2(c − b), and every such hexagon arises this way; so every 2-face of every permutohedron is a truncated triangle or a rectangle, and DICTO's golden cuts are gap ratios (Chapter {{ch:three}}, Lemma {{s:o1.1}}). The 36 edges of Kelvin's cell are 12 swaps of each of three pairs (Chapter {{ch:kelvin}}). The golden loop of DICTONIC is the symmetric spacings of four values, each filling space with its reciprocal in one way only, and the fourth harmonic falls silent at its golden stop alone (Chapter {{ch:slider}}); the weight that drives the web of bonds is the relative difference of the gaps, d = (g − m)/(g + m) (Chapter {{ch:bonds}}, Theorem {{s:o3.1}}). DICTO's fourteen Hemi RDs close into the chamfered cube, a zonohedron that is not a permutohedron, and with cube pyramids it fills space, both exactly (Chapter {{ch:rd}}).

(2) The centre: DICTO's scutoid tissues. Scutoids are swaps seen in a slice, and they make flat-faced shells and a cycle that fills space (Chapter {{ch:scut3}}). The 150 two-dimensional faces of the 4D permutohedron lie in exactly 65 central slices of four kinds, and five DICTO 1:2 Truncated Octahedra, slotted along ten regular hexagons, make its heart; a face lies on a mirror exactly when the Mirror Law says so (Chapter {{ch:web}}). A sweep of the 4D permutohedron is a scutoid tissue, 26 births, 66 swaps and 26 deaths (Chapter {{ch:centre1}}), and a sweep of any simple 4-polytope is a scutoid tissue counted by its h-vector, with a balance law between the Queens (Chapter {{ch:centre2}}). Which uniform polytopes sweep cleanly and which only in rosettes is read from the Coxeter chain, and every rosette tissue of the Atlas balances too, by symmetry; without symmetry the law fails (Chapter {{ch:rosettes}}). The Swap Rule of close packing below s = 1/4 is proved stacking by stacking and its walls are swaps one dimension up (Chapter {{ch:walls}}).

(3) Every dimension. The Vacuus slices of the permutohedron of n values are counted for every n, 3, 15, 65, 225, 770, 2443, 7761, and its sweep by the Eulerian numbers (Chapter {{ch:everyn}}); the golden cut through the 4D permutohedron filling is a filling of space by cells of exactly 35 kinds, non-periodic in one direction only (it repeats along a plane), whose window version has 60 tile shapes (Chapter {{ch:golden}}).

(4) The summit and the return. Moving the walls instead of the values gives the permutohedron's whole family, from Kelvin's cell to the associahedron, whose canonical form is a scattering amplitude; the road between them keeps the permutohedron's shape to the last moment, and in three dimensions the members that fill space by sliding are exactly Fedorov's five (Chapter {{ch:family}}). English change ringing walks the permutohedron: on four bells there are exactly 5396 extents, the three double methods are the most symmetric, and the plain hunt is the only shortest way from rounds to backrounds, for every number of bells (Chapter {{ch:bells}}).

How the book is made

Each chapter opens with a paragraph for a general reader (in a box with a red rule), retells earlier results through the permutohedron or adds new ones, works one example by hand, and ends with the question it hands to the next chapter. Under every chapter title a line names the papers it comes from with their DICTO DOIs, or says that the chapter is new. Theorems, figures and sections are numbered by chapter (Theorem {{s:o6.2}} is the second numbered statement of Chapter {{ch:web}}). Every result is labelled as it stands: proved (with the proof or the paper that proves it), exact (computed in exact arithmetic, with the study named), numerical, or open; the Epilogue lists the main claims of every chapter with their status, and the appendix names the study and commit behind each check. The known theory is credited to its authors throughout. Where a result has its home in another book of the series, the chapter retells it and names the home.

Definitions used

The series takes its definitions from the DICTOnary (Book One, II: The DICTOnary of Atoms): one name per shape, an established literature name kept and DICTO's beside it. This book uses these names: permutohedron (classical; Guilbaud and Rosenstiehl); Kelvin's cell, the truncated octahedron (Kelvin 1887); truncated triangle (Coxeter's t{3}; #20, #88); elongated hexagon (DICTO, Chapter {{ch:rd}}); the golden cuts and the Greater and Lesser Golden Cut solids (#88, #89); Icosaxes and Unborn Axes (#48); DICTO 1:2 and DICTO 2:1 Truncated Octahedron (#105); Hemi RD (DICTO's half rhombic dodecahedron, Chapter {{ch:rd}}); Vacuus (#87); scutoid (Gómez-Gálvez et al. 2018) and 4D scutoid (#104); White Queen and Red Queen, the 600-cell and the 120-cell (#63, #65); 13-COA, the augmented cuboctahedron (Stewart 1970; DICTO's name, Chapter {{ch:family}}).
