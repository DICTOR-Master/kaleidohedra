Change how far apart the four values are, keeping them balanced about their middle, and the solid keeps all the cube's symmetry while it changes shape: a slider, from the octahedron through Kelvin's cell to the cuboctahedron. Every stop on it fills space with one partner, in one way only. And if you listen to the corners as notes on a sphere, the slider falls silent in its fourth tone at exactly one stop, the golden one.

The 24 orders of 1, 2, 3, 4 are the corners of Kelvin's truncated octahedron. Space the four values differently and the corners move, but the solid keeps its 6 four-sided faces and 8 hexagons (Chapter {{ch:o1}}). Most spacings break its symmetry. This chapter shows which spacings keep all of it, and that those are exactly the solids of DICTONIC's golden loop.

Proposition {{S:o2.1}}. The permutohedron of four values v₁ < v₂ < v₃ < v₄ has the 24 symmetries of the regular tetrahedron for every choice of values, and the 48 symmetries of the cube exactly when the values are symmetric about their mean (v₁ + v₄ = v₂ + v₃, gaps g : m : g).

Proof. Permuting the four coordinates maps the set of orders to itself: 24 symmetries. The solid is a truncated octahedron combinatorially, whose combinatorial symmetries number 48, so there are 24 or 48. A group of 48 orthogonal maps containing the tetrahedral group is the full octahedral group, which contains the central inversion x ↦ −x (mean at the origin); so all 48 occur exactly when the inversion is a symmetry, that is, when −v₁, …, −v₄ are again v₁, …, v₄: the values are symmetric about their mean. ∎ (Checked by computing the full symmetry group of the corners for four symmetric and three non-symmetric spacings: symmetry.py.)

Theorem {{S:o2.2}} (#48, Theorem 4; #105, Theorem 4; #93, Theorem A). Let T(x), x > 0, be the permutohedron of four values with gaps x : 1 : x (the hull of the signed orders of (1 + x, x, 0); Koca et al.'s orbit). Then copies of T(x) at the points of a cubic lattice and of T(1/x) at the centres of its cubes fill space, face to face, and every tiling by copies of the two is this one.

Proof. The filling is #48, Theorem 4; the identification of #48's T(x) with the permutohedron of gaps x : 1 : x is #105, Theorem 4; the uniqueness is #93, Theorem A, where T(x) and T(1/x) are Pμ and Qμ with μ = 1 + x/(1 + x) (#90, Proposition 6). ∎

The silent station

A solid can be listened to. Give each of its corners, seen from the centre, a unit weight on the sphere and expand in spherical harmonics: the strength Ql of each degree l is the solid's harmonic fingerprint, the bond-orientational order of Steinhardt, Nelson and Ronchetti (1983), with which physicists tell cubic, hexagonal and icosahedral order of atoms apart. Cubic solids ring in the fourth degree; icosahedral ones never do. #94 applied it to the sweep Pμ = C ∩ μC* of the cube and its polar octahedron, of which the slider of this chapter is the octahedron half: T(x) is Pμ with μ = 1 + x/(1 + x) (#90, Proposition 6), from the octahedron (μ = 1) through Kelvin's cell (μ = 3/2) to the cuboctahedron (μ = 2).

Theorem {{S:silent}} (#94, Main theorem (A), (B)). On the slider the fourth harmonic Q4 vanishes at exactly one stop, μ = φ, the golden stop x = φ, Icosaxes. There Ql vanishes exactly for l = 2, 4, 8, 14 among the even degrees up to 16, the even degrees with no icosahedral harmonic: the 24 corners of Icosaxes are the corners of two icosahedra.

Proof (#94). For 1 < μ ≤ 2 the corners are the 24 points (±1, ±a, 0) in all orders, a = μ − 1. With the cubic harmonic K4 = x⁴ + y⁴ + z⁴ − (3/5)r⁴, Q4 vanishes exactly when the mean of K4/r⁴ over the corners does, that is when (1 + a⁴)/(1 + a²)² = 3/5, or a⁴ − 3a² + 1 = (a² + a − 1)(a² − a − 1) = 0; in 0 < a < 1 the only root is a = 1/φ, μ = φ. At a = 1/φ the corners are the cyclic arrangements of (0, ±1, ±1/φ) in both orders, the two icosahedra of the pyritohedral pair, and an icosahedron is silent in every degree that carries no icosahedral harmonic. ∎

Worked example: the silent station. At μ = φ, a = μ − 1 = 1/φ ≈ 0.618, so a² = 1/φ² ≈ 0.382 and a⁴ ≈ 0.146, and a⁴ − 3a² + 1 ≈ 0.146 − 1.146 + 1 = 0. Exactly: 1/φ² = 2 − φ, so a⁴ − 3a² + 1 = (2 − φ)² − 3(2 − φ) + 1 = φ² − φ − 1 = 0. At Kelvin's cell (a = 1/2) the same expression is 1/16 − 3/4 + 1 = 5/16, not zero: Kelvin's cell is loud in the fourth tone, as every cubic solid is.

So the slider carries two special stops for two different reasons. Kelvin's cell is where the pair swap stands still, and the laws it carries there (filling space alone, the most wall per cell) are forced by that swap (Chapter {{ch:kelvin}}). The golden stop is where the corners become two icosahedra; the swap study found no swap of this line whose fixed point is φ, so the silence is a law of its own kind, a gap ratio meeting the five-fold symmetry, not a fixed point.

Symmetric gaps give one slider; each stop fills space with its partner in one way only, and at the golden stop the fourth harmonic falls silent. What holds each pair together?
