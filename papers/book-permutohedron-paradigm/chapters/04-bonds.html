Put two kinds of site in space, like the caesium and chlorine atoms of caesium chloride, one at the corners of a cubic grid and the other at the centres of its cubes, and let one kind pull harder than the other. Each site claims the space closest to it in the weighted sense, and the cells they claim are permutohedra of four values. How hard they pull is how unevenly the values are spaced.

In the web of bonds (#52, #53) one number drives everything: the weight difference d between the two kinds of site of the body-centred cubic arrangement. Sliding d from −1 to 1 moves the shared walls, and the cells pass from the octahedron through the DICTO 1:2 (d = −1/3), Kelvin's truncated octahedron (d = 0), the golden bond (d = 1/τ³) and the DICTO 2:1 (d = 1/3) to the cuboctahedron; #52 and #53 call this the golden loop. Chapter {{ch:o2}} read the cells as permutohedra of four values; this chapter shows that the weight itself is a statement about those values: it measures how unevenly they are spaced.

Theorem {{S:o3.1}} (#90, Proposition 6, and #52, read through #105, Theorem 4). Place sites of kind A at the points of 2ℤ³ and of kind B at 2ℤ³ + (1, 1, 1), and divide space by power distance |y − s|² − ws with wA − wB = d, −1 < d < 1. Then the cell of each A site is the permutohedron of four values spaced with gaps g, m, g, where

d = (g − m) / (g + m),

and the cell of each B site is the permutohedron whose gaps are in the reciprocal ratio, m, g, m (the same formula with −d).

Proof. By #90 (Proposition 6) the A cell is C ∩ μC* with μ = (3 + d)/2, that is {|yi| ≤ 1, |e·y| ≤ (3 + d)/2} for the eight e = (±1, ±1, ±1): the walls towards the six nearest A sites 2ei and the eight nearest B sites e. (No other site cuts it: the power cell lies inside this region, and these regions for A and B already fill space by #90, so the two are equal; checked also exactly, corner by corner, against every site within ±8 at d = −99/100, −1/3, 0, 1/τ³, 1/3 and 99/100.) The permutohedron of −q, −p, p, q is, after the isometry of #105 (Theorem 4), the hull of the signed orders of (p + q, q − p, 0): its squares lie on the planes |yi| = p + q and its hexagons on |e·y| = 2q. Scaling to p + q = 1 and matching 2q = (3 + d)/2 gives q = (3 + d)/4 and p = (1 − d)/4, so the gaps are g = q − p = (1 + d)/2 and m = 2p = (1 − d)/2, and (g − m)/(g + m) = d. Changing d to −d exchanges g and m. ∎ (Checked exactly at d = −1/3, 0, 1/τ³, 1/3 and 2/7, corner by corner: weight_map.py, log kept with the study.)

At d = 0 (equal weights, the body-centred cubic Voronoi tiling) the gaps are equal: Kelvin's cell. The partner T(1/x) of #48 is the same rule seen from the other kind of site. The golden bond is the golden spacing. At d = (τ − 1)/(τ + 1) = 1/τ³ the outer gaps are τ times the middle one; by Chapter {{ch:o1}} every hexagon of the cell is then a golden truncated triangle. The DICTO pair is the spacing 1 : 2. At d = −1/3 (μ = 4/3) the A cell is the permutohedron of 1, 2, 4, 5 and the B cell that of −5, −1, 1, 5; by #93 (Theorem A, P4/3 and Q4/3) they fill space in this one way only.

In #48's parameter, x = (1 + d)/(1 − d) is the gap ratio g/m, and the weight is d = (x − 1)/(x + 1), the form given in #53 (Corollary 1); #52 states the same walk at the scale L = φ (φ = τ, the golden ratio), where the weight difference carries the factor φ²/4; #90's μ is (3 + d)/2.

Worked example: the values 1, 2, 4, 5. The gaps are g = 1, m = 2, g = 1, so d = (1 − 2)/(1 + 2) = −1/3: the A cell is the DICTO 1:2 Truncated Octahedron. Its partner, with −d = 1/3, has gaps in the reciprocal ratio, 2 : 1 : 2, for example the values −5, −1, 1, 5 (gaps 4, 2, 4): the DICTO 2:1. Equal gaps give d = 0, Kelvin's cell; gaps φ : 1 : φ give d = (φ − 1)/(φ + 1) = 1/φ³, the golden bond.

The bond weight is d = (g − m)/(g + m), a ratio of swap lengths, and every edge of every bond cell is a swap. Are all of DICTO's solids built by swaps?
