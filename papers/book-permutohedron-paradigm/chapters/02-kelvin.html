In 1887 Lord Kelvin proposed the truncated octahedron as the way to divide space into cells of equal volume with the least wall between them; slightly curved, it stood as the best answer for more than a century, until Weaire and Phelan beat it in 1994 with a foam of two kinds of cell. The truncated octahedron is also the solid of all 24 orders of 1, 2, 3, 4, joined by swaps. This chapter reads Kelvin's cell as that solid: its 36 edges are swaps of three kinds, its squares and hexagons are the two ways two swaps can meet, and it is the one solid of its line that fills space alone.

Write the four values 1, 2, 3, 4 in every order, as points of space: (1, 2, 3, 4), (2, 1, 3, 4), and so on, 24 points in all. They lie in the flat three-dimensional space of points whose coordinates add up to 10, and their hull is a solid with 6 squares and 8 regular hexagons, every edge of one length: the truncated octahedron, Kelvin's cell. Each corner has three edges, one for each pair of neighbouring values, 1 and 2, 2 and 3, 3 and 4.

Thirty-six swaps of three kinds

Proposition {{S:kelvin36}} (classical, read as swaps). The 36 edges of the permutohedron of four values are 12 swaps of each of the three pairs of neighbouring values. Its 6 four-sided faces are the places where a swap of the two smallest values and a swap of the two largest are made in either order; its 8 hexagons are the places where three values next in size cycle, 4 for the values 1, 2, 3 and 4 for 2, 3, 4.

Proof. At every corner one edge swaps each pair k, k + 1 (Chapter {{ch:three}}), so there are 24 · 3 / 2 = 36 edges, and each pair is swapped along 24 / 2 = 12 of them. By Corollary {{s:o1.2}} a 2-face moves either two disjoint pairs, here only {1, 2} with {3, 4}, which gives the four-sided faces, 4!/(2! 2!) = 6 of them (the positions of the two smallest values), or three consecutive values, {1, 2, 3} or {2, 3, 4}, with the remaining value at any of the four positions: 4 + 4 = 8 hexagons. ∎

Worked example: at the corner (1, 2, 3, 4). Swapping the first pair gives (2, 1, 3, 4), the last pair (1, 2, 4, 3), and both, in either order, (2, 1, 4, 3): four corners, a square, because the two swaps touch different values and commute. Swaps among 1, 2, 3 go round a hexagon, one swap of neighbouring values at a time: (1, 2, 3, 4) → (2, 1, 3, 4) → (3, 1, 2, 4) → (3, 2, 1, 4) → (2, 3, 1, 4) → (1, 3, 2, 4) → back, six corners, the values 1 and 2 and then 2 and 3 swapped in turn. Counting all the corners, edges and faces: 24 − 36 + 14 = 2, Euler's number of every solid without holes.

The one that fills space alone

Spread the four values symmetrically, with gaps x, 1, x, and Kelvin's cell becomes the slider of Chapter {{ch:slider}}, from the octahedron to the cuboctahedron. Every solid of the slider fills space with a partner (Theorem {{s:o2.2}}). Kelvin's cell is the only one that fills space alone, and #100 proves it with Dehn's invariant, the sum over the edges of length ⊗ angle (Dehn 1901), which vanishes for every solid that tiles space by copies of itself (Debrunner 1980): on the sweep from the octahedron through Kelvin's cell and the cuboctahedron to the cube, the invariant is zero only at Kelvin's cell and at the cube (#100, Theorem 1.1; Figure {{f:dehn}}).

The swap explains where the zero falls. The two cells of the two-site pattern behind the slider (Chapter {{ch:bonds}}) change places under μ ↔ 3 − μ, and Kelvin's cell, at μ = 3/2, is the one left in place. On that line the Dehn coefficient of the cell is 12√2(3 − 2μ), a quantity that changes sign under the swap, so it must vanish at the fixed point; and the wall area per cell is the same at μ and 3 − μ and bends down, so it is largest there (studies/2026-11-13-swaps-and-fixed-points, checks K3 and K4, proved there in exact arithmetic). Kelvin's cell is where the swap stands still. This is one of the few places where a law sits at the fixed point of a swap for a stated reason; the study that catalogued the swaps of the series found that most laws do not.

Kelvin's cell with its neighbours

In the filling, each Kelvin's cell meets 14 others, 8 across its hexagons and 6 across its squares. #26 builds the thirteen of them that sit round one cell in three layers, 4 + 5 + 4: twelve neighbours, 8 across hexagons and 4 across squares, with the top and bottom squares left open as two windows onto the gold centre (Figure {{f:to13}}). It is one of the seven periodic thirteens of #26, each a cell and twelve neighbours in its honeycomb. Seen through this book, every contact in the block is a face of the solid of orders: a hexagon where three values cycle, a square where two far-apart swaps commute. The same cell also arose from the other side: rhombic dodecahedra cut in half and rejoined in Polyhedraverse gave a slightly irregular truncated octahedron that still filled space (#58); Chapter {{ch:rd}} follows a later build of that kind to where it leaves the family.

With equal gaps the swaps build Kelvin's cell, which fills space alone, at the one place where the pair swap of its line stands still. What if the gaps, the lengths of the swaps, move?
