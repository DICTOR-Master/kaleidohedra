Stack layers of balls as greengrocers stack oranges. Each layer sits in the hollows of the one below, in one of three positions, A, B or C, and a layer is c if its two neighbours differ and h if they are alike. The holes between the balls divide space into pieces, and the Swap Rule of #61 says how those pieces add up. Where the pieces change, a single cell in four dimensions trades its neighbours: a 4D scutoid.

The swaps of neighbours of Chapters {{ch:scut3}}, {{ch:centre1}} and {{ch:centre2}} have a physical home: the close packing of spheres, where #61's Swap Rule says which pieces each layer holds. This chapter gives #104's proof of the rule below s = 1/4 and reads its walls as exchanges of neighbours one dimension up.

The Swap Rule below s = 1/4

Setup (#61, The Swap Rule). Layers of balls (spacing 1 in a layer) are stacked in a word in A, B, C with layer distance d, d² = (2/3)s (s = 1: close packing). Between two layers lie octahedral and tetrahedral holes; space is divided among the holes by power distance |x − p|² − w, w = 0 for an octahedral hole and w = λ for a tetrahedral one, with λ in #61's range −1/4 − s/8 ≤ λ ≤ 3s/8 (for s ≤ 1/4). A layer is c if its two neighbours differ, h if they are equal. A hole's piece in the gap between layers k and k + 1 is keyed by its role (octahedral, or one of the two tetrahedral kinds) and the types of the four layers k − 1, k, k + 1, k + 2; the types of the two inner layers are (t | t′), the two outer ones the context. V(t | t′) is the piece's volume with the context held fixed. #61 proved V(c|h) + V(h|c) = V(c|c) + V(h|h) when s ≥ 1/4.

The four-layer condition. A piece's context is its role (octahedral, or one of the two tetrahedral kinds) together with the types, c or h, of the four layers k − 1, k, k + 1, k + 2 round its gap. A stacking satisfies the four-layer condition at (s, λ) when each of its pieces is determined, up to a motion, by its context. The six stackings below contain all sixteen contexts, and their pieces are computed directly; for any other stacking Theorem {{s:o9.1}} assumes the condition. Below λ = −9s/8 it fails: a piece reaches two gaps.

Theorem {{S:o9.1}} (#104, Theorem 3; computer-assisted proof). For every s in [1/8, 1/4], in the six stackings ABC, AC, ACBC, ACABC, ACBCAC, ACBABC (which contain all sixteen four-layer contexts), and in every stacking that satisfies the four-layer condition, the Swap defect D = V(c|h) + V(h|c) − V(c|c) − V(h|h) of each role is the same for all four contexts and equals: for −9s/8 ≤ λ ≤ −(4 − s)/24 (when s > 1/7), δtet = −(1 − 4s)³/(54s), δoct = (1 − 4s)²(72λ + 8 + 13s)/(108s); for max(−9s/8, −(4 − s)/24) ≤ λ ≤ −5s/8, δoct = (8λ + 5s)³/(64s), δtet = −(8λ + 5s)²(8λ + 2 − 3s)/(64s); and D = 0, the Swap Rule holds, from λ = −5s/8 to 3s/8 (a further wall at −s/8 leaves D = 0). Below −9s/8 a piece reaches two gaps and the four layers do not fix it (open).

Proof (exact, computer-assisted). s enters only the vertical metric, so every power-diagram plane has a normal linear in s and an offset linear in s and λ; for fixed s only the right-hand sides carry λ, so corners and slacks are affine in λ, and a slack is non-negative on a cell of the map iff it is on the cell's two bounding walls: one-variable rational functions of s. On each cell of the map, built at one sample point for the six words, every corner was re-solved symbolically over Q(s) and every plane's slack proved non-negative on the whole cell (Bernstein sign tests over the s-interval, exact root counting otherwise; 35,561,184 exact sign checks); the volumes and D(s, λ) agree with the closed forms as rational identities. Neighbourhood. The cells were built from all sites within squared distance 24 (every plane's slack proved non-negative on the whole cell). No farther site can cut a cell: for any point x of a hole's cell, the octahedral holes of the nearest gap form a triangular lattice of spacing 1 at vertical offset ≤ d/2, so the nearest of them, pk, has |x − pk|² ≤ 1/3 + 1/24 = 3/8, and hence |x − pi|² ≤ 3/8 + |λ| < 0.67: every cell lies within ρ < 0.82 of its site, and (√24 − 0.82)² − 0.82² > 15.9 exceeds every weight difference |λ| ≤ 0.29. The walls were first found exactly along λ (the Hopscotch walk: each wall is a root where a corner meets a plane) at six spacings. ∎

What happens at the walls. The λ-stack of the 3D power diagrams is a 4D cell complex; in a generic weight walk its 3D slices change by the 3D T1 moves of Chapter {{ch:centre1}}, Theorem {{s:o7.1}}′ for d = 5, one contact gained or one lost at a time. At #61's walls the lattice's central symmetry forces a loss and a gain onto one point: six cells meet there and the four exchanging holes lie in one plane with a common midpoint (a degenerate 4-4 flip, the rosette kind of event, not Chapter {{ch:centre1}}, Theorem {{s:o7.1}}'s pure swap); in that plane the exchange is a 2D scutoid swap. At s = 1/5, summed over the six words' unit cells, the faces exchanged are: at −(4 − s)/24, 57 between octahedral holes in one layer for 57 between tetrahedral holes in one layer; at −5s/8, 21 between octahedral holes two layers apart for 21 tetrahedral; at −s/8, 126 for 126 between neighbouring layers; at −9s/8, 31 long octahedral contacts lost and 173 gained. The Swap Rule switches on at the moment the last octahedral contact across a whole layer lets go.

One dimension up: the 4D cells of the holes

Each hole's power cell, stacked over λ in an interval [λa, λb], is a convex 4-polytope {(x, λ) : n·x ≤ b₀ + b₁λ, λa ≤ λ ≤ λb} whose 3D slices are the cells. Such a 4-cell, whose 3D slices change neighbours at a wall where cells stop touching and others start, is a 4D scutoid (#104). At s = 1/5, across the wall λ = −1/8, 25 holes (of the six words) have 4D cells with faces that end or begin exactly at the wall: the octahedral cells' faces towards octahedral holes two layers away end there, the tetrahedral cells' new faces begin there (Figure {{f:o9.2}}).

Walls in a cone of heights

Why are the walls of Figure {{f:o9.1}} straight lines? Because the weight walk is a walk in a cone of heights, the same kind of space as the permutohedron's family of Chapter {{ch:family}}. A power diagram of sites pi with weights wi is the projection of the lower hull of the lifted points (pi, |pi|² − wi); its dual is the regular subdivision of the sites with those heights, and the heights giving one subdivision form a cone of the secondary fan (Gelfand, Kapranov and Zelevinsky; for periodic sites, Voronoi's L-type domains and their generalisation by Dutour Sikirić, Schürmann and Vallentin).

Proposition {{S:cones}} (a reading, proved; DICTO's draft The Permutohedron Family, Proposition 5). In the setting of the Swap Rule the lifted heights of the fixed site set are affine in (s, λ). Hence the chamber map of the Swap Rule is a planar affine section of the secondary fan of that periodic site set, and each of its walls is a straight line in the (s, λ) plane.

Proof. Write each site as p = (a, b, k), k its height in layer units; in true coordinates it is (a, b, dk). The power distance is Qs(x − p) − w(p) with the positive definite form Qs(v) = va² + vb² + (2s/3)vk². Expanding, Qs(x − p) − w(p) = Qs(x) − 2Bs(x, p) + (Qs(p) − w(p)), and the first term is common to all sites, so the power diagram is dual to the regular subdivision of the sites with heights h(p) = a² + b² + (2s/3)k² − λ·[p tetrahedral]. This is affine in (s, λ), so the preimage of each secondary cone is convex and each wall is the preimage of a hyperplane: a line. ∎ The walls of Theorem {{s:o9.1}}, λ = −(4 − s)/24, −5s/8, −s/8, −9s/8 and 3s/8, are indeed straight.

The switch theorem. #53 proves that every flip of a two-kind weight walk sits at the one root λ* = S0/SA of an affine lifted determinant, and that at a switch point every dual cell trades the edges of the hull of one kind for those of the other, so a switch is always single; in the language of regular subdivisions each switch point is a wall where two regular subdivisions of one non-simplicial cell meet. Read here, the weight walk of Chapter {{ch:bonds}} is a line in a secondary fan, its flips are wall crossings, and the cells it passes are members of the permutohedron's family; the line of Chapter {{ch:family}} is a line in the permutohedron's own cone of heights. The two are walls in cones of heights, related through Gale duality and McMullen's type cones; we claim only the reading.
