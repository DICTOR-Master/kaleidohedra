Pass a flat sheet up through a solid and watch the slice. In three dimensions the slice is a polygon that gains and loses sides. In four dimensions the slice is a whole solid whose faces are pieces of the cells, a tissue, and as the sheet rises cells are born, swap neighbours and die, like the cells of a growing organ. This is the scutoid tissue of #104, the centre of the book: the solid of all orders of five values, swept, is a tissue of 26 births, 66 swaps and 26 deaths.

A warm-up in three dimensions: Kelvin's cell swept

Take Kelvin's cell, the 24 orders of 1, 2, 3, 4, and a height u · x with u1 < u2 < u3 < u4. Each corner has three edges, and an edge goes down when it swaps a value k standing to the left of k + 1 (the argument of Proposition {{s:o5.1}} below). So the corners with 0, 1, 2, 3 edges below number 1, 11, 11, 1, the Eulerian numbers of four values. A slice is a polygon whose sides are cut from the faces of the solid. At a corner with one edge below the polygon gains a side and two of its sides stop meeting; at a corner with two below it loses a side and two sides begin to meet (Figure {{f:kswept}}). In three dimensions the gain and the loss of a contact come at different corners; one dimension up they come together.

The 4D permutohedron swept: births, swaps and deaths

Proposition {{S:o5.1}}. Sweep the 4D permutohedron, whose corners are the 120 orderings of 1, …, 5, by the height f(x) = u · x with u1 < u2 < … < u5. The edge from an ordering to the one with the values k and k + 1 exchanged goes down exactly when k stands to the left of k + 1. So a corner has as many edges below as there are such k, and the corners with 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 edges below number 1, 26, 66, 26, 1. The scutoid exchanges of the slice happen at the 66 orderings in which exactly two values k stand to the left of k + 1.

Proof. Exchanging k (at position p) and k + 1 (at position q) changes f by up − uq, negative exactly when p < q. A corner with two edges below is where the slice makes an exchange of neighbours (#104, Theorem 1); with one, a cell is born; with three, one dies. The counts are the Eulerian numbers, checked by listing all 120 orderings for three directions u (swap_orderings.py). ∎

A 4-polytope is simple if each corner lies in exactly four cells (equivalently, four edges). Take a generic height, a linear function with different values at all corners. Slicing at height t gives a 3-polytope whose faces are the cross-sections of the cells that cross the hyperplane; two cells are neighbours in the slice when their shared 2-face crosses it. At a simple corner the faces through it correspond to the subsets of its four edges, and a face has the corner as its top iff all its edges there go down.

Theorem {{S:o7.1}} (#104, Theorem 1). In a generic sweep of a simple 4-polytope the tiling of the slice by the cells changes only when the hyperplane passes a corner, and then in exactly one of three ways, according to the number i of the corner's edges that go down: i = 1, one cell is born (a small triangle appears); i = 2, a scutoid swap: one pair of cells stops touching and one pair starts, nothing else; i = 3, one cell dies. The numbers of births, swaps and deaths are h₁, h₂ and h₃, the middle entries of the polytope's h-vector, the same for every generic direction.

Proof. Between corner heights the slice keeps its combinatorics. At a corner v let e₁, …, e₄ be its edges and Fj the cell not containing ej. Just below v the slice cuts the downward edges, just above the upward ones. i = 1 (e₁ down): above v the slice cuts e₂, e₃, e₄, and the cell containing all three, F₁, appears as a small triangle (v is its lowest corner). i = 2 (e₁, e₂ down): below v the slice's local edge joins the crossings of e₁ and e₂ and lies in F₃ ∩ F₄, the 2-face spanned by e₁ and e₂; v is its top, so above v the two cells no longer share a face crossing the hyperplane (two cells of a polytope share at most one 2-face); symmetrically F₁ ∩ F₂, spanned by e₃, e₄, has v as its bottom and F₁, F₂ begin to touch. No other face through v has v as top or bottom, so nothing else changes. i = 3 is i = 1 upside down. The number of corners with i downward edges is hi, independent of the generic height (the Morse-type count of the h-vector, with hi = h4−i; McMullen; Ziegler, Lectures on Polytopes, Lecture 8; Buchstaber–Panov, Toric Topology, ch. 1). ∎ In the dual triangulation of the slice's boundary (one vertex per cell) the three events are the bistellar flips 1-3, 2-2 and 3-1 (Pachner; Lickorish); the theorem is classical in that language.

Classical and new. Classical: that the number of corners of index k is hk for every generic linear function (McMullen 1970; the line shellings of Bruggesser and Mani 1971; Ziegler, Lecture 8), the Dehn–Sommerville relations, and the bistellar moves. New here: the reading of the three indices as the birth, scutoid swap and death of the cells of a tissue, with h₂ counting the swaps; the counts of Theorem {{s:o7.2}}; the balance law, Theorem {{s:o7.4}}; and the walls of Chapter {{ch:o9}}.

What is new. In the sources checked (listed in #104: MathWorld and Wolfram on the scutoid, the Buchstaber invariant of simple polytopes, The Best Ways to Slice a Polytope, Izmestiev, Klee and Novik on simplicial moves of balanced complexes, and web searches of 10 and 11 November 2026 for scutoids in four and higher dimensions and for sweeps of simple polytopes by bistellar flips), no earlier work reads the sweep of a polytope as a scutoid tissue or links scutoids to the h-vector. The counting of corners by the h-vector is classical (McMullen; Bruggesser and Mani); the reading as births, swaps and deaths is #104's.

The worked example: the 4D permutohedron

For the 4D permutohedron Theorem {{s:o7.1}}'s three moves fall on the orderings (Proposition {{s:o5.1}}): a corner with one edge below, where one value k stands to the left of k + 1, gives a birth (26 of them); two, an exchange of neighbours (66); three, a death (26); with the first and last corner, the Eulerian numbers 1, 26, 66, 26, 1 are its h-vector. Its 30 cells are of two types, ten permutohedra and twenty hexagonal prisms, and every corner sees one cell of each of the four node types of the chain, two permutohedra and two hexagonal prisms (#108, Section 4: the omnitruncated 5-cell), so the balance law below (Theorem {{s:o7.4}}: each kind of swap is matched by its reverse) applies to it as well: its three kinds of exchange are each matched by their reverses, 6, 16 and 11 each way.

Worked example: the 66 swaps. A corner is an order of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. Read it left to right and count the values k that stand somewhere to the left of k + 1. In 1 2 3 4 5 all four do: the top corner. In 5 4 3 2 1 none do: the bottom corner. In 2 1 4 3 5 exactly two do, 2 before 3 and 4 before 5 (1 stands after 2, and 3 after 4), so it has two edges below and is a swap. The orders with exactly two such k number 66, with one 26, with three 26: the Eulerian numbers 1, 26, 66, 26, 1.

The swap that reverses to itself

Run the sweep backwards, by the height −u. Every edge that went down now goes up, so a corner with i edges below becomes one with 4 − i below: every birth becomes a death and every death a birth. A swap stays a swap: the pair of cells that let go now meets, and the pair that met now lets go. Among the moves of a four-dimensional tissue the scutoid swap is the one event that is its own reverse, and Theorem {{s:o7.1}} counts it by h2, the middle of the h-vector, in every direction. This is a law at the fixed point of a swap, the reversal of the sweep, and it holds for a stated reason: the reversal fixes only the middle index (studies/2026-11-13-swaps-and-fixed-points, row 4, checked exactly for d = 2 to 7).

The self-reverse class exists only in even dimension, where the middle index i = d/2 is a whole number; and only in dimension four is it the pure swap, one contact lost and one made at a single corner. In three dimensions the contact lost and the contact gained come at different corners (the warm-up above); in five they come at the two indices 2 and 3, which are each other's reverse; in six and above the middle corners touch only lower faces (Theorem {{s:o7.1}}′ in Chapter {{ch:centre2}}). The scutoid swap of a tissue is a four-dimensional event.

The 30 cells of the 4D permutohedron, sliced, are a tissue: 26 births, 66 swaps, 26 deaths, the swaps the one move that is its own reverse. Is this special to the permutohedron?
