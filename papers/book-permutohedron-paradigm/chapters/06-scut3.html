In 2018 Gómez-Gálvez and colleagues found that cells lining curved living tissue take a shape nobody had named, the scutoid: five neighbours at the top, six at the bottom, and in between, two neighbours swap contact. It is the swap of this book seen inside a tissue: not two values changing places in an order, but two cells trading one neighbour for another as a slice moves through them.

Chapter {{ch:o4}} found where the permutohedron stops. This chapter returns to its edges, the exchanges of neighbours, and follows them into the cells of living tissue. A scutoid is a cell whose outline changes on the way through: two cells that touched stop touching, and two others start (Gómez-Gálvez et al. 2018). In this book's language it is an exchange of neighbours, the move that joins two orderings on an edge of the permutohedron, seen in a slice.

One swap of neighbouring cells

Take four cells round a short wall in a flat tissue: A and B share the wall, C sits above it and D below. Let A and B draw apart and C and D close in. The wall shrinks to a point where all four cells meet, and then reopens across: now C and D share a wall and A and B no longer touch (Figure {{f:oneswap}}). Foam physicists call it a T1 transition (Weaire and Rivier); biologists see it when tissue reshapes. Stack the frames in height and the cells become columns, and the two that met halfway up have gained a side: that column is a scutoid. One contact lost, one contact made, at one point: this is the swap that the rest of the book follows into four dimensions.

Worked example: a scutoid. Follow one cell from bottom to top: six neighbours below, five above. Halfway up, two of its neighbours that were apart meet across it, and one wall of the cell ends there in a point: the Y-corner. Every scutoid of this chapter makes that one trade; the flat-faced scutoid of the next section has 13 faces, every one of them flat.

A flat-faced scutoid

Take the hexagonal tiling, shrink every second vertical edge of every second row to a point and reopen it across: a cell that is a hexagon below and a pentagon above, with the Y-corner halfway and 13 faces, every one of them flat (Figure {{f:o5.1}}).

Theorem {{S:o5.2}} (the scutoid shell). Shrink every hexagon–hexagon edge of a truncated icosahedron to a point (the surface becomes an icosidodecahedron) and reopen each point across, between the two pentagons (it becomes a truncated dodecahedron). The 32 cells between the three nested surfaces, 12 growing pentagon → pentagon → decagon and 20 shrinking hexagon → triangle → triangle, have every face flat for any three radii that nest, and fill the shell exactly once.

Why flat. Each core edge and the middle edge above it are perpendicular to the same mirror plane, so they are parallel and the side face between them is a flat trapezoid; a shrinking edge gives a triangle. Checked: every face flat to 10⁻¹³, the 32 volumes add to V(outside) − V(core).

Theorem {{S:o5.3}} (the polar flip). The polar of that sequence is pentakis dodecahedron → rhombic triacontahedron → triakis icosahedron: each pair of triangles across a 6–6 edge flattens into a rhombus and folds back along the other diagonal. For any sizes with the pentakis dodecahedron inside the triakis icosahedron, the shell between them splits into 30 congruent cells, one per rhombus, every face flat. On triangles the scutoid move cannot run; in the polar it becomes a diagonal flip.

Proof. The polar of a truncated icosahedron is a pentakis dodecahedron, of an icosidodecahedron a rhombic triacontahedron, of a truncated dodecahedron a triakis icosahedron; their corners lie on the 12 five-fold and 20 three-fold axes. A rhombus of the triacontahedron has corners on two five-fold axes a, c and two three-fold axes b, d; over it the pentakis dodecahedron has the triangles abd and bcd (folded along the three-fold diagonal bd, the polar of a 6–6 edge) and the triakis icosahedron the triangles abc and acd (folded along ac): a diagonal flip. The plane through the centre and two neighbouring axes of a rhombus, a and b, is a mirror of the icosahedral group. The cones from the centre over the 30 rhombi fill space; the cell over a rhombus is the part of its cone outside the pentakis dodecahedron and inside the triakis icosahedron. Its four side faces lie in the four mirror planes of the cone, each a quadrilateral with two corners of each solid on the axes a and b, so every side face is flat; its bottom and top are two triangles each. The cells fill the shell exactly once, because the cones do, and they are congruent, because the symmetry group takes any rhombus to any other and fixes both solids. ∎ Exact certificate (scutoid_exact.py, in ℚ(√5)): the three polars computed exactly, the folds as stated, every side plane a mirror (it maps the 32 axes to themselves), the 120 side faces coplanar with the centre, and at five-fold radii 1 and 1.55 the 30 cell volumes equal and adding to V(triakis icosahedron) − V(pentakis dodecahedron) exactly (6.4660211935 at these radii).

Theorem {{S:o5.4}} (the cycle). The scutoid move and the flip each undo themselves, and with the two polar swaps they close a square: truncated icosahedron → truncated dodecahedron → triakis icosahedron → pentakis dodecahedron → truncated icosahedron. As nested shells this fills all space except the centre, self-similarly: with four scutoid shapes (for example at scale 71/45 = 1.578 per period), or through the polar with two flip shapes (for example at scale 155/86 = 1.802).

Proof. The scutoid move shrinks each hexagon–hexagon edge of the truncated icosahedron to a point and reopens it across, between the two pentagons; on the truncated dodecahedron the same move shrinks each decagon–decagon edge and reopens it between the two triangles, which gives back the truncated icosahedron: the move undoes itself, and so does the flip of Theorem {{s:o5.3}}. The polar of the truncated dodecahedron is the triakis icosahedron, the flip takes it to the pentakis dodecahedron, and the polar of that is the truncated icosahedron again: the square closes. For the onions, take nested solids K0 ⊂ K1 ⊂ λK0 with the centre inside K0: the shells λk(K1 ∖ K0) and λk(λK0 ∖ K1), k = …, −1, 0, 1, …, fill all of space but the centre, each point once. Through the polar, K0 is the pentakis dodecahedron and K1 the triakis icosahedron, and Theorem {{s:o5.3}} cuts each shell into 30 congruent cells, one shape for the forward flip and one for the flip back. For the scutoid onion the shells are the scutoid shell S of Theorem {{s:o5.2}} (truncated icosahedron → icosidodecahedron → truncated dodecahedron) and the reverse shell A (truncated dodecahedron → icosidodecahedron → truncated icosahedron × λ), which share the truncated dodecahedron corner for corner; each has two cell shapes, one per kind of face, since the symmetry group acts on each kind transitively. ∎ Exact certificate (scutoid_exact.py, in ℚ(√5)) at circumradii 1, 1.18, 1.42 for S and 1.42, 1.499, 71/45 for A: every half-cell (core face to middle face, middle face to outer face) is the convex hull of its two end faces, and its faces are exactly the faces of the construction, so every face is flat; each shrunk edge reopens across; in each shell the 64 half-cells have pairwise disjoint interiors (2016 pairs, each separated by an exact plane), lie between the core and the outer solid, and their volumes add exactly to the shell; the nesting K0 ⊂ K1 ⊂ λK0 holds strictly for both onions (λ = 71/45 and 155/86). The filling of the scutoid shells is certified at these radii; the flatness of Theorem {{s:o5.2}} holds at any radii by its mirror argument.

Layers that change neighbours

A block of eight. Eight convex scutoids, every one exchanging pentagon and hexagon between its ends, fill one flat layer, and the layers stack straight up; the pieces are chunky (sphericity 0.825–0.835, about that of a cube). Turned on its side the same group builds a wall whose face is a mosaic of pentagon and hexagon ends, sloped back like an ishigaki castle wall (Figures {{f:o5.5}}, {{f:o5.6}}).

Proposition {{S:o5.5}} (truncated icosahedra in a crystal). Truncated icosahedra of edge 1 in layers, every link a pentagon facing a hexagon, stacked as closely as the links allow: space cut into one cell per ball makes every cell exchange neighbours twice, below and above its layer's middle plane. The layer spacing is an algebraic number of degree 8, h = 3.43700237509909…, and the four exchange corners form one orbit at ±0.34113 from the middle plane (computed to 400 digits).

Back to the book. In the 4D permutohedron the exchanges of neighbours come one at a time, 66 of them, each at an ordering (Proposition {{s:o5.1}}); round the truncated icosahedron they come all at once, 30 in one shell, held flat by its mirrors (Theorem {{s:o5.2}}). Chapters {{ch:centre1}} and {{ch:centre2}} count them in four dimensions for every simple polytope.

A scutoid is a cell through which two neighbours swap contact. Layers of scutoids are slices of a solid one dimension up. What does the permutohedron of five values look like, and what does it do when a slice moves through it?
