Keep every wall of Kelvin's cell facing the same way but slide it in or out: edges may shrink away but never turn, so every edge stays parallel to a swap. Everything that can be made this way is one family, and every solid of the climb is in it: the hexagon, Kelvin's cell, the slider, the bonds, the DICTO 1:2 and 2:1 Truncated Octahedra (its stops x = 1/2 and x = 2), Fedorov's five space-fillers, and, deep inside, the associahedron, whose canonical form hands out a law of particle physics.

Chapters {{ch:three}} to {{ch:golden}} kept the walls of the permutohedron where they are and moved its values. This chapter moves the walls. Keep every wall facing the same way and slide it in or out: some edges shrink and vanish, but no edge ever turns. Everything that can be made this way is the permutohedron's family, the generalized permutohedra of Postnikov. The family is large: the simplex, the octahedron, the parallelepipeds, the matroid polytopes and the associahedron, the solid whose corners are the ways to bracket a product, all belong to it. At its top sits Kelvin's cell, which fills space; deep inside sits the associahedron, which in the work of Arkani-Hamed, Bai, He and Yan hands out a law of particle physics. This chapter walks the road between them.

The family and its cone

For values x1 < … < xn the permutohedron Π(x) is the convex hull of the n! orderings of (x1, …, xn) in ℝn; it has one facet for each nonempty proper subset S of [n] = {1, …, n}, and its edges are parallel to the roots ei − ej. A generalized permutohedron is a polytope obtained from it by moving facets parallel to themselves, edges allowed to shrink to points but never to turn (Postnikov). Equivalently (Postnikov, Reiner and Williams), every member is

P(z) = {y : Σi∈[n] yi = z[n], Σi∈S yi ≥ zS for every S},

with heights z in a polyhedral cone, the deformation cone, whose points are the submodular functions (the link to polymatroids goes back to Edmonds). Modulo translations the cone has dimension 2n − n − 1, eleven for the truncated octahedron. Writing z through the simplices ΔI = conv{ei : i ∈ I} gives every member as a signed Minkowski sum Σ yIΔI: the permutohedron is the sum of the segments Δ{i,j}, Loday's associahedron the sum over the intervals Δ[i,j]. Replacing the braid fan by the mirrors of another reflection group gives the Coxeter permutohedra and their cones (Ardila, Castillo, Eur and Postnikov): in three dimensions the B3 permutohedron is the truncated cuboctahedron and the H3 one the truncated icosidodecahedron.

Two ends of the family carry laws. At the top the permutohedron is the Voronoi cell of the lattice An−1* (Conway and Sloane), so it fills space and counts the points of its lattice. Inside, the kinematic associahedron of Arkani-Hamed, Bai, He and Yan has as canonical form (the rational function with simple poles on its facet planes, Arkani-Hamed, Bai and Lam) the tree amplitude mn of the bi-adjoint scalar theory. The amplituhedron is in general not a polytope and is not a member; it is named so that the family is not over-read.

The line to the associahedron

In ℝn let Π have heights zΠ(S) = C(|S| + 1, 2) (the values 1, …, n) and let L be Loday's associahedron, zL(S) = the number of intervals inside S. The Postnikov line is P(t) = (1 − t)Π + tL, t ∈ [0, 1]: the interval facets stay put and every other facet moves outward at its own speed DS = zΠ(S) − zL(S). The equal-speed line moves every non-interval facet at the same speed s.

Theorem {{S:o14.1}} (the road from the crystal to the law). Let n ≥ 3.
(i) Proved, every n. For 0 ≤ t < 1, P(t), the Postnikov line from the permutohedron Π to Loday's associahedron L, has the permutohedron's labelled face lattice; at t = 1 all non-interval facets, 2n − 2 − (C(n + 1, 2) − 1) of them, vanish together (5 in 3D, 16 in 4D). The same holds on the line to the ABHY associahedron (the kinematic associahedron of Arkani-Hamed, Bai, He and Yan).
(ii) Proved, every n. P(t) fills space by translations only at t = 0; for 0 < t ≤ 1 it is not even centrally symmetric.
(iii) Proved, every n. On the equal-speed line DS = Σb<b′ kbkb′, where kb are the lengths of the blocks (maximal intervals) of S, and facet S is present exactly for s < DS. Exact, n = 6: unlike the straight line, the equal-speed line leaves the family for 1 < s < 5, where 16 edges turn off the root directions; it comes back before the end.
(iv) The canonical form at the centre Y of Π is 2n−1n/(n − 1)! (proved, every n) and at the end equals the amplitude, Ω(L; Y) = mn+2 (proved, every n, given the theorem of Arkani-Hamed, Bai, He and Yan). Along the line it is one rational function of t (exact, n = 4, 5), falling from 16/3 to 88/27 = m6 in 3D and from 10/3 to 49/36 = m7 in 4D.

Proof of (i). The normal fan of a Minkowski sum is the common refinement of the summands' fans, and the braid fan refines the fan of every member (Postnikov, Reiner and Williams), in particular L's; so for 0 < t < 1 the fan of P(t) is the braid fan. At t = 1 it is L's, whose facets are the interval facets. The ABHY associahedron has the same fan as L. ∎

Proof of (ii). P(z) is centrally symmetric about c exactly when zS + z[n] − z[n]∖S = 2Σi∈S ci for every S, a system linear in (z, c). Since z(t) is affine in t, the t at which it can be solved form an affine subset of [0, 1]; it contains 0 (Π is symmetric) and not 1 (L has pentagonal 2-faces, or is a pentagon for n = 3). So it is {0}, and a polytope that fills space by translations is centrally symmetric (Venkov; McMullen). ∎

Proof of (iii). zΠ(S) = C(K + 1, 2) with K = Σ kb, and zL(S) = Σ C(kb + 1, 2), since every interval inside S lies in one block; the difference is Σb<b′ kbkb′. At s = DS the inequality of S is the sum of the block inequalities, interval facets that never move, so it is tight only on a face of lower dimension and S is gone; for s > DS its plane misses the solid. For s < DS: let ε(s) be the largest ε for which a point of the plane of S satisfies every other inequality with slack ε; the conditions are linear in (s, x, ε), so ε is concave in s, and ε(0) > 0. It is enough to give a point of the solid at s = DS on the plane of S. Give the block bp the values 1, …, kp, the complement G of S the values K + 1, …, n, and add Ep = kp(kp+1 + … + km) to the first element of the gap after bp. For any U, bounding the values block by block gives ΣU x − C(|U| + 1, 2) ≥ −DS, and for an interval U the extras in the gaps it contains cover the cross terms, so every interval inequality holds as well. ∎ The point was checked for every non-interval S, n = 3 to 13 (15 914 facets), and the whole proof read line by line by an independent reader.

Proof of (iv). Π is simple and the n − 1 facets at a corner are those of a chain T1 ⊂ … ⊂ Tn−1, with a unimodular basis of normals; so Ω(Π; Y) is the sum over the n! chains of ∏ 1/XTk(Y). At the centre XT = |T|(n − |T|)/2 depends only on |T|, every chain gives 2n−1/((n − 1)!)², and the sum is 2n−1n/(n − 1)!. For L, in X[a,b] + X[a+1,b+1] − X[a,b+1] − X[a+1,b] the variables cancel and the constants give 1: Loday's heights are the ABHY slice with every constant 1, and Arkani-Hamed, Bai, He and Yan give Ω(L) = mn+2. ∎ The table is computed in rational arithmetic by two formulas (a pulling triangulation and Warren's vertex formula).

Proposition {{S:o14.2}} (the test case; the theorem is Arkani-Hamed, Bai, He and Yan's, the check is ours; exact). For n = 4 to 10 the kinematic associahedron cut out by the ABHY slice with random positive rational constants has Catalan(n − 2) corners, all simple and unimodular, and its canonical form equals mn exactly at three random rational points (identically for n ≤ 6). A tilted slice still cuts a simple polytope with Catalan(n − 2) corners, but some corners get determinant 2 and the form is no longer mn (n = 6, 7). The law belongs to a particular member of the associahedron's type cone, not to its combinatorics alone.

Read as a road: on the straight line the solid keeps the permutohedron's shape until the last moment, when all its extra faces go at once; it stops filling space at its first step; and its form slides down to the amplitude. On the equal-speed line the extra faces leave in stages, each exactly when its own gap DS has closed. The combinatorial passage from the permutohedron to the associahedron by deleting facets is known (removahedra and permutreehedra: Hohlweg and Lange; Pilaud and Pons), and so is the permutohedron's canonical form as a sum over orders (Arkani-Hamed, Bai and Lam); no source checked follows this line in the deformation cone and records its walls, its loss of tiling or its form. The app world Generalized Permutohedra takes a staged route through the same cone, as DICTO designed it: its slider removes the non-interval faces one at a time.

The equal-edged members of three cones

Inside the families of the truncated octahedron, the truncated cuboctahedron and the truncated icosidodecahedron (the A3, B3 and H3 permutohedra), which members have all edges of one length? #49 proved that sliding an equal-edged solid by one edge length along an edge direction keeps it equal-edged, and left open which stretches of the Archimedean solids exist; every stretch along a root direction lies in these cones.

Theorem {{S:o14.3}} (exact). (i) On each cone every edge length is a linear form in the cone parameters, carried on the walls of the Coxeter fan, so "all edges of length 1" is the condition ℓw(ℓw − 1) = 0 on every wall w: the equal-edged members are the 0/1 wall labellings that close round every face. (ii) Up to symmetry they are 17 solids in A3, 101 in B3 and 4427 in H3. (iii) Within these cones the only Platonic or Archimedean solids that can be stretched along a cone direction are the tetrahedron, octahedron, cuboctahedron, truncated tetrahedron and truncated octahedron, each in B3 along the cube axes. (iv) For equal-edged members P, P′ of one cone, P ⊕ P′ is equal-edged exactly when no wall carries an edge of both.

Proof of (i) and (iv). The face of a member in a direction inside a wall w is an edge parallel to w's root, or a point; its length ℓw is linear in the support function, and faces of Minkowski sums are sums of faces, so ℓw(P ⊕ P′) = ℓw(P) + ℓw(P′). (iii) is read off the complete list: every other Platonic or Archimedean solid of these groups has an edge along every root direction of its cone, so any stretch doubles an edge. ∎

The atoms and the 13-COA. The two atoms of A3 (and of B3) are known solids. One is the augmented octahedron (a 60° rhombohedron with one corner cut; P3,33 in Timofeenko's list). The other is the augmented cuboctahedron (Stewart 1970), which DICTO named the 13-Corner Octet Atom (DICTO 13-COA) for its role as an atom of the permutohedron family: a cuboctahedron with a square pyramid on one square, whose four pyramid triangles lie in the planes of the neighbouring triangles and merge with them into 60° rhombi (13 corners, 24 edges; 4 triangles, 5 squares, 4 rhombi; Figure {{f:o14.2}}). It is P3,35 in the list of 78 convex polyhedra with regular and composite faces of Gurin and Zalgaller, proved complete by Timofeenko, and it is in the list of Doskey, Kaufman and Waterman (2006), which credits it to Stewart. What the sources checked do not discuss is its role here: the 13-COA is indecomposable, an equal-edged member of the A3 cone with no equal-edged summand. It sits on the octet lattice and is not centrally symmetric (6 of its 13 faces come in parallel pairs).

Its Vacuus. In the sense of #87 (one fin from the centre to every edge, one strut to every corner) the 13-COA has a Vacuus: its corner centroid lies strictly inside all 13 face planes. Its 24 fins lie in 22 planes; only two pairs of fins are coplanar and merge into flat plates (Figure {{f:o14.2}}, right). This was checked in floating point (vacuus_a316.py), not exactly.

In H3 the 367 atoms have regular triangles and pentagons together with rhombi of 36°/144°, 60°/120° and 72°/108° and hexagons of 72°/144° and 108°/144°. Of them 13 (regular faces and 60° rhombi only) are in Timofeenko's complete list of 78, 14 (regular faces and 72°/108° rhombi) match entries of Kaufman's list of 108° rhombic polyhedra by their face counts, and one, H3-16, is Subbotin's five-rhombic pyramid (2022). The other 339 were not found in any source checked (PRIOR-ART.md lists them); as far as those sources show they are new, and so is the role of all 369 as atoms of these cones.

Which members fill space

Proposition {{S:o14.4}} (proved). (i) Every full-dimensional graphical zonotope Σ cijΔ{i,j} (cij > 0, ij the edges of a graph G) fills its hyperplane by translations, whatever the lengths cij. (ii) For n = 4 a full-dimensional member fills space by translations exactly when it is the graphical zonotope of a connected graph on four vertices, and the five such graphs give Fedorov's five parallelohedra: a tree the parallelepiped, the 4-cycle the rhombic dodecahedron, a triangle with a pendant edge the hexagonal prism, K4 minus an edge the elongated dodecahedron, and K4 the truncated octahedron.

Proof. (i) A zonotope is centrally symmetric with centrally symmetric faces; its belts correspond to the rank-2 contractions of its generator directions, and for graph directions these are graphic of rank 2, with at most three parallel classes (a triangle). So every belt has 4 or 6 facets, and Venkov and McMullen give a translation tiling; only directions enter, so the lengths are free. (ii) A 3-polytope that tiles by translations has centrally symmetric faces, so it is a zonohedron (Coxeter); a zonotope member is a graphical zonotope (Postnikov, Reiner and Williams), and full dimension needs G connected. The five connected graphs on four vertices have 3, 4, 4, 5 and 6 edges, and their zonotopes have the face counts of Fedorov's five. ∎ The graph-by-graph match with Fedorov's five was not found stated in the sources checked; it is claimed here only as this short proof from classical facts.

By Theorem {{s:o14.1}} (ii) every member on the open line to the associahedron fails, and so does the 1:2 Truncated Octahedron of Chapters {{ch:o1}} and {{ch:o6}} on its own (its hexagons are truncated triangles, not centrally symmetric), although it fills space together with its partner, the 2:1. Chapter {{ch:o4}}'s chamfered cube is not a member at all: it has seven edge directions, and a member of this family at most the six root directions.

Repeated values: the tesseract's sections (#89). #89 slices the tesseract with corners (±1, ±1, ±1, ±1) by the hyperplanes x1 + x2 + x3 + x4 = 2h across a long diagonal, and finds for 0 < h < 1 the tetrahedra cut at x = (1 − h)/(2 − h): the Greater Golden Cut Tetrahedron at h = 1/φ², the Archimedean truncated tetrahedron at h = 1/2, the Lesser Golden Cut Tetrahedron at h = 1/φ (#89, Main Theorem (a); home Book Nine, The Golden Ladder, Chapter 6). Read through this book, each of these sections is a permutohedron of four values with one value repeated: its corners are exactly the orders of (−1, 2h − 1, 1, 1), that is, after x ↦ (x + 1)/2, of the values 0, h, 1, 1, with gaps h and 1 − h, so its hexagons have sides in the ratio of the gaps (Chapter {{ch:three}}). At h = 1/2 these are 0, 1/2, 1, 1, the classical truncated tetrahedron as the permutohedron of 0, 1, 2, 2; at the golden heights the gaps are in the ratio φ. The section through the centre (h = 0) is the octahedron, the values −1, −1, 1, 1, and beyond the corner layer (1 < h < 2) the sections are tetrahedra, the values 2h − 3, 1, 1, 1. We checked this reading against #89's own construction and data, at its three stored heights and at four controls, in exact arithmetic (check89.py, FINAL ok). All of these are members of the family with repeated values.

Sliding a solid: Minkowski sums

#49 (the Polyhedral Stretching Theorem) proved that a dodecahedron slid one edge length in a straight line sweeps a solid whose edges are all equal, in every direction except along an edge, and that the same holds for every solid whose edges are all equal. The swept solid is the Minkowski sum of the solid and a segment, and the family of this chapter is closed under adding segments along swap directions: slide a member along a root and it stays a member, its new edges parallel to the slide. That is why every stretch of Theorem {{s:o14.3}} (iii) lies in the cones, and why the stretches of the Archimedean solids that #49 left open could be settled there: within these cones only five solids can be stretched along a cone direction. #49's own five-fold stretch of the dodecahedron is not in the H3 cone (it fails the cone's inequalities), as Theorem {{s:o14.3}} records.

One move each (#50). #50 obtains five classical space-fillers that the literature makes by cutting or gluing, among them the regular-hexagon and the classical elongated dodecahedron, the Bain rhombic dodecahedron, the Bilinski dodecahedron and the 60° rhombohedron, each by one setting of three lattice moves (Fedorov's moves). Each tiles space by translations, so each is, up to its proportions, one of Fedorov's five of Proposition {{s:o14.4}}. The table that matches the two lists name by name is not made here; it is an open item of the series (home of #50: Book Two, Space-Fillers and Lattice Moves, Chapter 3).

Back to the book

The climb began with the permutohedron's values and reaches its summit with its walls. Moving the values gave the solids of the first chapters (Chapters {{ch:o1}} to {{ch:o3}}); moving the walls gives the whole family, with Kelvin's cell at the top and the amplitude at the associahedron, and in three dimensions the members that fill space by sliding are exactly the five classical space-fillers, one for each connected network of four dots.

Pointer. The canonical form of the associahedron and the physics it carries are told in Book Eleven, A Brief Geometry of Space, Chapter 13; this chapter keeps Proposition {{s:o14.2}} as the far end of the family, a check of the theorem of Arkani-Hamed, Bai, He and Yan.

All the solids of the climb are one cone, from Kelvin's cell, which fills space, to the associahedron, which hands out a law, and every edge in it is a swap. The oldest rule of order is the ringers': only neighbours may swap.
