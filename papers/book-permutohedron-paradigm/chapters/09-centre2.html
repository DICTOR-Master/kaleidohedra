The permutohedron is not special. Every solid in four dimensions with four cells at each corner, swept by a moving slice, is a tissue of births, swaps and deaths, and the numbers are the solid's h-vector, the same in every direction. Between the two Queens, the 600-cell and the 120-cell, the swaps balance exactly: as many trade a contact of one kind for the other as the other way round, 601 each way.

Every simple 4-polytope

Theorem {{s:o7.1}} of Chapter {{ch:centre1}} was stated for every simple 4-polytope, and its proof used nothing of the permutohedron but its four cells at a corner. This chapter carries it through every dimension and along the road between the Queens.

Remark (the g-theorem in tissue terms). For a simple 4-polytope the g-theorem (McMullen's conditions, proved by Stanley 1980 and Billera and Lee 1981) gives g₂ = h₂ − h₁ ≥ 0 and g₂ ≤ C(h₁, 2). So every generic sweep of a simple 4-polytope has at least as many swaps as births, and at most births + C(births, 2): for the three polytopes of Theorem {{s:o7.2}}, 366 ≥ 116, 966 ≥ 716 and 2166 ≥ 716.

Theorem {{s:o7.1}}′ (#104; every dimension d ≥ 4). In a generic sweep of a simple d-polytope, d ≥ 4, two facets touch in the slice exactly when their common ridge crosses the hyperplane. Passing a corner with i downward edges is the bistellar i → (d − i) move on the dual triangulation of the slice's boundary, and it changes the facets' contacts only for i = 1 (a facet is born), i = 2 (one contact is gained), i = d − 2 (one contact is lost) and i = d − 1 (a facet dies). For d = 4, i = 2 = d − 2 and the gain and the loss happen together (the swap of Theorem {{s:o7.1}}); for d = 5 they are separate moves at i = 2 and i = 3; for d ≥ 6 the corners with 3 ≤ i ≤ d − 3 change no facet contact (they move lower faces only). The number of corners of index i is hi.

Proof. A ridge Fa ∩ Fb through v is spanned by the d − 2 edges other than ea, eb; it has v as its top iff they all go down (so i = d − 2 and {a, b} are the up edges), as its bottom iff they all go up (i = 2); otherwise it crosses on both sides. ∎ Confirmation (floating point, predictions first, three random directions each): the 5-cube, h = (1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1): 5 births, 10 contacts gained, 10 lost, 5 deaths; the 5D permutohedron (the 720 orderings of 1, …, 6), h = the Eulerian numbers (1, 57, 302, 302, 57, 1): 57, 302, 302, 57; the 4D permutohedron, (1, 26, 66, 26, 1): 26 births, 66 swaps, 26 deaths. No regularity is needed: three random simple 4-polytopes (polars of hulls of 24, 36, 54 random points near the 3-sphere, no symmetry) gave exactly the predicted (20, 57, 20), (32, 103, 32), (50, 142, 50). Remark. At a corner of a non-simple polytope the moves clump; a generic small shift of the facet hyperplanes makes any polytope simple and splits each clump into births, swaps and deaths.

Remark (the indices). In a simple d-polytope: index 1, a facet is born; index 2, a contact is gained; index d − 2, a contact is lost; index d − 1, a facet dies. Only for d = 4 do the gain and the loss share an index, and make the swap; for d = 5 they separate (2 and 3); for d ≥ 6 the indices 3, …, d − 3 touch only lower faces. For d = 3 two of them coincide the other way: index 1 is both a birth and a contact lost, and index 2 both a contact gained and a death (the slice polygon gains a side where two sides stopped meeting, and loses one where two begin to meet).

Theorem {{S:o7.2}} (#104; the polytopes between the Queens). On the path of uniform truncations from the 600-cell (the White Queen, #63) to the 120-cell (the Red Queen, #65) (the stages between: #76; the uniform 4-polytopes: Conway and Guy 1965; Coxeter, Regular Polytopes), the 120-cell, the truncated 120-cell and the bitruncated 600-cell are simple, with (births, swaps, deaths) = (116, 366, 116), (716, 966, 716) and (716, 2166, 716) in every generic sweep.

Proof. Their vertex figures are tetrahedra; h₁ = f₃ − 4 and h₂ = f₀ − 2 − 2h₁ follow from the Dehn–Sommerville relations; then Theorem {{s:o7.1}}. Confirmation: computer sweeps along generic directions give the predicted counts (the bitruncated 600-cell along nine directions). The four cells at the lowest corner are in the slice from the start and four remain at the top corner, so births + 4 = deaths + 4 = cells (120 = 116 + 4; 720 = 716 + 4). ∎

Lemma {{S:o7.3}} (#104, Lemma 5; the truncated 600-cell has no pure swap). At every corner of the truncated 600-cell, in every direction, at least three contacts change at once.

Proof. Six cells meet at each corner (one icosahedron and five truncated tetrahedra); the vertex figure is a pentagonal pyramid, whose graph is the wheel W₅ with 10 edges. A pure swap needs the down/up split of the vertex figure's vertices to leave exactly one edge inside each side; but the largest cut of W₅ has 7 edges (the hub with two non-adjacent rim vertices), so at least three edges lie inside the two sides, each a contact that is lost or gained. ∎ In five computer sweeps the moves were 1 ↔ 3, 2 ↔ 0 and 1 ↔ 0 clumps.

Interpretation. Biologists know the same contrast in living tissue: a T1 transition involves four cells at a point, while a rosette, five or more cells meeting at one point and resolving together, reshuffles several neighbours at once (Blankenship, Backovic, Sanny, Weitz and Zallen 2006). In that language the truncated 600-cell, with its groups of five truncated tetrahedra round each icosahedron's corner, is a rosette tissue, and the bitruncated 600-cell, one stage on, a T1 tissue.

Theorem {{S:o7.4}} (#104; the balance law). Let a simple 4-polytope have its cells in two kinds, W and R, with 2 W + 2 R cells at every corner. Then in every generic sweep the swaps that exchange a W–W contact for an R–R contact and those that exchange R–R for W–W are equally many, (R–R faces) − (R cells) + 1, and every other swap exchanges one W–R contact for another. For the bitruncated 600-cell (W: its 600 truncated tetrahedra, the 600-cell's cells cut back; R: its 120 truncated icosahedra, in the places of the 120-cell's cells): 601 each way and 964 mixed.

Proof. With 2 W and 2 R at a corner, the pair that lets go and the pair that meets are complementary: W–W ↔ R–R or W–R ↔ W–R. Every R–R contact appears exactly once in a sweep: at the first corner (one), at the birth of an R cell after the start (one each, R cells − 2 of them) or by a W–W → R–R swap; so these swaps number (R–R faces) − (R cells − 2) − 1; by deaths the same count goes the other way. Bitruncated 600-cell: 720 − 118 − 1 = 601; the triangles check it, 1200 = 601 + 598 + 1, and 2166 − 1202 = 964. Confirmed in nine directions. ∎

Interpretation. The Queens are the two ends of a channel and this layer, where their cells meet, is where they communicate, equally in both directions.

The balance law in every chain

Theorem {{s:o7.4}} is one case of a law for every simple 4-polytope whose corners all see the same cell types (#108, Theorem 1): every kind of exchange X → Y is matched by Y → X, in every sweep direction, with the count in(X) = NX − Σ cX + pX. At the middle of each four-node chain the balanced count is the number of independent loops of the chain's regular polytope, E − V + 1 (#108, Theorem 3): 6 for the 5-cell, 17 for the tesseract, 73 for the 24-cell and 601 for the 600-cell, the 601 of Theorem {{s:o7.4}}.

Worked example: 601 each way. The bitruncated 600-cell has 600 truncated tetrahedra (W) and 120 truncated icosahedra (R), two of each at every corner. Its R cells meet across its 720 pentagons, one R–R contact each. Every R–R contact appears once in a sweep: at the first corner (one), at the birth of an R cell (118 of them, all R cells but the two at the first corner), or by a W–W → R–R swap. So these swaps number 720 − 118 − 1 = 601, and by the deaths the same number go the other way.

Every simple 4-polytope sweeps as a scutoid tissue counted by its h-vector, and between the Queens its swaps balance. In a simple polytope the swaps come one at a time. What happens when many crowd into one corner?
