Five values give 120 orders, the corners of a solid in four dimensions. Joined by swaps they make a web with four swaps at every knot; its squares and hexagons are the rules the swaps obey. Cut through its centre, the solid shows a Vacuus of 65 slices, walls through its centre, and at its heart five DICTO 1:2 Truncated Octahedra (truncated octahedra whose hexagons have sides 1 and 2 in turn) slot together. Which faces lie on mirrors is told by one law, read off a chain of numbers.

Chapter {{ch:o5}} met the exchange of neighbours one ordering at a time. Here all 120 orderings of five values are taken together: as the corners of the 4D permutohedron, joined by the exchanges, and cut through the centre.

The web of exchange

The 4D permutohedron is the convex hull of the 120 points (p₁, …, p₅) whose coordinates are 1, …, 5 in some order; it lies in the 4-space p₁ + … + p₅ = 15, centred at (3, 3, 3, 3, 3). It is simple (four edges at each corner) and has 240 edges, 150 two-dimensional faces (60 hexagons, 90 squares) and 30 cells: 10 three-dimensional permutohedra of four consecutive values (one coordinate fixed at 1 or at 5; classically the truncated octahedron) and 20 hexagonal prisms. Its edges join orders that differ by swapping two neighbouring values, so its skeleton is the web above: four exchanges at every knot, in ten directions. The following facts are classical and were recounted exactly (web_of_bonds.py): the number of exchanges between two orders is the number of pairs they put in opposite order; 54321 is farthest from 12345, ten exchanges away, along 768 shortest routes (Stanley's count of reduced words of the longest permutation); the 2-faces are the web's rings, a square for two swaps of disjoint value pairs and a hexagon for the braid ring sksk+1sk = sk+1sksk+1. In #104 the same solid was swept by a moving 3-space: 26 cells are born, 66 pairs swap and 26 cells die, and these are the numbers of orders with one, two and three descents (1, 26, 66, 26, 1): the h-vector of the permutohedron is the Eulerian numbers (Postnikov, Reiner and Williams 2008).

The rules among swaps. Two swaps can meet on the solid in only two ways (Corollary {{s:o1.2}}). If they move values far apart in size, such as 1, 2 and 4, 5, they commute: either order gives the same result, and the four orders form a square. If they share a value, such as 1, 2 and 2, 3, they braid: three swaps each way round give the same result, and the six orders form a hexagon (Figure {{f:rules}}). The 90 squares and 60 hexagons of the 4D permutohedron are all the rules there are: the classical presentation of the group of orders by neighbour swaps (Coxeter; Humphreys), drawn as faces.

The 4D Vacuus

The Vacuus of a centrally symmetric polytope is the set of hyperplanes through its centre spanned by its ridges (the faces one dimension below the cells), each ridge lying in exactly one; a slice is the polytope cut by one of them. The Vacuus solids of #87 are built from such central slices (there as fins, one per edge, which for these solids fill central discs): the octahedron from three squares through its centre that carry all its edges, the cuboctahedron from four hexagons. One dimension up the slices are 3D and carry the 2-faces. A 2-face of the 4D permutohedron does not pass through the centre, so together with the centre it spans exactly one 3-space through the centre: its central slice. The 4D Vacuus is the set of these slices.

A 2-face is an ordered split of the positions {1, …, 5} into three blocks B₁, B₂, B₃ of sizes (a, b, c): the positions in B₁ hold the a smallest values in any order, those in B₂ the next b, those in B₃ the largest c. Its central slice has the normal vector that takes the value c₁ = bc(b + c) on B₁, c₂ = −ac(a + 2b + c) on B₂ and c₃ = ab(a + b) on B₃ (it is orthogonal to (1, …, 1), constant on each block, and orthogonal to the vector of centred block sums S₁ = −a(b + c)/2, S₂ = b(a − c)/2, S₃ = c(a + b)/2, so its hyperplane passes through the face; checked against exact elimination for every 2-face).

Counting: splits (1, 1, 3) and (1, 2, 2) give 5!/(1!1!3!) = 20 and 5!/(1!2!2!) = 30 slices; (1, 3, 1) and (2, 1, 2) give C(5, 3) = 10 and C(5, 1) = 5; in all 65, and 30·2 + 5·6 = 90 squares, 20·2 + 10·2 = 60 hexagons. Slices with the same split are images of one another under a permutation of the coordinates, so they are congruent. The shapes were computed in exact rational arithmetic: corners are the solid's corners on the slice and the points where edges cross it; every face proposed by a hull routine was confirmed exactly (all its corners on one plane, every other corner strictly on one side), and every shape has Euler number 2. ∎

An earlier floating-point count split the 30 congruent slices of the first row into several apparent types; the exact computation above supersedes it.

The heart: five permutohedra of 1, 2, 4, 5

The five slices of the last row are the slices pi = 3: the middle value held in one place while the other four take 1, 2, 4, 5 in every order. Each is the 3D permutohedron of the values 1, 2, 4, 5: the 1:2 Truncated Octahedron, a truncated octahedron in shape (the same faces meeting in the same way as Archimedes', but not his equal-edged solid) with squares of side 1 and hexagons whose sides are 1 and 2 in turn (√2 and 2√2 in these coordinates), and it carries the six squares of the 4D solid in which the middle value stays put. These five are the heart of the Vacuus (Figure {{f:o6.2}}; the orderings of 1, 2, 4, 5 on the solid are drawn in Figure {{f:o1.2}}). Sweeps of sections parallel to a cell, which pass through such a slice, are known (J. Bowers; Galoomba, Polytope Wiki); we found none that names the central one as the permutohedron of 1, 2, 4, 5 or shows the five together.

Theorem {{S:o6.2}} (#105, Theorem 2) (how the heart is slotted together). Any two of the five central permutohedra, pi = 3 and pj = 3, cross in a regular hexagon through the centre, with side equal to its radius 2√2; its corners are the points with pi = pj = 3 and the other three coordinates 1, 3, 5 in any order. Three of them share a segment through the centre, and four or five share only the centre.

Proof. A point lies in the permutohedron exactly when its coordinates sum to 15 and its k largest coordinates sum to at most 5 + 4 + … (the k largest values), for every k (majorization; Rado 1952, after Hardy, Littlewood and Pólya). With pi = pj = 3 and the other three q₁, q₂, q₃ summing to 9, these conditions reduce to 1 ≤ qm ≤ 5: a hexagon with corners the orders of (1, 3, 5), each at distance √8 from the centre and from its neighbours. The plane meets no corner or edge of the solid; its six corners are where it pierces 2-faces, which was confirmed exactly by intersecting the plane with all 150 faces. Three conditions pi = pj = pk = 3 leave q₁ + q₂ = 6 with 1 ≤ q ≤ 5, a segment; four leave the centre. ∎

The heart nests the dimensions inside one another: the 4D permutohedron of 1, …, 5 holds at its centre, five times, the 3D permutohedron of the four values that remain when the middle one is taken out; in the same way the hexagon of the orders of 1, 2, 3 holds the segment between the orders of 1 and 3. This works whenever the number of values is odd, so that there is a middle value to hold still. (For any value v the hyperplane pi = v meets no edge inside it, since an edge changes two coordinates from k, k + 1 to k + 1, k through non-integers; so the slice pi = v is the permutohedron of the other n − 1 values, and it passes through the centre exactly when v is the middle value.)

The Queens' Vacuus and the Mirror Law

The heart of the 4D Vacuus is the permutohedron's own: none of its slices is a mirror. The rest of this chapter retells #107, which asks the same question of every polytope built from a reflection group, the Queens (the 600-cell and the 120-cell) among them, and answers it by one rule read off the group's chain, the Mirror Law (which faces of a polytope built from a reflection group lie on the group's mirrors). The rule decides which faces lie on mirrors, the fixed places of the group's reflections; Book Eight, The Platonic Solids, applies it to the five solids (its Chapter 4, a retelling), and this chapter is its home.

The setting

A finite reflection group in n dimensions is generated by reflections in n mirrors whose unit normals α1, …, αn are its simple roots; the mirrors of the group are the hyperplanes orthogonal to its roots, and every root is a combination of simple roots whose coefficients are all ≥ 0 or all ≤ 0 (Humphreys 1990, §1.3). Its Coxeter diagram joins nodes i, j whose mirrors meet at angle π/m, m ≥ 3, by a bond labelled m. The groups here have diagrams that are chains: B3 4–3 (the cube), H3 5–3 (the icosahedron), A4 3–3–3 (the 5-cell), B4 4–3–3 (the tesseract), F4 3–4–3 (the 24-cell) and H4 5–3–3 (the Queens).

Wythoff's construction places a seed point x with labels λi = αi · x ≥ 0 and takes the hull of its images. The nodes with λi > 0 are the ringed nodes. Its faces are the images of the faces FJ = hull(WJ x), where WJ is generated by the reflections of a node set J whose every connected piece contains a ringed node (Coxeter 1973; Champagne, Kjiri, Patera and Sharp 1995). The orbit study of 11 November read these seeds back from the corners of the solids of the series: the Queens' path is the seed sliding along the H4 chain, from the 120-cell (1, 0, 0, 0) through the truncated 120-cell (1, 1, 0, 0), the bitruncated 600-cell (0, 1, 1, 0) and the truncated 600-cell (0, 0, 1, 1) to the 600-cell (0, 0, 0, 1); the golden loop of DICTONIC (the truncated octahedra T(x) of Chapter {{ch:slider}}, which fill space in pairs) is the B3 seed (0, x, 1).

The Mirror Law

Theorem {{S:o10.1}} (the Mirror Law; #107, Theorem 1). Let P be the Wythoff polytope of a seed x, and J a node set of size n − 2 giving faces of P (so they have dimension n − 2: edges in three dimensions, 2-faces in four). The faces of type J lie on mirrors of the group if and only if the roots of the unringed nodes include one orthogonal to αj for every j in J.

Proof. The face FJ lies in the affine space x + span(αJ), so the hyperplane through the centre that contains it is span(x, αJ), and its other faces of type J are images of FJ, which carry mirrors to mirrors. That hyperplane is a mirror exactly when its normal is a root, that is, when some root r is orthogonal to x and to every αj, j ∈ J. Write r = Σ cl αl, all cl of one sign. Then r · x = Σ cl (αl · x) after expressing x in the basis dual to the simple roots, which is Σ λl cl; with λl > 0 on the ringed nodes and every cl of one sign, this vanishes exactly when cl = 0 on every ringed node, so that r is a root of the unringed nodes. ∎

The law was checked against direct measurement, the normal of span(x, αJ) compared with every root, for every seed of zeros and ones and every face type in A3, B3, H3, A4, B4, F4 and H4: 228 cases, no disagreement (mirror_law.py).

Corollary {{S:o10.2}} (the Queens). Every face of a regular polytope lies on a mirror. The 1200 triangles of the 600-cell and the 720 pentagons of the 120-cell lie on the same 60 central slices, the 60 mirrors of H4: 20 triangles on each, and 12 pentagons on each.

Proof. A regular polytope has one ringed node at an end of the chain, node 1 say, and one face type J = {1, 2} (in four dimensions); node 4 is unringed and α4 is orthogonal to α1 and α2. So every face lies on a mirror. Every mirror carries the same number of faces because the group moves any mirror to any other (its roots form one orbit), so 1200 / 60 = 20 and 720 / 60 = 12; that no other slice carries a face, and that both Queens use all 60 mirrors, was checked by computing the central hyperplane of every face (mirror_check.py). ∎

Corollary {{S:o10.3}} (the middles). For a four-node chain 1–2–3–4 and the seed (0, 1, 1, 0), the faces of the two end bonds lie on mirrors and the faces of the middle bond (2m-gons, m its label) lie on none. In B4 and H4 these are exactly the faces between cells of different kinds.

Proof. The face types are {1, 2}, {2, 3} and {3, 4}; the unringed nodes 1 and 4 are not joined, so their only roots are ±α1 and ±α4. α4 is orthogonal to α1 and α2, and α1 to α3 and α4, so the end faces lie on mirrors (Theorem {{s:o10.1}}); neither is orthogonal to both α2 and α3, since node 1 is joined to 2 and node 4 to 3, so the middle faces lie on none. A middle face lies between a cell of type {1, 2, 3} and one of type {2, 3, 4}; they are congruent when the chain reads the same from both ends (A4 3–3–3, F4 3–4–3) and differ otherwise. ∎

Back in three dimensions

Corollary {{S:o10.4}}. For the golden loop T(x) (the permutohedra of four values with gaps x : 1 : x, Theorem {{s:o2.2}}), the B3 seed (0, x, 1), the 12 edges of type {3} lie on the three coordinate mirrors and the 24 edges of type {2} lie on none.

Proof. Node 1 is the only unringed node; α1 is orthogonal to α3 (nodes 1 and 3 are not joined) but not to α2. ∎ This is the split seen in the 3D Vacuus of the 1:2 Truncated Octahedron (Figure {{f:o11.1}}; #105, Figure 4) and measured on the Icosaxes and the Unborn Axes: three discs of four edges, the other edges each on a central plane of its own with one partner.

The slices read through the Mirror Law

None of the 65 slices is a mirror of the permutohedron's symmetry group: its seed (1, 1, 1, 1) rings every node of the chain 3–3–3, so by the Mirror Law (#107, Theorem 1) no 2-dimensional face lies on a mirror. The five heart slices of Theorem {{s:o6.2}} carry six faces each; every other slice carries one face and its opposite, two. Across all the polytopes of the seven reflection-group chains, slices off the mirrors that carry three or more faces occur in four dimensions only here and in the runcinated 5-cell, which shares these five (#108, Observation 4): the heart of the 4D Vacuus is the permutohedron's own, not its group's.

Two kinds of Vacuus

The Mirror Law accounts for every Vacuus slice that is a mirror. Chapter {{ch:o6}} met the other kind: the 4D permutohedron's seed rings every node, so none of its faces lies on a mirror, yet five slices through its heart carry six faces each. Across all the reflection-group polytopes such slices off the mirrors occur only in the quasi-regular cuboctahedron and icosidodecahedron and, in four dimensions, in the permutohedron and the runcinated 5-cell (#108, Observation 4). In three dimensions the Mirror Law also reads the Dogstar of #42 (Hart's stellation 8 of the dodecahedron): 114 of its 138 edges lie on 15 central discs, the 15 mirrors of the icosahedral group.

Permutohedra inside the Queens' cousins. The permutohedra of four values appear inside other four-dimensional solids too. In the tesseract trimmed by its polar 16-cell, K ∩ μK*, at μ = φ², 8 of the 24 cells are Icosaxes, the permutohedron of four values at the golden stop of Chapter {{ch:slider}}, and the other 16 are Lesser Golden Cut Tetrahedra: every cell is a golden cut (#91; home Book Nine, The Golden Ladder, Chapter 8).

Worked example: the 150 faces on 65 slices. The 2-faces of the 4D permutohedron are the splits of five places into three blocks of consecutive values. Blocks of sizes 3, 1, 1 in some order give the hexagons: 5!/(3! 1! 1!) = 20 for each of the three orders of the sizes, 60 in all. Blocks 2, 2, 1 give the squares: 5!/(2! 2! 1!) = 30 for each of three orders, 90 in all. 60 + 90 = 150. Each lies on one central slice, and the slices number 20 + 30 + 10 + 5 = 65 (Theorem {{s:o6.1}}): a split and its reverse share a slice, and the five heart slices carry six faces each.

The 4D permutohedron is the web of swaps: its squares say that far-apart swaps commute, its hexagons that three values braid, and its Vacuus carries all of them on 65 central slices, five 1:2 Truncated Octahedra at its heart. Now sweep it.
