The Spheres of Dharma Turned Inside Out

Polar reciprocation reverses the Spheres of Dharma into a new exact φ³ cycle of nested icosahedral shells: one kind of piece per layer, an evenly stepped nest, the great stellated dodecahedron locked in the outer gap, a dual pair in the inner gap, the golden frame, the cycle repeating inward to its core, the original and reversed cycles sharing one space, and the checks that keep each step exact

The Spheres of Dharma, the Hecatodihedron, the idea of turning the cycle inside out by polar reciprocation, the call for an even nest, the stars and the Hecatodihedron tried in the gaps, the dual pair and its reversed order, the golden rectangles, the endless core, the two cycles together as a toggle, the demand for stricter checks and the name Ogdoecontahedron are DICTO's. Computation, exact arithmetic and renders were done with Claude (Anthropic) as a research assistant.
Abstract. The Spheres of Dharma nest seven icosahedral solids exactly, from an icosahedron out to the icosahedron φ³ larger. DICTO asked what the cycle looks like turned inside out. Replacing every station by its polar reciprocal reverses the nesting and gives a new exact cycle: dodecahedron ⊂ icosidodecahedron ⊂ Ogdoecontahedron ⊂ pentakis dodecahedron ⊂ icosahedron ⊂ rhombic triacontahedron ⊂ dodecahedron φ³ larger (volume ratio φ⁹). Its one new station, the dual of the DICTO Hecatodihedron (80 faces: 20 regular hexagons and 60 kites), is the DICTO Ogdoecontahedron, described in a companion paper. Cutting each layer by cones over the faces of its inner solid gives one kind of piece per layer (12, 12, 20, 60, 20, 30), every volume exact in ℚ(√5) with remainder 0. The cycle inherits two loose ends. Tightening them gives an evenly stepped nest, still one kind per layer and exact. Left loose, the large outer gap holds the great stellated dodecahedron at exactly one size: its 20 points on the outer dodecahedron's corners, its 12 valleys on the triacontahedron's tips, cutting the gap into 60 tetrahedra and 60 pentagonal pyramids. The inner gap admits no star and no locked dual pair, but the truncated icosidodecahedron pair fits in reverse order (as polarity demands), scaled by 6/φ² and 2/15 with play √5·φ³/9. The golden rectangles that frame the whole are the outer dodecahedron's own small stellation, and the cycle repeats inward without end. The original and reversed cycles can share one space: a sphere of polarity exists that makes them share the icosahedron and dodecahedron, and another that makes them share the icosidodecahedron and triacontahedron, but no sphere does both. We also record the checks this study forced on us, because three of our first tables were wrong.

1. The Spheres of Dharma turned inside out

The Spheres of Dharma (DICTO DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.23310833) is an exact cycle of nested icosahedral solids: icosahedron ⊂ icosidodecahedron ⊂ dodecahedron ⊂ truncated icosahedron ⊂ Hecatodihedron ⊂ rhombic triacontahedron ⊂ icosahedron φ³ larger, each layer filled by a few repeated pieces. Its seventh stage, closed as a convex solid, is the DICTO Truncated-Icosahedral Hecatodihedron (DICTO DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.23360858): the truncated icosahedron with a Shield on each hexagon, 102 faces (12 pentagons, 30 rhombi, 60 triangles), 80 corners.

DICTO's question was what happens to the cycle turned inside out. Polar reciprocation in a sphere about the common centre sends every plane to a point and every point to a plane; a solid goes to its dual, and inclusion reverses: if P ⊂ Q then Q* ⊂ P* (a classical fact of convex geometry; see e.g. Firsching 2015). So the reversed cycle is again a nest, read from the other end:

D ⊂ ID ⊂ Ogdoecontahedron ⊂ PD ⊂ I ⊂ RT ⊂ D·φ³

(dodecahedron, icosidodecahedron, the Hecatodihedron's dual, pentakis dodecahedron, icosahedron, rhombic triacontahedron, dodecahedron). We computed every pole exactly in ℚ(√5) from the snapped original corners. The outer dodecahedron is exactly φ³ times the inner one, so the volume closes at 38 + 17√5 = φ⁹. Duality swaps solids in pairs, so the icosidodecahedron and the triacontahedron, and the icosahedron and the dodecahedron, appear in both cycles in swapped places; the truncated icosahedron becomes the pentakis dodecahedron, and the Hecatodihedron becomes something new.

2. The new station

The polar of the Hecatodihedron is a new solid, which DICTO named the Ogdoecontahedron for its 80 faces: 20 regular hexagons and 60 kites, with 102 corners and 180 edges. The solid itself (its edges, its sphere of corners, its face arrangement and its relation to quasicrystals) is described in a companion paper, The DICTO Ogdoecontahedron. In the nest, its 20 hexagons lie in the planes of the icosidodecahedron's 20 triangles, each hexagon holding a triangle, and its 60 kites lie in the faces of the pentakis dodecahedron around it.

3. One kind of piece per layer

We cut each layer A ⊂ B by the cones from the centre over the faces of the inner solid A: each piece is cone(F) ∩ B beyond F, convex by construction (the Kion construction of Theaetetus, DICTO DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.23378070). Faces of A that already lie on B's surface give no piece. Every corner was solved exactly as the meeting point of three exact planes, and every volume summed exactly against its layer.

4. Two loose ends, and the even nest

The faithful copy keeps the original closure (outer dodecahedron = inner × φ³), and that leaves two stations floating: the triacontahedron could grow ×1.894 before touching the outer dodecahedron, and the inner dodecahedron ×1.232 before touching the icosidodecahedron. The four middle stations touch. DICTO asked for a more even nest. Tightening both ends, the inner dodecahedron grows by exactly 11/18 + 5√5/18 until its corners touch the icosidodecahedron's triangles, and the outer one shrinks by exactly 5 − 2√5 until the triacontahedron's twelve tips touch its face centres. The inradii then step evenly (0.495, 0.567, 0.623, 0.653, 0.714, 0.764, 0.898), the outer layer falls from 25.2 to 1.9, and every layer is still one kind of piece, exact with remainder 0: the inner layer becomes 12 near-antiprism columns, (−35/729 + 71√5/2187) each, and the outer 30 wedges on the rhombi, (−7352/3 + 1096√5) each. The price is the golden closure: the tight cycle repeats at linear ratio 1.8146 (squared 49815/2 − 22275√5/2), not a power of φ. We keep both versions.

5. The great stellated dodecahedron in the outer gap

DICTO asked whether a Kepler–Poinsot solid could sit in the faithful nest's large outer gap. We treated each star as a filled body (points of nonzero winding number; the densities at the centre come out 3, 3, 7, 7 as they should) and found the range of sizes that keeps the triacontahedron inside the star and the star inside the outer dodecahedron. The great icosahedron does not fit. The great and small stellated dodecahedra float between two sizes. The great stellated dodecahedron fits at exactly one size: its 20 points are the outer dodecahedron's 20 corners, and its 12 valleys, where five spikes meet, are the chain's icosahedron corners, which are also the triacontahedron's five-fold tips. We checked this exactly: each of the 12 icosahedron corners lies on five of the star's face planes.

The star is the chain's icosahedron with a spike on each face (Kepler; our In-betweens paper, DICTO DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.23345502, #27), and its volume is exactly 3 icosahedra, as follows from the standard formulas. Its surface is 60 triangles, each seen once from the centre, and the cones over them cut the gap into two exact layers: inside the star, around the triacontahedron, 60 congruent tetrahedra (point, triacontahedron corner and two valleys), (188/3 − 28√5) each; outside it, up to the dodecahedron, 60 congruent pentagonal pyramids, (112/3 − 248√5/15) each. Both sum to their layers with remainder 0.

The fit is rhythmic rather than lucky. The squared ratio of the star's point radius to its valley radius is 3φ³/√5: apart from the fixed √3 and √5 that convert between three-fold and five-fold radii, it is φ³, the step by which the whole cycle closes.

6. The inner gap and a dual pair in reverse order

In the inner gap, between the dodecahedron and the icosidodecahedron, none of the four stars fits. The Hecatodihedron itself fits but floats: its size can vary ×1.232, touching 20 points at either end and never both. DICTO then asked for a dual pair instead. A pair X, Y locks when three contacts hold at once (dodecahedron on X, X on Y, Y on the icosidodecahedron). No icosahedral Archimedean–Catalan pair locks. The truncated icosidodecahedron does not fit inside its dual; as DICTO pointed out, in the reversed cycle the pair must go in reverse order too, and so it does: the disdyakis triacontahedron inside the truncated icosidodecahedron. Exactly: the disdyakis triacontahedron (from the dual of the standard truncated icosidodecahedron) is scaled by 9 − 3√5 = 6/φ², the truncated icosidodecahedron by 2/15, and the play between them is 5/9 + 2√5/9 = √5·φ³/9 ≈ 1.0525. The truncated icosidodecahedron's 12 decagons lie exactly in the icosidodecahedron's pentagons. The layers cut into 12 pieces of one kind, 120 of one kind and 50 caps of two kinds, all exact with remainder 0. The nearest pair to a lock is the rhombicosidodecahedron inside the deltoidal hexecontahedron, play exactly 62/123 + 137√5/615 ≈ 1.00218: short of locking by 0.2%.

7. The golden frame

DICTO asked whether golden rectangles would frame the whole. The 15 golden rectangles of an icosahedron (five perpendicular triples) clear the whole nest when the icosahedron is the next turn of the cycle, the chain's icosahedron × φ³ (the smallest icosahedron around the dodecahedron is not enough). Then each of the outer dodecahedron's 30 edges lies along one of the rectangles' 30 long edges, centred, and the long edge is φ³ times as long (corner radius² 10 + 2√5, midpoint radius² 4). This is classical: the 30 long edges are the edges of the outer dodecahedron's small stellated dodecahedron, made by extending its edges (Kepler 1619), a pentagram chord being 1 + 2φ = φ³ times the pentagon side. The frame that holds the cycle is its outer dodecahedron's own first stellation.

8. The core: the cycle repeats inward

What fills the inner dodecahedron? The cycle itself. Its outer dodecahedron is exactly φ³ times its inner one, so the whole nest scaled by 1/φ³ fills the inner dodecahedron with no gap. Inside that sits the next turn, and so on to the centre. In the faithful version every turn brings back the large gap, so the great stellated dodecahedron locks at the heart of every turn: its points on that turn's dodecahedron corners and its valleys holding the next triacontahedron. The golden frame of Section 7 is the next turn outward, and the core is the next turn inward.

9. Both cycles at once: the toggle

DICTO asked whether the original cycle and its reversed image could share one space. They can, and the sphere of reciprocation is the one free choice. Polarity in a sphere of radius ρ sends radius r to ρ²/r, so in log-radius it is a reflection about log ρ; the cycle's φ³ repeat is a translation. Together they give mirror spheres every φ3/2 in radius: the union of the two cycles reads the same outward and inward, a palindrome in scale, and the polarity swaps the two cycles.

  • Ends setting, ρ² = φ²/4 = (3 + √5)/8, mirror sphere radius φ/2: the polarity sends the original icosahedron to the original dodecahedron, so the two cycles share I and D. The reversed turn nests almost entirely inside the original icosahedron.
  • Middle setting, ρ² = 3φ²/4, mirror sphere radius √3·φ/2: they share the icosidodecahedron and the triacontahedron instead. The reversed cycle is then exactly 3 times its ends-setting size.
  • Never both. Moving from one setting to the other scales ρ² by 3, while the cycle only repeats under powers of φ³; 3 is not a power of φ, so no sphere gives both. Nor can the loose ends be bent to allow it: the original icosahedron can be scaled only within [1/1.232241, 1.894427] before the nest breaks, and sharing both pairs would need a scale of 3/φ³ ≈ 0.708 or 3.

Cut against each other, the two cycles form one solid of convex pieces, with every piece and layer summing exactly; that interlock, and the shapes of its pieces, are reported separately (studies/2026-10-16-interlock).

10. Method: the checks this study forced on us

Three of our first tables in this study were wrong, and each was caught before publication. Standard coordinate lists for the Archimedean solids come in two mirror golden frames, and most of ours were in the wrong one, so they did not share the chain's axes. A slack formula was inverted. A float face count reported 103 kinds of piece in a layer of 120 congruent pieces. DICTO asked for rules that would prevent this, and every study script now calls a shared module (studies/_lib/geomcheck.py) that stops with an error instead of printing a wrong table:

  1. Frame: every generated solid must be invariant under the chain's own five-fold and three-fold turns.
  2. Both orders: every pair is tested both ways; polar reversal swaps the order of a dual pair.
  3. Known case first: every formula reproduces a known answer (the dodecahedron's slack 1.232 in the icosidodecahedron; the triacontahedron locked in the star; star densities 3, 3, 7, 7) before it is used.
  4. Kinds by symmetry: pieces are the same kind when the icosahedral group maps one onto the other, after reducing float hulls to their true corners.
  5. Exact gate: a float result is a lead; a claim is made when the exact ℚ(√5) remainder is 0. Exact corners come from plane triples that reproduce the point.
  6. Labels: every number is marked exact or float, faithful or adjusted, and originals are never overwritten.

The module's tests replay each of our slips and show the check catching it.

11. Relation to known work, and status

Known and credited: polar reciprocation and the reversal of inclusion (convex geometry; Firsching 2015); the largest copy of one polytope in another (Croft 1980; Firsching 2015 computes it exactly; our fits are the concentric, same-orientation case); nested icosahedral shells in clusters, viruses and fullerenes (Takakura et al. 2007, the Tsai cluster of the Spheres of Dharma; Twarock, Valiunas and Zappa 2015; Dechant et al. 2014); the Kepler–Poinsot solids, the great stellated dodecahedron as an icosahedron with twenty spikes and the small stellated dodecahedron as the dodecahedron's extended edges (Kepler 1619; Poinsot 1810); the 15 golden rectangles of the icosahedron. Hafner (2007) dissects the small and great stellated dodecahedra into the triacontahedron and hexecontahedron using 60 tetrahedra, each 1/6 of a prolate golden rhombohedron; our 60 tetrahedra between the triacontahedron and the star are different pieces: each has exactly φ² times the volume of his on the same triacontahedron edge (checked in ℚ(√5)).

Not found in our reference library and web searches (16 October 2026): the reversed Spheres of Dharma as an exact nest with one piece kind per layer; the tight nest; the great stellated dodecahedron's lock in the reversed cycle, which follows directly from classical facts and is presented as an observation about this cycle, not a new theorem; the inner-gap dual pairs and their exact scales and play; the exact pieces and volumes; the two settings of the toggle and the proof that they exclude each other.
