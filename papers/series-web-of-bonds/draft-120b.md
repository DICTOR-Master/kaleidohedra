# From the Octet to Every Stacking: the Web of Bonds

**Main Theorem:** In every close-packed stacking, along the whole weight walk, each tetrahedral hole's piece is the piece of the cubic or the hexagonal stacking named by its nearest layer, and each octahedral hole's piece is a top half and a bottom half, each the half of the cubic or the hexagonal piece named by the layer on that side; so the pieces of all stackings are finite in kind and an octahedral piece's volume is always the mean of its two parents' volumes [7].

## 1. Pieces that fill space: #47 Platonic mixes → #48 Golden Split → #51 Delian Bond

DICTO's idea establishes that the fundamental space-filling units arise from mixing Platonic solids and splitting the icosahedron to fill the gaps left by its neighbors. This section traces the lineage from the general mixing of Platonic solids to the specific "Golden Split" that creates convex space-fillers, culminating in the "Delian Bond" where two solids share equal volume at a precise cut.

The journey begins with the general result that every mix of the 31 sets of Platonic solids of edge 1 fills space using a kit of seven connector pieces [8]. From this general capacity, DICTO identified the specific geometry of the icosahedron, which cannot fill space alone, and proposed the "Golden Split" to resolve its gaps. In the simple cubic lattice of icosahedra, the gaps consist of squeezed icosahedra (the Unborn) and thin tetrahedra (the AXE). DICTO's theorem states that cutting every AXE from the point $1/\phi$ of the way along its axis and distributing the four resulting pyramids to the touching neighbors transforms the icosahedra and Unborns into two convex truncated octahedra: the Icosaxes and the Unborn Axes [9]. This split is unique; it is the only cut point where both resulting solids are convex.

This geometric resolution leads to the "Delian Bond," where DICTO considers the octet (the space-filling of regular tetrahedra and octahedra). By sliding a flat cut through every tetrahedron–octahedron pair, DICTO generates a family of pairs of convex solids that fill space, ranging from Kepler's rhombic dodecahedron to the cube. At a specific cut position, the two solids, named by DICTO as Samson and Delilah, have exactly equal volume: the tetrahedron's volume doubles and the octahedron's halves, mirroring the ancient Delian problem of doubling the cube [10]. This establishes the first "bond" where two distinct shapes share a face and a volume ratio of 1:1.

## 2. The walk and the web of bonds: #52 Web of Bonds → #53 Global Web of Bonds

DICTO's concept of the "walk" treats space-filling as a continuous deformation where a single weight parameter slides between two kinds of points, rewiring the network of shared faces. The "web of bonds" is the dual structure of this process, where the edges of the weighted Delaunay triangulation represent the physical bonds between pieces.

Paper #52 [11] proves that both the golden loop and the Delian loop are weighted Voronoi (power) diagrams of one fixed crowd of points of two kinds, with one weight slid between the kinds. In this framework, the bond between unlike pieces never breaks inside a loop, while the two like bonds hand over: gradually in the golden loop and all at once at the octet in the Delian loop. The physical interpretation is that the weight corresponds to the square of a ball radius, bounded by Pauling's classical radius-ratio limits.

Paper #53 [12] extends this to the "Global Web of Bonds," computing the weighted Voronoi diagram exactly along the whole weight line for ten classical two-kind crystal site sets and one three-kind set. The study confirms that the two hand-overs of the DICTONIC loops recur in diverse crystal networks. The gradual hand-over appears in zinc blende, the holes of hexagonal close packing, and the A15 and C15 alloy structures, while the single-switch hand-over appears in the square lattice, the honeycomb, and rock salt. In every two-kind network, the bond between unlike pieces remains intact, while the like bonds transfer ownership of the space.

## 3. Pairs along the walk: #55 David and Goliath → #56 Sonsam and Lahdeli (+ #42 Sunstar as keyed switch point)

DICTO's walk reveals specific named pairs of solids that emerge at critical weight values in different crystal structures, such as the A15 alloy and the twinned octet. These pairs, named by DICTO, represent the physical realization of the abstract weight walk in specific geometric contexts.

In the A15 (Cr$_3$Si) structure, the power diagram with a weight difference of $3/4$ between the Si and Cr sites yields a face-to-face tiling by Goliath and David [13]. Goliath is the convex hull of Jessen's orthogonal icosahedron, and David is a tetragonal disphenoid. There are two Goliaths and six Davids per cubic cell. This result identifies the A15 icosahedron as Jessen's convex hull, a fact not previously found in the standard literature.

On the twinned octet (the holes of ideal hexagonal close packing), the weight walk passes through a point where all pieces have equal volume $\sqrt{2}/6$, yielding the pair Sonsam and Lahdeli [14]. Sonsam has 19 corners with one face being a nine-sided polygon lying on the mirror plane, while Lahdeli has 12 corners. This equal-volume point is the single root of an irreducible cubic. The walk continues to a stop of Ian Durys and $60^\circ$ rhombohedra, and ends in Blockheads alone. DICTO notes that on the cubic octet, the same walk goes from the octet straight to cubes, with Samson and Delilah equal at $\lambda = 3/8 - \sqrt[3]{4}/4$. The "Sunstar" paper [2] serves as a keyed switch point in this broader context, providing the twin of the DICTO Jewel and the exact stellation of the Dogstar, which complements the space-filling logic of the octet.

## 4. Every stacking: #40 OG family on every stacking (early signpost) → #57 The Rule of Halves (summit; Janus c/h pieces)

The culmination of the series is the generalization of the weight walk to *every* possible close-packed stacking, not just the pure cubic or hexagonal ones. DICTO's "Rule of Halves" provides a finite set of building blocks for all such stackings, derived from the two pure cases.

Paper #40 [4] first signposted that the DICTO OG family (Ogdoecontahedron and its shells) can be placed in the tetrahedral–octahedral honeycomb stacked in any order, using one exact kit for the octet, the gyrated octet, classical polytypes, and a non-repeating Fibonacci stacking. This established that the geometry of the OG family is robust across different stackings.

The summit, Paper #57 [7], states the "Rule of Halves." In every close-packed stacking, a tetrahedral hole's piece is determined solely by the nearest layer (cubic or hexagonal). An octahedral hole, sitting halfway between two layers, takes a piece composed of a top half and a bottom half, each being the half of the cubic or hexagonal piece named by the layer on that side. Consequently, the volume of any octahedral piece is always the mean of its two parents' volumes. Where a cubic layer meets a hexagonal one, "Janus c/h pieces" appear, which DICTO named Chucky and Fang. These pieces are finite in kind, meaning that despite the infinite variety of stackings, the number of distinct piece shapes and volumes is finite.

## Epilogue: the Janus kit (physical), Quanta of Space (new centre)

The series concludes with the physical realization of the Janus kit, a set of pieces that can be assembled to model any close-packed stacking, and the proposal of a "Quanta of Space" as a new center for this geometry. The Janus kit consists of the finite set of pieces derived from the Rule of Halves, allowing for the construction of any polytype. The "Quanta of Space" represents the fundamental unit of this web, where the bonds and weights define the local geometry. DICTO's work suggests that the "Quanta of Space" is not a single solid but the dynamic interplay of the Janus pieces and the weight walk, providing a new framework for understanding crystal structures and foam geometries.
