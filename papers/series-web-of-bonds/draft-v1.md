*A series paper. It re-tells, theorem first, the arc of papers #47–#57; every result here is proved in the paper cited where it enters, and those papers are unchanged.*

**Main theorem (proved in #57).** In every close-packed stacking, along the whole weight walk, each tetrahedral hole's piece is the piece of the cubic or the hexagonal stacking named by its nearest layer, and each octahedral hole's piece is a top half and a bottom half, each the half of the cubic or the hexagonal piece named by the layer on that side. So the pieces of all stackings are finite in kind, and an octahedral piece's volume is always the mean of its two parents' volumes.

However layers of balls are stacked, the gaps between them are filled by pieces taken from just two pure stackings, cubic and hexagonal. This paper tells how the series reached that rule, starting from the octet.

## 1. Pieces that fill space: #47 → #48 → #51

*Mixing the Platonic solids, then cutting the awkward gaps in the right place, gives pairs of convex solids that fill space, and one cut gives a pair of equal volume.*

Paper #47 showed that for each of the 31 sets of Platonic solids of edge 1 there is a filling of space using exactly those solids, with connectors from a kit of seven pieces.

Icosahedra cannot fill space alone. In the simple cubic lattice of icosahedra two gaps are left: a squeezed icosahedron, the Unborn, and thin tetrahedra, the AXE. Paper #48, the Golden Split, proves that cutting every AXE from the point $1/\varphi$ of the way along its axis, and giving each of its four pyramids to the neighbour it touches, turns the icosahedra and the Unborns into two convex truncated octahedra, the Icosaxes and the Unborn Axes, which fill space; this is the only cut point at which both are convex. In the Lotus Jewel network the same cut gives a keyed pair, the Lotus Jewel Axes and the Dodecaxes, of volumes $\sqrt5$ and $2$.

Paper #51, the Delian Bond, turns to the octet of regular tetrahedra and octahedra. One flat cut, slid through every tetrahedron–octahedron pair, gives a family of pairs of convex solids that fill space, from Kepler's rhombic dodecahedron through the octet to the cube. At one cut the two solids, which DICTO named Samson and Delilah, have exactly equal volume: twice the octet's tetrahedron and half its octahedron, the doubling of the ancient Delian problem in both directions at once. Two other cuts make every edge equal.

## 2. The walk and the web of bonds: #52 → #53

*Draw a line, a bond, between every two pieces that share a face. As one number slides, the pieces change but the web of bonds rewires in only two ways.*

Paper #52 proves that both DICTONIC loops, the golden loop of #48 and the Delian loop of #51, are weighted Voronoi (power) diagrams of one fixed crowd of points of two kinds, with one weight slid between the kinds. The bonds are the edges of the dual weighted-Delaunay web. The bond between unlike pieces never breaks inside a loop, while the two like bonds hand over: gradually in the golden loop, and all at once, at the octet, in the Delian loop. Read physically, the weight is a ball radius squared: the golden loop is the caesium chloride structure, the Delian loop the fluorite structure, and the range where the balls can touch is bounded exactly by Pauling's classical radius-ratio limits.

DICTO proposed that these two hand-overs turn up elsewhere. Paper #53 computes the power diagram exactly along the whole weight line for ten classical two-kind crystal site sets and one three-kind set. The proposal holds: the hand-over with a single switch recurs in the square lattice with its centres, in the honeycomb with its hexagon centres and in rock salt; the gradual hand-over recurs in zinc blende, in the holes of hexagonal close packing, and in the A15 and C15 alloy structures.

## 3. Pairs along the walk: #55 → #56, with #42

*At special points of the walk, named pairs of solids appear.*

Paper #55 proves that in the A15 (Cr$_3$Si) structure the power diagram with weight difference $3/4$ between the Si and Cr sites is exactly a face-to-face tiling of space by Goliath, the convex hull of Jessen's orthogonal icosahedron, and David, a tetragonal disphenoid, two Goliaths and six Davids per cubic cell; DICTO named the pair. The tiling itself is the known picture of A15 as icosahedra and tetrahedral holes; what we did not find in the sources searched is that the weight walk lands exactly on it, and the plain statement that the A15 icosahedron is Jessen's convex hull.

Paper #56 follows the walk on the twinned octet, the holes of ideal hexagonal close packing. It passes exactly through the octet, then one point where all pieces have equal volume $\sqrt2/6$, at the single root of an irreducible cubic: DICTO's Sonsam (19 corners, one face a nine-sided polygon lying on the mirror plane) and Lahdeli (12 corners). Then comes a stop of Ian Durys and $60^\circ$ rhombohedra (volumes $13\sqrt2/54$ and $\sqrt2/54$), and the walk ends in Blockheads alone ($\sqrt2/4$). On the cubic octet the same walk goes from the octet straight to cubes, with Samson and Delilah equal at $\lambda = 3/8 - \sqrt[3]{4}/4$. Keys change the pieces but never the web.

The keyed switch points of #56 also take in the space filling of paper #42, the DICTO Sunstar, in which regular dodecahedra and Dogstars fill space: the dodecahedron is a cube with six roofs added, and the Dogstar is the same cube with its neighbours' roofs taken away.

## 4. Every stacking: #40 → #57

*The cubic and hexagonal stackings are only two of endlessly many; the rule of halves covers them all.*

Paper #40 was an early signpost: two short rules, the twin rule and the fit rule, place DICTO's Ogdoecontahedron and its family in the tetrahedral–octahedral honeycomb stacked in any order, with one exact kit for the octet, the gyrated octet, the classical polytypes and a Fibonacci stacking that never repeats.

Paper #57, the Rule of Halves, takes the weight walk of #51, #53 and #56 onto every close-packed stacking at once and proves the main theorem above. A tetrahedral gap always takes the piece it would have in the pure stacking of the layer it sits closest to; an octahedral gap, half-way between two layers, takes a top half and a bottom half, each decided by the layer on its side. So every stacking, of any length or pattern, such as ABAC or ABCACB, is built from the two pure ones, and the kinds of piece are finite; #57 lists them all with exact volumes. Where a cubic layer meets a hexagonal one, octahedral pieces appear that are half cubic and half hexagonal: the Janus c/h pieces, which DICTO named Chucky and Fang.

## Outlook

Two pieces of work grow from the rule of halves; both are plans, not results. The first is DICTO's Janus kit: printed pieces, tetrahedral pieces and octahedral halves joined at the mid-plane, from which any close-packed stacking can be built by hand. The second is DICTO's essay *Quanta of Space*, which will take the rule of halves as its new centre: every close-packed stacking built from a finite catalogue of pieces.
