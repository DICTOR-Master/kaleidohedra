Loops and Polygons on Close-Packed Stackings
What Is Known, What Is Machine-Checked in Lean, and What Is Open
Corollary A, the Flip Formula and Theorem 5 (a) of #102

For polygons crossing each bilayer at most twice, every site of every close-packed stacking has the same number of each length; flipping one bilayer changes the count of all polygons by a finite sum counted before the flip; and up to length 17 every site of every stacking has as many polygons of each length as diamond. All proved in Lean 4 with Mathlib.

1. Introduction

This paper has two purposes. The first is to compose, in one place, all that is known and all that is still unknown about counting closed loops and polygons on close-packed stackings. The second follows from the first: on investigation, less appears to be known than actually is, because the results lie scattered across physics, chemistry and combinatorics, under different names, and are hard to correlate. We correlate them, show where they are the same statement, and bring them under one umbrella of Lean proofs. Each statement is marked as one of four kinds: proved and machine-checked here; proved elsewhere and not yet machine-checked; a new question; or open in the field.

What is known, what is new. Thorpe (1972) showed that closed walks are equally many on periodic polytype structures [5]. Paper #102 proved the Loop Equality for every stacking, periodic or not, and checked it in Lean [1]: every site of every close-packed stacking has the same number of closed non-backtracking loops of each length. Version 6 of #102 added Theorem A, a half-turn surgery across one bilayer, and Corollary A: polygons that cross each bilayer at most twice are equally many in every stacking [1, §5]. New here: complete Lean 4 / Mathlib proofs of Corollary A, for every pair of stackings and for every site against diamond's site, with finiteness and a witness that the counts are not zero; of Theorem B of #102 in counting form, the flip formula, with the facts it rests on (a polygon is one cycle; a polygon is two linkages joined at the cut); and of Theorem 5 (a) of #102: at every site of every stacking the polygons of each length up to 17 are as many as at a site of diamond (#102 states it to 16; length 17 is odd). All are in a Palomar entry checked by three independent kernels. The questions answered: is the count of such polygons through a site the same at every site of every stacking, is the change under one flip a finite sum computed before the flip, and do all polygons up to length 16 agree with diamond's, by proofs a computer can check? Yes, yes and yes (Theorems 8, 9, 13, 14 and 21).

Contributions: (1) the formal statements and proofs of Lemmas 1 and 2 of the surgery, the cut lemma and the parity lemma (Section 3); (2) Theorem A for polygons crossing a bilayer at most twice, as a bijection that keeps length and crossing edges (Section 3); (3) Corollary A across one flip, by the mirror, along any run of flips, with locality, for every pair of stackings and at every site, with finiteness and a witness (Section 4); (4) the degree lemma, the product structure, the flip transfer and Theorem B in counting form (Section 5); (5) the minimal-size lemma, the cancellation of the shortest root linkages at every length, the locality of the far asymmetry for four crossing points and the regrouping by translation class, with which Theorem 5 (a) to length 17 follows from 1,507 small kernel evaluations (Section 5); (6) the package in the format of the Palomar registry, with its checks (Section 6); (7) a correlation of the known results, their names and their OEIS entries, with the Lean status of each (Section 8).

2. The network and its polygons

We use the definitions of #102 as they stand in its Lean package [1, 9]. A point of the plane is a pair (i, j) of integers standing for (i a₁ + j a₂)/3, with a₁, a₂ unit vectors at 60°. The positions A, B, C are the residues 0, 1, 2 mod 3, and a point has position c when i ≡ j ≡ c. A stacking is a map σ : ℤ → ZMod 3 with σ(k+1) ≠ σ(k). Bilayer k has a lower atom (k, p, 0) and an upper atom (k, p, 1) at every point p of position σ(k); these are the sites. Each lower atom is bonded to the upper atom above it (the vertical bond) and each upper atom to the three lower atoms of the next bilayer at distance 1/√3. Bilayer k is hexagonal (h) when σ(k−1) = σ(k+1), and cubic (c) otherwise.

An edge set is a finite set of unordered pairs of atoms. A polygon is a nonempty finite set of bonds in which every atom has even degree, and no nonempty proper subset has that property: a minimal even edge set, which is a cycle. The cut at bilayer k separates the far half (the upper atom of bilayer k and everything above) from the root half; an edge crosses it when one end lies on each side. In Lean: IsPolygon, IsAbove, Crosses, crossings, and the set polysAtMostTwice σ v l of polygons through v with l edges that cross every bilayer at most twice.

A linkage with ends B is an edge set with degree 1 at each atom of B, degree 0 or 2 elsewhere, and no nonempty subset in which every degree is even (IsLinkage). At the cut of bilayer k, a set P of crossing positions has the lower atoms (k, p, 0) on the root side and the upper atoms (k, p, 1) on the far side; a root linkage and a far linkage ending there pair up the points of P, each by the paths it contains. The two pairings close into one cycle when no nonempty proper subset of P is closed under both (OneCycle).

3. The surgery

Flipping the c/h letter of one bilayer is the same as turning everything above it half a turn. A polygon that crosses that bilayer twice can be turned with it, by the half-turn that swaps its two crossing points.

Lemma 1 (one flip is one twist; flip_is_twist, flipAt_hex). Let flipAt σ k swap the letters above bilayer k by the map that fixes σ(k) and exchanges the other two. It is a stacking whose c/h letter differs from that of σ at bilayer k and nowhere else. The half-turn p ↦ c − p of the far half, with c ≡ 2σ(k) in both coordinates mod 3, carries the sites of σ onto those of flipAt σ k, fixes the root half, and within each half keeps bonds and non-bonds.

Lemma 2 (halfturn_two_points). Two points p ≠ q of one position are exchanged by the half-turn about their midpoint, and its centre p + q satisfies the centre condition.

Lemma 3 (the cut; cut). A bond from the far half to the root half at bilayer k is the vertical bond of bilayer k at one point.

Lemma 4 (parity; even_card_cross). A loopless edge set in which every atom has even degree crosses every cut an even number of times.

Theorem 5 (Theorem A of #102, at most two crossings; surgery_polygon, bijOn_two_crossings). Let E be a polygon crossing bilayer k at most twice, and let the half-turn about c carry its crossing edges onto themselves. Keep its root edges and crossing edges and turn its far edges. The result is a polygon of flipAt σ k with the same number of edges and the same crossing edges. For two crossing points p ≠ q and c = p + q, this is a bijection between the polygons through a root atom v with those crossing edges in the two networks.

Proof in outline. Degrees: root atoms keep their edges; a far atom has after the surgery the degree its half-turned image had before, because the crossing edges are carried onto themselves (degS_surg_root, degS_surg_far). So the result is even at every atom, and its edges are bonds by Lemma 1 and Lemma 3. Minimality: a smaller even subset of the result would have 0 or 2 crossing edges by Lemma 4. These are carried onto themselves, so the same surgery takes it back to a smaller even subset of E, which is impossible. The surgery undoes itself, which gives the bijection. ∎

4. From one flip to every stacking

Theorem 6 (Corollary A across one flip; ncard_polysA1_flip). If v lies in the root half of bilayer k, then σ and flipAt σ k have equally many polygons through v with l edges crossing every bilayer at most twice, for every l.

The half-turn is taken about the midpoint of each polygon's own two crossing points at k; a polygon that does not cross k is kept as it is. The surgery keeps the crossing number at every bilayer (crossN_surg), so the condition "at most twice" is kept too.

Four further steps reach every stacking. The mirror (bilayer j ↦ −j, lower and upper exchanged) keeps the counts and turns a flip below v into a flip above (ncard_polysA1_mirror, ncard_polysA1_flipBelow). Any finite run of flips keeps the counts, and flips the c/h letter of each bilayer once for each time it is named (ncard_polysA1_flipRun, flipRun_hex). Locality: a polygon through v that reaches the far side of a cut must cross it, by minimality, and different cuts are crossed by different edges, so a polygon with l edges stays within l + 1 bilayers of v; two stackings that agree there have the same such polygons (must_cross, reach_bound, polysA1_local). Finally a relabelling of the positions that fixes the letter of v's bilayer, and a lattice translation back to v, keep the counts (ncard_polysA1_relabel, ncard_polysA1_translate).

Theorem 7 (Corollary A of #102, every stacking; corollaryA1). For any two stackings σ, τ and an atom v that is a site of both, the number of polygons through v with l edges crossing every bilayer at most twice is the same, for every l.

Proof in outline. Flip exactly the c/h letters in which σ and τ differ within l + 1 bilayers of v; the counts do not change. The result has the letter of v's bilayer and the c/h word of τ there. If its next letter differs from τ's, relabel the two other positions and translate back to v. Two stackings with the same letters at two neighbouring bilayers and the same c/h word agree on the whole window (eq_window), and locality finishes the proof. ∎

Theorem 8 (every site; corollaryA_everySite, polysAtMostTwice_finite, polysAtMostTwice_diamond_six_pos). For every stacking σ and every site v of it, the polygons through v with l edges crossing every bilayer at most twice are finitely many and as many as at diamond's site (0, (0, 0), 0), for every l. At l = 6 there is at least one: the hexagon of the puckered layer between bilayers −1 and 0.

Theorem 7 compares two stackings at one atom that is a site of both. Theorem 8 compares any site with one fixed site of diamond: a shift of the bilayers, a translation of the plane and the mirror carry every site to an atom that is also a site of diamond, without changing the counts (ncard_polysA1_shift, ncard_polysA1_pos, ncard_polysA1_mir). Finiteness holds because a polygon with l edges stays within 2l of its atom in every coordinate (polygon_in_box). The witness is an explicit polygon, checked bond by bond (hexE_polygon).

Theorem 9 (Corollary A of #102, profile by profile; corollaryA_profile, corollaryA_everySite_profile). Fix a crossing profile π, the number of crossings at each bilayer, every entry at most 2. Theorems 7 and 8 hold for the polygons with exactly that profile: for any two stackings at an atom that is a site of both, and at every site against diamond's site, with the profile read from the site's bilayer (upward from a lower atom, downward from an upper one; profileFrom).

Every step of Theorems 7 and 8 matches polygons with the same crossing numbers bilayer by bilayer: the surgery, relabelling and translation keep them, the shift moves them with the bilayers, and the mirror reads them downward (crossN_surg, crossN_htS, crossN_trE, crossN_shift, crossN_mirE). So the proofs run unchanged with any condition on the crossing numbers that keeps them at most 2 (PolysQ, corollaryQ); a fixed profile is one such condition. Entries of 1 give no polygons, by Lemma 4, so this is #102's statement with entries 0 or 2.

5. Polygons as cycles, and the flip formula

Cut a polygon at one bilayer and it falls into two linkages, one on each side, joined by its vertical bonds. Flip the bilayer, and the far linkage is turned half a turn: so the polygons after the flip can be counted from linkages before it.

Lemma 10 (a polygon is one cycle; polygon_deg_le_two). Every atom of a polygon has degree 0 or 2.

Proof in outline. Remove one edge s(x, y). Its ends are still joined, by parity in the component of x; every atom is still joined to x, or the edges at the component of x, with the removed edge, would be a smaller even set; and no cycle remains, because a cycle's edges would be a smaller even set. So what remains is a tree, with one edge fewer than the polygon has atoms; the polygon has as many edges as atoms, and as every degree is even and positive, every degree is 2. ∎

Theorem 11 (product structure; parts_to_polygon, polygon_to_parts). A root linkage ending at the lower atoms of P, the vertical bonds at P, and a far linkage ending at the upper atoms of P form a polygon if their pairings close into one cycle. Conversely every polygon crossing bilayer k at the points P splits so, uniquely.

Proof in outline. Every degree of the union is 0 or 2. A nonempty even subset contains, with any edge at an atom, every edge at that atom (the degree is at most 2), so it contains whole paths; the crossing points it reaches are closed under both pairings, hence are all of P, and it is the whole union (propagate). Conversely a smaller set of crossing points closed under both pairings would carry a smaller even subset of the polygon. ∎

Lemma 12 (flip transfer; flip_transfer). For a centre c ≡ 2σ(k) in both coordinates mod 3, the half-turn p ↦ c − p carries the far linkages of σ ending at the upper atoms of P onto the far linkages of flipAt σ k ending at those of c − P, with the same number of edges and the same pairing; the root linkages are the same in both.

Theorem 13 (Theorem B of #102, counting form; theoremB). Let v lie in the root half of bilayer k and c ≡ 2σ(k). The number of polygons through v with l edges in flipAt σ k equals the number in σ plus the sum, over the nonempty sets P of crossing positions within 2l of v whose vertical bonds are bonds, of #flipPairs(P) − #stayPairs(P). Here stayPairs(P) are the pairs (root linkage through v ending at P, far linkage ending at P) of σ that close into one cycle, with l edges in all; flipPairs(P) are the pairs whose far linkage ends at c − P and closes the root linkage into one cycle once half-turned. Every term is counted in σ alone.

Proof in outline. Sort the polygons by their crossing set at k (ncard_polys_sum). Those that do not cross lie on the root side and are the same in both stackings (polysCross_empty_flip). For the others, Theorem 11 counts the polygons of each stacking crossing at P as pairs of linkages (ncard_polysCross), and Lemma 12 turns the far linkages of flipAt σ k into far linkages of σ (ncard_partsCross_flip). ∎

Theorem 14 (Theorem B of #102, in its own form; theoremB_symmetric). Let g be the half-turn p ↦ c − p of crossing points and pairs. For each crossing set P, pairing M of its points by a root linkage and size r, let W(P, M, r) count the root linkages through v, and F(P, M′, s) the far linkages, with those ends, pairing and size; let Aw(P, M, r) = W(P, M, r) − W(gP, gM, r) and Af(P, M′, s) = F(P, M′, s) − F(gP, gM′, s). Then 2 · (pl(v; flipAt σ k) − pl(v; σ)) = − Σ Aw(P, M, r) · Af(P, M′, s), over the crossing sets of a finite family closed under g and the pairings M, M′ that close into one cycle with r + |P| + s = l. This is #102's Δ = −½ Σ Aroot · Afar, in whole numbers.

Proof in outline. Split each count of Theorem 13 by the two pairings and the two sizes: the pairs of linkages with given pairings and sizes are a product of two sets (ncard_genParts), so each count is Σ W · F, and the change at P is − Σ W(P, M, r) · Af(P, M′, s) (change_at). Extend the sum to a family of crossing sets closed under g; the added terms vanish, because a polygon crossing at P lies in the window (change_zero). Reindexing by g turns Af into −Af (Af_g, sum_g), and adding the two forms of the sum gives the asymmetry of W. ∎

Lemma 15 (#102, Lemma 7, first part; term_zero_of_large). A linkage none of whose edges joins two of its ends has at least as many edges as ends; on either side of the cut, no two crossing atoms are bonded. So in Theorem 14 every term whose crossing set has more than l / 3 points is zero; for l ≤ 20 only crossing sets of at most 6 points contribute.

Theorem 16 (#102, Theorem 5 (a), for short polygons; theorem5a_short). For every stacking and every site v of it, the polygons through v with l ≤ 11 edges are as many, length by length, as at diamond's site.

Proof in outline. By Theorem 11 and Lemma 15, a polygon crossing a bilayer n times has at least 3n edges (three_crossN_le); by Lemma 4, n is even. So with at most 11 edges it crosses every bilayer at most twice (polys_eq_polysA1), and Theorem 8 applies. ∎

Beyond length 11 a polygon can cross one bilayer four times, and Theorem B is needed. Its terms are products of a root count and a far asymmetry; the next four results show which terms vanish, which cancel, and how the rest become a finite computation.

Lemma 17 (minimal size; Af_min). A far linkage with as many edges as ends consists of arcs of two edges, end – middle – end. Moving each middle atom m to p + q − m, the centre of the other triangle on its two ends p, q, is a bijection onto the minimal far linkages of the flipped stacking with the same ends and pairing. So the far asymmetry of Theorem 14 is zero at minimal size.

Lemma 18 (shortest root linkages; minRoot_zero). At every length, the terms of Theorem 14 whose root linkage has as many edges as crossing points sum to zero.

Proof in outline. Group these root linkages by the centre c′ of the arc through v: twice v's point if v is an end, the sum of the two ends if v is a middle atom. Read through the mirror, the root side is a far side, and the half-turn about c′ followed by the middle swap of Lemma 17 maps the linkages of one group onto those of the half-turned crossing set, fixing v and its centre. The far asymmetry changes sign under every good half-turn (far counts are invariant under lattice translations), so the terms cancel in pairs. ∎

Lemma 19 (locality for four crossing points; Af_four). For crossing points paired a b | e f, the far count is the sum, over the sizes, of the products of the single-arc counts from a to b and from e to f, less the pairs of such arcs with an atom in common. The products are unchanged by the half-turn, so the far asymmetry is the change in the number of meeting pairs.

This rests on two structural facts proved on the way: a linkage on two ends is the edge set of exactly one simple path (arc_to_path, path_unique), and a linkage on four points is two arcs with no atom in common (fc_four). With them, every count is a count of explicit lists of paths, produced by a search that is proved to list every simple far path exactly once (mem_ext, ext_nodup).

Lemma 20 (regrouping by translation class; sum16_regroup, sum_through_eq_weighted). The sum over crossing sets is a sum over one representative of each class under lattice translations, with v moved by the lattice instead; and summing a class's terms over the translates of v counts each root linkage once for every atom of it of v's kind (bilayer and sublayer). This is the weighted root side of #102.

Theorem 21 (#102, Theorem 5 (a), to length 17; theorem5a_upto17). For every stacking and every site v of it, the polygons through v with l ≤ 17 edges are as many, length by length, as at diamond's site.

Proof in outline. Polygons have even length (theorem5a_odd), and up to length 11 Theorem 16 applies. It is enough that one flip keeps the count at each even length, since Theorem 7's assembly then carries diamond to every stacking (theorem5a_of_law). At length 12 every term of Theorem 14 vanishes, by Lemma 15, single arcs and Lemma 17. At length 14 only terms with a shortest root linkage remain, and they cancel by Lemma 18. At length 16, after Lemma 18, only four crossing points with six edges on each side remain. Such linkages are arcs of two and four edges and stay in the two bilayers next to the cut (arc_layer), so the terms depend only on the letters of bilayers k − 1, k, k + 1 (G16_congr). By Lemmas 19 and 20 they become, for each pattern of these letters, a finite sum over 17 classes of crossing sets, each class weighted by root counts and meeting pairs. Lean's kernel evaluates these sums in 1,507 small checks (decide +kernel, one shape or one piece each, so that none needs much memory): the list of classes misses no crossing set with a non-zero term (G16_complete), and the sum is zero for every pattern and every kind of v. ∎

Theorem 5 (a) of #102 also gives diamond's numbers (12, 24, 240, 1128, 8148, 50016 for l = 6, 8, …, 16); these numbers are not part of the Lean statement, which compares every site with diamond's.

7. Scope

Corollary A is formalised in full, in aggregate and profile by profile (Theorems 7–9), Theorem B in both its forms (Theorems 13 and 14), the first part of Lemma 7 (Lemma 15), the locality of the far asymmetry for four crossing points (Lemma 19), and Theorem 5 (a) to length 17 (Theorem 21). Not formalised in this version: Theorem A for four or more crossings (its symmetric cases in #102), the diamond numbers themselves, and Theorem 5 (b), the laws at lengths 18 and 20. The method of Theorem 21 applies to them, but at length 18 the far side has 131 classes of crossing sets with meeting arcs instead of 17, and the kernel checks would not fit the registry's time budget as they are; a faster encoding is the next step.

(a) Closed by others, and formalised here. Thorpe's equality of closed walks on periodic polytypes [5] is the periodic case of the closed-walk theorem W_closed of the Loop Equality package [1], proved there for every stacking. As far as we know this is its first formal proof: we searched Mathlib, the formal-conjectures and atlas-lean collections and Lean code on GitHub, and found Lean definitions of the fcc, hcp and diamond bond graphs in physlib [18] and Mathlib's general count of walks by powers of the adjacency matrix, but no walk counts on these lattices.

(b) Closed by others, not formalised. Diamond's polygon counts of Guttmann and Jensen [6], the constants that #102 Theorem 5 starts from; the self-avoiding walk counts of Herrero on the ice networks [7]; the enumeration of Benito et al. [8], by which self-avoiding walks on hcp and fcc agree up to 5 steps and first differ at 6 (1,573,728 against 1,573,716); the results of Grigorchuk and Żuk and of Lenz, Pogorzelski and Schmidt on zeta functions of infinite graphs [10, 11], for each stacking.

(c) New questions raised here. Is there an exact local law for open self-avoiding walks, on ice and on the sphere packings, as #102 gives for polygons? Does the local law fix the difference in residual entropy between hexagonal and cubic ice to all orders of Nagle's series [4]? Which statements about the per-site zeta function hold uniformly over all stackings, beyond what the Loop Equality gives at once?

(d) Long-standing open problems that this setting touches. The exact connective constant of the diamond lattice [14, 15]; the exact residual entropy of ice [17, 13]; whether hexagonal and cubic ice have equal residual entropy [12, 13]. #102 decides none of them.

The OEIS trail. The same facts appear in several OEIS entries that do not point to one another [19]. A002895 (the Domb numbers) counts closed walks at a site of diamond; by W_closed these are the counts at every atom of every stacking, and closedWalks_domb_small checks Domb's closed form against them for n < 10. A002899 counts the closed walks at a sphere of fcc; Thorpe's equality and #102 make them the counts at every sphere of every close-packed stacking (proved in #102, not machine-checked). A402231 is its fcc analogue (#102 Corollary 3, not machine-checked; the general formula Graph.loops_ncard_eq_FD applies once the closed-walk counts are known). A038515 lists closed self-avoiding walks on diamond, 6, 12, 120, 564, 4074, 25008, 174618, 1181100 for lengths 6 to 20; the polygons through an atom of diamond are exactly twice these, 12, 24, 240, 1128, 8148, 50016 to length 16 (the normalisation of #102, whose enumerations reproduce them; not machine-checked). Theorems 7, 8, 9, 13, 14 and 21 are statements about these polygons, and by Theorem 21 every atom of every stacking has diamond's counts up to length 17. A288457 and A288461, the Chebyshev coefficients of the densities of states of diamond and fcc, and A293237, the escape probability of the random walk on fcc, are fixed by the closed-walk counts at a site: the first two as their own comments say, the third through the escape probability 1/Σ Wk/12k. So they are the same at every site of every stacking: for diamond (A288457) from W_closed, for fcc (A288461, A293237) from the closed-walk equality for the sphere packings of #102, which is not machine-checked; the one-line step itself is not machine-checked either.

9. Conclusion

The half-turn surgery of #102 is now a machine-checked proof: for polygons that cross each bilayer at most twice, every site of every close-packed stacking has the same count of each length, and these counts are finite and not all zero. Theorem B of #102 is machine-checked in counting form: one flip changes the count of all polygons by a finite sum counted before the flip. Theorem 5 (a) of #102 is machine-checked to length 17: up to there, every site of every stacking has diamond's number of polygons of each length. The package is in the registry's format and passes its checks. The contributions are the formal Lemmas 1–4, 9 and 11, Theorem 5 as a bijection, Theorems 6–8 and 10, Theorem 13, Lemmas 17–20 and Theorem 21, the checked package, and the correlation of the known results with their names, their OEIS entries and their Lean status.
