Opening

Quanta of Space

Is space smooth all the way down, like water? Or is it built from pieces, the way a wall is built from stones? People have asked this for two and a half thousand years. This book takes the question literally. It looks for exact sets of solid pieces that fit together with no gaps, into shells, into cycles and into the whole of space.

What follows is a family tree of an idea, not a claim about physics. The pieces in this book are shapes you could build and hold. They share a question with the old thinkers and with modern science: what are the smallest pieces of space, and how do they join?

Atoms and the empty space between them

In ancient Greece, Leucippus and Democritus taught that the world is made of tiny pieces that cannot be cut, the atoms, moving through empty space. The atoms differ only in their shape, their arrangement and their position. The Roman poet Lucretius put the teaching into verse: “nothing exists per se except atoms and the void”. So the atoms were pieces. But no one said what shapes they had. Plato gave them shapes, Aristotle asked that they fill space, and Kepler asked how equal units pack; their story opens Part Four.

The quanta of modern science

In 1900 Max Planck found that energy comes in small fixed amounts, quanta. In one modern theory of gravity, called loop quantum gravity, space itself does too. In 1995 Carlo Rovelli and Lee Smolin showed that in this theory area and volume come in separate steps, not smoothly. In 2011 Eugenio Bianchi, Pietro Doná and Simone Speziale showed that each small amount of volume can be read as a little solid with flat faces, fixed by the sizes and directions of its faces. Space becomes a network of such solids, joined face to face. The phrase “quanta of space” itself comes from this theory (Rovelli and Upadhya, 1998).

There is an honest difference. In that theory two neighbouring pieces share a face of the same area, but in general not of the same shape (Freidel and Speziale, 2010). Their pieces need not fit. Our pieces always fit exactly, corner to corner. Other approaches, such as causal sets, build space and time from points rather than from solids.

So this book makes no claim about physics. It shares the question and the form: what are the smallest exact pieces from which space can be built, and how do they join?

Five orders

DICTO's answer is given here as seven postulates, and the works that bear them out fall into five orders, one for each of the five Platonic solids of his name, taken in the order of the series: the cube, the icosahedron, the tetrahedron, the octahedron and the dodecahedron. Each order is a part of this book. Each part states its principle in a few lines, then introduces the books of that order in number order, with the works each book develops, three to five lines to a work, and a table of its works at the end. In the cube order pieces fill space. In the icosahedron order they join in bonds and close in golden shells and cycles. In the tetrahedron order every close packing comes down to a finite catalogue of pieces that change only by swapping. In the octahedron order the five solids of the old schools are taken apart. In the dodecahedron order the same pieces reach beyond three dimensions, and come back. The book ends on the road through the cycle to its last book, A Brief Geometry of Space.

Book Two. Space-Fillers and Lattice Moves

Which solids fill space alone, which fill it only together, and which hold by shape alone.

#13The DICTO Dodeca-13. Twelve dodecahedra round one touch only along the edges of the centre and leave thin gaps of about ten degrees where three meet. Thirty wedges and twenty needles fill the gaps exactly: 63 pieces in one closed solid, which comes apart into the bare centre and twelve finned units. Its best stacking by sliding alone is body-centred cubic. Shown in Book Two, Space-Fillers and Lattice Moves (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24221379).

#14The DICTO-Star. An icosidodecahedron, 12 dodecahedra and 20 cut icosahedra close exactly round every edge, with no filler piece; two stars can share a dodecahedron, but no lattice of stars fits. Told in its paper (#14, DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23284213), with its entry in Volume II.

#17Kagome Hulls. Clusters that share corners follow one rule: a hull with a given number of corners sits on a network where each cluster has that many neighbours, four for the diamond network, six for the simple cubic, eight for the body-centred cubic, twelve for the face-centred cubic. The Dodeca-13 is the exception: twelve corners, at most three neighbours, never a lattice, where icosahedral symmetry meets periodic space. Developed in Book Two, Space-Fillers and Lattice Moves (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24221379).

#18The DICTO Hexa. Eight DICTO Jewels on a two-by-two-by-two block, passing through each other inside, make one cube with 24 diamond windows. The space between Hexas is one solid, which DICTO named the Hexa-Key, and Hexas and Hexa-Keys fill space like a checkerboard, five parts to three by volume, as Jewels and stellas do one level down. Developed in Book Two, Space-Fillers and Lattice Moves (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24221379).

#25DICTO Spheroid-13 RD. A rhombic dodecahedron with its twelve face neighbours fills space by sliding alone, in exactly 8 ways, all one arrangement turned. Rounded, with every face flat and every piece convex, the block is nearly as round as one rhombic dodecahedron; its centres are islands that would fill space on their own, so the block holds two space-fillings, one inside the other. Developed in Book Two, Space-Fillers and Lattice Moves (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24221379).

#26DICTO ATOM-13×13. Thirteen clusters of twelve round one, side by side, with 24 kits of 13 to 213 pieces that fill between them, every one checked piece by piece. Two golden clusters, the Lotus seed and the Bodhi seed, close exactly into larger copies of themselves, and every periodic block fills space by sliding alone: seven shapes wearing one tiling. Developed in Book Two, Space-Fillers and Lattice Moves (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24221379).

#36Golden Bodhi Pyrochlore. The gap between packed DICTO Ogdoecontahedra is filled by one piece, DICTO's Bull-horns OG adaptor, so the whole packing is built from two shapes. Shown in Book Two, Space-Fillers and Lattice Moves (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24221379); told in its paper (#36, DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23451210), with its entry in Volume II.

#41The DICTO Jewel. Euclid's dodecahedron, carved by the stella octangulas of its six neighbouring cells, keeps the whole cube and twelve thick Penrose rhombi as windows. Jewels on the white cubes and stellas on the black fill space exactly, three parts to one, in the pattern of table salt. DICTO's clusters, the Jewel-Tetra, Jewel-Octa and Jewel-Rhombi, become single solids that fill space with loose stars, and the Hexa is proved here. Developed in Book Two, Space-Fillers and Lattice Moves (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24221379).

#42The DICTO Sunstar. The Jewel's twin: regular dodecahedra on alternate cubes and Dogstars* between them fill space. Each Dogstar holds a whole Sunstar, smaller by a golden factor, which holds the next, for ever: DICTO's Star Chain Reaction. The Sunstar clusters fill space in exactly the ways the Jewel clusters do. Developed in Book Two, Space-Fillers and Lattice Moves (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24221379).

* Dodecahedron stellation 8 (Hart, 1996). Rediscovered by DICTO as the Dogstar.

#47Every Mix of the Platonic Solids Fills Space. Choose any of the five Platonic solids, one, several or all, at one edge length: they pack with no gaps using one fixed kit of seven helper pieces, by a layer cake of slabs with flat tops and bottoms. The cube alone, the tetrahedron with the octahedron, and these three together need no helper; every mix with the dodecahedron or the icosahedron needs one. Developed in Book Two, Space-Fillers and Lattice Moves (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24221379).

#49The Polyhedral Stretching Theorem. Slide any solid with equal edges one edge-length in a straight line, in any direction but along one of its own edges, and every edge of the shape it sweeps is one edge-length long: DICTO's stretched solids. Told in its paper (#49, DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23566671), with its entry in Volume II.

#50One Move Each. Three moves of the Kaleidohedra app, a shear, the equal-edged cell and a stretch, give five classical space-fillers exactly, each at one setting: the Bain rhombic dodecahedron, the regular-hexagon elongated dodecahedron*, the classical elongated dodecahedron, Bilinski's golden dodecahedron and the 60° rhombohedron. A rhombic dodecahedron can be stretched into an elongated dodecahedron that still fills space in exactly three directions. Developed in Book Two, Space-Fillers and Lattice Moves (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24221379).

* Fedorov's elongated dodecahedron with regular hexagons (Fedorov, 1885). Rediscovered by DICTO as the regular-hexagon elongated dodecahedron.

Part Two

Part Two · The icosahedron order (blue)

Pieces join in bonds that hand over along a line; the golden ratio decides when shells close and cycles return; star and core turn into each other. The icosahedron fills no space on its own, so its order follows the pieces that join it to its neighbours and the shells that close round it.

Book Three. DICTONIC Bonds

Pairs of pieces that fill space together, and the web of shared faces between neighbouring pieces.

#37The DICTO Ogdoecontahedron on the Octet Truss. Four families of exact kits put the DICTO Ogdoecontahedron (OG) in the octahedra of the octet: the golden-key octet; the Penrose-core octet, whose middle piece holds three Penrose cores; golden envelopes, which fill 55.73 per cent of space and leave one piece; and one glue piece whose faces are imprints of the OG, so the OG and the glue fill space, two shapes, the OG whole. Shown in Book Three, DICTONIC Bonds (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24091643).

#37aThe DICTO Ogdoecontahedron on the Gyrated Octet. Across each layer of the gyrated octet the OG meets its twin, the OG turned 60°. Cut each octahedron into the OG and eight caps, keep the tetrahedra whole, and one kit fills the octet, the gyrated octet and every stacking between. DICTO's cut solids of the golden envelope, the DICTO Belted Triacontahedron (DBT) and the DICTO Octahedrally Truncated Triacontahedron (OTT), fit as well. Developed in Book Three, DICTONIC Bonds (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24091643).

#40The DICTO OG Family on Every Stacking. Two short rules: a solid's mirror image across a layer is the same solid turned a sixth of a turn (the twin rule), and a solid that stays inside its own layer fits every stacking, whatever the word (the fit rule). So the OG, the OTT, the OCD Stella and the DBT fit every stacking, the Fibonacci stacking too, where the pieces per cell come out as golden numbers: a crystal periodic in two directions that never repeats in the third. Developed in Book Three, DICTONIC Bonds (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24091643).

#48The Golden Split. Cut each AXE* between the icosahedra and the Unborns** on a cubic grid into four pyramids from one inner point, and give each pyramid to the piece it touches. At exactly one point, the golden point, nothing sticks out: the two become truncated octahedra, the Icosaxes*** and the Unborn Axes****, which fill space together. In DICTO's Lotus Jewel network the same cut gives the keyed pair, the Lotus Jewel Axes and the Dodecaxes. Developed in Book Three, DICTONIC Bonds (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24091643).

* Mosseri–Sadoc tile t6, scaled by 1/φ (Mosseri and Sadoc, 1982). Rediscovered by DICTO as AXE. ** The pseudoicosahedron (Koca et al., 2015). DICTO's name: Unborn. *** The truncated octahedron x = φ (Koca et al., 2015). DICTO's name: Icosaxes. **** The truncated octahedron x = 1/φ (Koca et al., 2015). DICTO's name: Unborn Axes.

#51The Delian Bond. One flat cut slid through the double cones of the octet runs from Kepler's rhombic dodecahedra through the octet to cubes, two convex solids filling space at every position. At exactly one position the two have the same volume: Samson, a cube with four alternate corners cut deep, and the regular octahedron*, one twice the octet's tetrahedron and the other half its octahedron, the Delian problem of doubling in both directions at once. Developed in Book Three, DICTONIC Bonds (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24091643).

* Named Delilah in DICTO's books: DICTO named the pair Samson and Delilah, from “Delian”.

—The DICTONIC SOLIDS and BONDS. Papers #48 and #51 together give six solids that fill space in three pairs, which DICTO calls the DICTONIC SOLIDS and the DICTONIC BONDS: the golden bond (the Icosaxes and the Unborn Axes), the keyed bond (the Lotus Jewel Axes and the Dodecaxes) and the Delian bond (Samson and the regular octahedron). A DICTONIC BOND is two solids that cannot fill space alone but fill it together; what is new in each is the bond itself, the cut that makes it and the way the pair fills space. The DICTONIC world of the Kaleidohedra app runs each bond through its family with one slider. Developed in Book Three, DICTONIC Bonds (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24091643).

#52The Web of Bonds. Both DICTONIC loops are weighted tilings of one fixed crowd of points, the caesium chloride and fluorite crystal structures, with one weight slid between two kinds, and the web of bonds, one bond per shared face, is their exact dual. The golden bonds hand over gradually; the Delian bonds switch at the octet. The weights read as squared atom sizes, the touching ranges end at Pauling's limits, and real crystals sit on the loops. Developed in Book Three, DICTONIC Bonds (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24091643).

#53The Global Web of Bonds. DICTO's idea that the hand-overs recur elsewhere, proved for ten crystal networks and the perovskite: every weight walk exactly, every switch, and the bonds on every stretch. The bond between unlike pieces can never break. Developed in Book Three, DICTONIC Bonds (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24091643).

#55David and Goliath. In the A15 structure the weight walk lands exactly, at one weight, on the hull of Jessen's orthogonal icosahedron* (Jessen, 1967) and a tetragonal disphenoid** (classical), two to six per cubic cell, filling space: the classical A15 picture of Frank and Kasper, reached by the global theorem. Developed in Book Three, DICTONIC Bonds (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24091643).

* Named Goliath in DICTO's books. ** Named David in DICTO's books.

#56Sonsam and Lahdeli. The web of bonds followed on the twinned, hexagonal octet finds its own equal-volume pair, Sonsam and Lahdeli, the mirror of the Delian bond, then Ian Dury with the 60° rhombohedron and the Blockheads; keys change the pieces, never the web. Names by DICTO. Developed in Book Three, DICTONIC Bonds (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24091643).

#58From the Octet to Every Stacking: the Web of Bonds. The series paper that tells papers #40 to #57 as one story, from DICTO's PET-bottle-cap spheres and the Polyhedraverse app to the Rule of Halves. It adds no new result; it is the map of how the octet's bonds hand over from one crystal network to the next. Developed in Book Three, DICTONIC Bonds (DICTO DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24091643).

Part Three · The tetrahedron order (red)

Every close packing is built from a finite catalogue of pieces that change only by swapping; its laws are straight lines in the share of hexagonal layers; the smallest swap, two neighbours changing places, is the unit.

The centre: the quanta of every close packing

Stack oranges in a crate and each new layer sits in the hollows of the one below, in one of two ways. Repeat one choice and the stack is cubic; repeat the other and it is hexagonal; mix them in any order and it is still a perfect packing. Nature uses many of these mixtures, in metals, ice, silicon carbide and the oxides of batteries.

DICTO's Rule of Halves (#57) shows that however the layers are stacked, periodic or not, the space between the balls is shared out into pieces of only finitely many kinds, every one borrowed whole or in half from the two pure stackings. That is a finite catalogue of quanta for every close packing there is: the clearest answer this work gives to the old question. The pieces change only by swapping (the Swap Rule, #61), so every count and every volume is a straight line in one number, the share of hexagonal layers (the Hexagonality Law, #66), and real crystals obey it. And the catalogue closes into just two tiles, one cubic and one hexagonal, that build every stacking in any order: HexOct (#75). The Law gave the pieces; the pieces prove the law.

Between the repeating and the never-repeating

Some patterns repeat, like the tiles of a bathroom floor. Others never repeat, yet are perfectly ordered, like the Penrose tiling. For most of history, order in matter meant repetition, and five-fold symmetry was thought impossible in a crystal. Then in 1982 Dan Shechtman found a metal alloy whose scattering pattern showed sharp five-fold symmetry. These quasicrystals, ordered but never repeating, earned him the Nobel Prize in 2011. Matter has two kinds of order, and the question became how they meet. The pieces of this work sit on that border: in the Fibonacci Spine (#30) and the Fibonacci stacking (#69) repeating pieces build an order that never repeats, and the two HexOct tiles build repeating and never-repeating stackings alike, by the order in which they are laid. One kit, two kinds of order: the pieces themselves are the bridge.

Book Seven. The Permutohedron Paradigm

Every possible order, followed by one move: two neighbouring values changing places.

The smallest swap is the unit of this book. Three bells, rung by swapping neighbours one at a time, draw a hexagon: the orders of three values are the truncated triangle, and the 24 orders of four values, joined by 36 swaps of three kinds, are Kelvin's cell, the one permutohedron that fills space alone. Swaps inside cells are scutoids, cells that change their neighbours from top to bottom: swept, the four-dimensional permutohedron is a scutoid tissue of 26 births, 66 swaps and 26 deaths, counted by the Eulerian numbers, and every simple four-dimensional polytope, swept, is a scutoid tissue counted by its h-vector (#104). The Swap Rule of close packing (#61) appears there one dimension up, its walls as swaps. At the summit stands the Permutohedron Family: slide the walls of the permutohedron without turning them and every edge stays a swap, in one cone of shapes from Kelvin's cell to the associahedron, whose canonical form is a scattering amplitude; in three dimensions the members that fill space by sliding are Fedorov's five parallelohedra. The book returns to the bells: ringers may only swap neighbours, so every extent is a tour of a permutohedron, and on four bells there are exactly 5396 extents. Part Four · The octahedron order (yellow)

The five solids of the old schools, taken apart with all the work has found.

Plato's kit of pieces

Plato gave the atoms shapes. In his dialogue the Timaeus, the four elements of matter are four regular solids: the icosahedron, the cube, the tetrahedron and the octahedron. All their faces are built from just two kinds of right-angled triangle. The fifth regular solid, the dodecahedron, was used “for the whole”. This was the first kit for building matter: a small set of exact pieces from which everything is put together.

Aristotle's pyramids

Aristotle replied that if atoms are solid, they must fill space with no gaps. He wrote (On the Heavens III.8) that among solids two do this: the pyramid and the cube. He did not say which pyramid. His commentators took it to be the regular tetrahedron, which on its own leaves a small gap, and argued over it for centuries. Read as he wrote it, he was right: many pyramids fill space, and #44 below proves which pyramids cut from the centres of the Platonic solids do.

Kepler's packings

In 1596 Johannes Kepler nested the five regular solids between the spheres of the planets. In 1611, in a small book on the six-cornered snowflake, he explained the snowflake by how equal small units pack together. That became the famous question of how tightly equal balls can be stacked.

Book Eleven. A Brief Geometry of Space

In progress: what follows is its agreed plan.

The last book tells DICTO's three aims as one story, in the order a reader meets them: pieces, then crystals, then the quasicrystal at the turn, then laws. It opens with the essay of this book, re-edited as its first chapter. It goes on to the pieces that fill space alone, and asks whether one piece could force the never-repeating kind of order; that question is open. It stacks the pieces into crystals, words in two letters, c and h, under the Hexagonality Law, and follows the Fibonacci stacking, the first never-repeating order made from repeating pieces. DICTO's sentence for the whole: the quasicrystal is the turning point of a line of crystals. It looks at the shadows of higher dimensions and the two Queens, and ends with a question from modern physics: can a shape hand out laws, the way one polytope, the amplituhedron, hands out the rules of particle collisions? Its thread is the swap at every level, from two neighbours changing places to a solid and its polar; a law is claimed at a swap's fixed point only where the swap forces it, and every open question is stated as open. It is written for a general reader, each theorem in words with one picture and its source, and it closes by coming back to Book One and the same pieces. Ending

The road through the cycle

The road starts here, with the oldest question about space, what are its smallest exact pieces and how do they join, and with seven postulates.

In the cube order the pieces fill space: very few alone, all five Platonic solids together with a small kit of helpers, some held by their shape alone. In the icosahedron order they join in bonds that hand over along a line, and close round a core in golden shells and cycles, star and core turning into each other. In the tetrahedron order every close packing comes down to a finite catalogue of pieces that change only by swapping, every count a straight line in one number, and the smallest swap, two neighbours changing places, becomes the unit of a whole paradigm. In the octahedron order the five solids of the old schools are taken apart: nested in one cell, cut into Aristotle's pyramids, rounded, and each cut golden in exactly two ways. In the dodecahedron order the same pieces reach beyond three dimensions, as slices and shadows of the solids of four dimensions, and come back.

Four things come back again and again. A small kit is enough: a handful of exact shapes builds shells and spaces without limit, and for close packing the kit is finite and complete, down to two tiles. The golden ratio appears wherever five-fold symmetry does, and it decides when shells close; it gives every Platonic solid exactly two golden cuts, and the same golden depths return as slices of the solids of four dimensions. The pieces join the two kinds of order in matter, the repeating and the never-repeating. And the same pieces reach beyond our space: the five pyramids that Aristotle named and his readers argued over are the cores of the regular solids of four dimensions.

The road ends at Book Eleven, A Brief Geometry of Space, which opens with this book's essay and closes on the same pieces: what is proved, what is near, and what is still open.

The ancients guessed that space was made of shaped pieces, and modern physics has come back to the idea. These works build such pieces exactly and test them, and each paper behind this book is checked exactly.

About the author

DICTO is the artist name of (Alan) J. Baker, an artist in Osaka, Japan. His background is architectural sculpture, stonecarving and Buddhist statuary. He trained as an architectural stonecarver at the City and Guilds of London Art School (1998–2001), worked at St James's Palace, and recarved the Roman lettering of the Civil Service Rifles War Memorial at Somerset House, London, for its rededication in 2002. Since 2009 he has worked in Japan in the sculpture and restoration of Buddhist statues, among them a statue at Takaosan Jingo-ji, Kyoto, traditionally attributed to Kōbō Daishi Kūkai.

He builds geometric sculpture from recycled PET bottles with two modular systems of his own, B1NR and DICTOspheres, joined without glue. He holds six Japanese design registrations; five rest on spherical projections of the Platonic solids, the five forms behind his name: Dodecahedron, Icosahedron, Cube, Tetrahedron, Octahedron. His sculptures have been shown in Kyoto at the Kyoto Municipal Museum of Art (Interdependent Origination III, 2025), the Insho Domoto Museum of Fine Arts, the Kyoto Botanical Garden, the Kansho Exhibition and the Kyoto Takasegawa Art Festival.

The geometry behind the sculptures led to the papers gathered in DICTO: Collected Geometric Works, and to the books of The Five Orders of Space, of which Quanta of Space: A Manifesto (this one) and its second volume, The DICTOnary of Atoms: Quanta of Space II, are Book One; the cycle page at the front lists them all. Every paper and book is published openly with its own DICTO DOI at Zenodo. More at https://dictospheres.com.
