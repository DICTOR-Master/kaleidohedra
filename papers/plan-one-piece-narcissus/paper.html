The aim (DICTO, 5 November). One piece, Narcissus with keys on its faces, that fills space alone and can do so only in a stacking that never repeats.

Prior art for the aim: a single three-dimensional tile with matching rules that tiles only non-periodically has been announced (Flicker 2026, preprint), and the Schmitt–Conway–Danzer prototile tiles only with a screw (weakly). What would be new here is a convex piece from the HexOct walk, keyed on its faces, whose forced order is the stacking itself.

2. The two ways round the blocks

Edge keys (Socolar–Taylor type). Both blocks come from one fact: a face key mates with exactly one face of one neighbour. A key placed on an edge where three or more pieces meet is shared, so a bump can sit in either of two neighbours. That removes the "accept either" cost (Block 2) and breaks the one-to-one mating behind the 2+2+2 against 3+3 count (Block 1). Socolar and Taylor's hexagonal monotile works this way in the plane: its rules reach second neighbours.

A different tile set. Kari and Culik sets carry numbers; other aperiodic Wang sets (Jeandel–Rao's 11 tiles, Ammann's, substitution sets) carry no carries at all. If one of them splits over a down site and an up site with at most 6 states each and without a zero-type repeating tiling, Block 1 does not arise.

DICTO's spiral key (6 November). The key runs diagonally and spirals round the faces. A stripe that crosses face edges must continue on the neighbouring piece, then the next: Socolar and Taylor's first rule (stripes continuous across edges), made physical, and a link across edges where three or more pieces meet. A single spiral is handed (the mirror piece differs, 3 placements per site instead of 6); a double spiral, one left and one right, keeps Narcissus its own mirror image and all 6 placements. The hexagon seam allows only 0°, 60° and 120°, so the spiral acts as a line that must continue, not as a screw. It becomes the concrete form of the edge key in step 3.
