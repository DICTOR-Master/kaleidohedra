The Reuleaux Platonic Solids

Balls as wide as the solid on every corner: four ball polyhedra beside the Reuleaux tetrahedron, named by their look, with their fewest face colours and their widths; dual pairs have exactly equal width ranges

The construction (a ball on every corner of a Platonic solid, radius = the solid's diameter) and the names by look (Reuleaux octahedron, cube, icosahedron and dodecahedron) are DICTO's idea; computation, proofs and drafting with Claude (Anthropic).
Abstract. DICTO's idea: put a ball on every corner of a Platonic solid, each as wide as the solid (radius = its diameter), and keep what all the balls share. On the tetrahedron this is the classical Reuleaux tetrahedron, and our numerical volume agrees with the known closed form to 1.1 × 10−8. On the other four it gives ball polyhedra which DICTO names by their look: balls on the cube's corners make the Reuleaux octahedron, on the octahedron's the Reuleaux cube, on the dodecahedron's the Reuleaux icosahedron and on the icosahedron's the Reuleaux dodecahedron. Each ball gives one face, and two faces meet along an arc exactly when their balls' centres are joined by an edge, so each body has the face pattern of the dual solid; the fewest colours with no two neighbours alike are 4, 2, 3, 3 and 4. They are not Reuleaux polyhedra in Sallee's sense: the corners are not vertices of the body, and the diametric pairs are fewer than 2m − 2. We prove that every width is at least the diameter, with equality through two opposite corners, and that along the axis through a face centre the width is exactly 2√(3/4 + r²) − 2r at circumradius ½ (r the inradius). Dual solids have the same inradius-to-circumradius ratio, so dual pairs share this width exactly: (√30 − √3)/3 ≈ 1.248392 for the Reuleaux octahedron and cube, (√(50 + 2√5) − √(5 + 2√5))/√15 ≈ 1.110989 for the Reuleaux icosahedron and dodecahedron. That the face axis gives the largest width is found numerically and not proved.

1. The construction

For a Platonic solid S with corner set X and diameter D (its widest span), DICTO's body is B = ∩v∈X B(v, D), the common part of the closed balls of radius D centred on the corners. In the language of [1, 2] it is a ball polyhedron. For the tetrahedron D is the edge and B is the Reuleaux tetrahedron [2, 3]. For the cube, octahedron, dodecahedron and icosahedron D is twice the circumradius R: the widest spans join opposite corners. Below, all lengths are at D = 1.

Every corner lies in B (no two corners are more than D apart), and the surface of B is made of one spherical face from each ball [3]: the part of the sphere of radius D about v that bounds B, lying across the body from v. DICTO named the bodies by their look, and the look is explained by the faces. The checks sample, for every pair of balls, the circle where their spheres meet, and find that two faces meet along an arc exactly when their centres are joined by an edge of S (Table 1). So the faces of B correspond to the corners of S and meet as those corners are joined, which is how the faces of the dual solid meet: the balls on the cube's 8 corners give 8 faces meeting like the octahedron's, and so on.

2. The tetrahedron check, and what is not a Reuleaux polyhedron

On the tetrahedron the construction is the classical Reuleaux tetrahedron [2, 3]. Its volume at width 1 is 8π/3 + √2/4 − (27/4) arccos(1/3) (Harbourne's formula, as quoted by Hynd [3]); our quadrature gives 0.4221577, equal to it to 1.1 × 10−8. Its widths run from 1 to 1.024944; the largest found is attained along the axis through the midpoints of two opposite edges, where the same argument as in Theorem 1 below gives exactly √3 − √2/2: the opposite edges are 1/√2 apart and every corner is ½ from that axis.

Hynd [3] and Montejano and Roldán-Pensado [2] call B(X) a Reuleaux polyhedron when X has the largest possible number of diametric pairs, 2m − 2 for m points (the Vázsonyi bound, proved by Grünbaum, Heppes and Straszewicz [3]), or, after Sallee, when the vertices of B are exactly the points of X [2]. The tetrahedron has 6 = 2 × 4 − 2. The other four do not qualify (exact checks): their diametric pairs are only the opposite corners, 4, 3, 10 and 6, against 14, 10, 38 and 22; and each corner lies on the sphere of exactly one other ball (the opposite corner's), so it is not a vertex of B, where at least three spheres meet [2]. They are ball polyhedra, not Reuleaux polyhedra, and the smoothing of edge pairs that turns a Reuleaux polyhedron into a body of constant width [2, 3] does not apply to them as it stands.

3. Widths

The width of B in a direction u is the distance between the two planes at right angles to u that touch B.

Proposition 1 (the smallest width is the diameter). For every one of the five bodies every width is at least 1, and the width along the line through any two corners at distance 1 is exactly 1.

Proof. By Pál's theorem, as cited in [2], the corner set X (of diameter 1) lies in a body K of constant width 1. K has diameter 1 and contains X, so every point of K is within 1 of every corner: K ⊂ B, and every width of B is at least 1. Now let v, w be corners with |v − w| = 1 and u = v − w. B lies in the ball of radius 1 about w, so u·x ≤ u·w + 1 = u·v on B, and v lies in B; so the largest value of u·x on B is u·v, and in the same way the smallest is u·w. The width is u·v − u·w = 1. ∎

Theorem 1 (the width along a face axis). Let S be a cube, octahedron, dodecahedron or icosahedron with circumradius ½ (diameter 1) and inradius r. Along the axis through its centre and a face centre the width of B is exactly 2√(3/4 + r²) − 2r.

Proof. Let u be the unit vector to the face centre and hv = u·v the height of a corner v. The rotations of S about the axis (3-, 4- or 5-fold) map B to itself and fix u, so they map the set of points of B where u·x is largest to itself; that set is convex, and its centroid lies on the axis. So the largest value of u·x on B is reached at a point zu of the axis. Since |v|² = ¼, |zu − v|² = ¼ + z² − 2z hv, and for z > 0 this is largest where hv is smallest. The plane of the opposite face touches S, so every corner has hv ≥ −r, with equality exactly at the corners of that face (checked exactly). Hence zu lies in B exactly when ¼ + z² + 2zr ≤ 1, that is z ≤ √(3/4 + r²) − r. S and B are centrally symmetric, so the width is twice this. ∎

Dual pairs. The width depends only on r, and r = ½ × (r/R). Dual solids have the same ratio r/R; the exact checks find (r/R)² = 1/3 for the cube and the octahedron and (5 + 2√5)/15 for the dodecahedron and the icosahedron. So the Reuleaux octahedron and the Reuleaux cube have exactly the same face-axis width, and so do the Reuleaux icosahedron and the Reuleaux dodecahedron:

  • cube, octahedron: r² = 1/12, 2√(3/4 + r²) = √30/3 and 2r = √3/3, so w = (√30 − √3)/3 ≈ 1.248392;
  • dodecahedron, icosahedron: r² = (5 + 2√5)/60, (2√(3/4 + r²))² = (50 + 2√5)/15 and (2r)² = (5 + 2√5)/15, so w = (√(50 + 2√5) − √(5 + 2√5))/√15 ≈ 1.110989.

The largest width (numerical). The width was computed without a mesh, from the exact candidate points of the support function (ball caps, the circles where two spheres meet, the points where three meet), over 3000 random directions with local refinement (widths.py). The largest widths found are 1.248391589161 for both the Reuleaux octahedron and cube and 1.110988655456 for both the Reuleaux icosahedron and dodecahedron, equal to the face-axis values of Theorem 1 to 12 digits, and 1.024944026382 = √3 − √2/2 for the tetrahedron. That the face axis (or, for the tetrahedron, the edge-midpoint axis) gives the global maximum is therefore numerical; Theorem 1 proves only the value along that axis. Since the smallest width is 1 (Proposition 1), the width ranges of each dual pair, and so their spreads (largest/smallest), are equal, given that numerical maximum.

4. Faces and colours

Because two faces meet along an arc exactly when their corners are joined by an edge of S (§1), colouring the faces so that no two neighbours share a colour is colouring the corners of S along its edges. The fewest colours, found by exhaustive search in the checks, are 4 for the Reuleaux tetrahedron (any two of its 4 faces meet), 2 for the Reuleaux octahedron (the cube's corners split into two sets with no edge inside either), 3 for the Reuleaux cube, 3 for the Reuleaux icosahedron and 4 for the Reuleaux dodecahedron, as in Figure 1. These are the numbers DICTO read from the look: 4, 2, 3, 3, 4.

5. Prior art and what is claimed

  • Known, credited: the Reuleaux tetrahedron and its volume (Harbourne, as quoted by Hynd [3]); ball polyhedra in general (Bezdek, Lángi, Naszódi and Papez [1]); Reuleaux polyhedra after Sallee [4], Meissner's solids and the Meissner polyhedra built from Reuleaux polyhedra (Montejano and Roldán-Pensado [2]); Pál's theorem (as cited in [2]); Hynd's perimeter and volume formulas for Reuleaux polyhedra, with a family of Reuleaux pyramids on regular n-gons for odd n [3]. The construction B(X) itself is the standard one of [1, 2, 3].
  • Claimed here: the four bodies on the cube, octahedron, dodecahedron and icosahedron (DICTO's idea and names), the fact that they are not Reuleaux polyhedra, their face patterns and fewest colours (the arcs found numerically), Proposition 1 for them, Theorem 1 and the equal face-axis widths of dual pairs with their closed forms, and the numerical width ranges and volumes.

The three library papers on these bodies [1, 2, 3] were searched in full for cube, octahedron, dodecahedron, icosahedron, Platonic and regular: the only regular solid they use is the regular tetrahedron or simplex (for the Reuleaux and Meissner tetrahedra, Hynd's pyramids and a lemma of [1]). Sallee's paper [4] is in the library as a link only and was not read. In the sources checked (the full text of the 81 library sources with a text layer listed in the appendix, searched on 21 October 2026; the library's 173 link-only entries, Sallee's among them, were not searched) we did not find the bodies on the other four Platonic solids or their widths.

Data and reproducibility

In studies/2026-10-21-reuleaux-platonic: reuleaux_checks.py (log reuleaux-checks.log, 45 PASS, no FAIL), in two parts. Part A is exact (ℚ(√5) with Python fractions): the diameters, diametric pairs and the corners lying on one other sphere; the corner heights along a face axis; (r/R)² of each solid; the closed forms of Theorem 1; and the tetrahedron's edge-midpoint axis.
