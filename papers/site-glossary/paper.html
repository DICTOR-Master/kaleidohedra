How to use it

  • Pair, don't replace. The real word stays; the picture comes first and the word follows: "packing a car boot with no gaps left: space-filling". The mathematics keeps its name, and the newcomer gets a way in.
  • Once per page. Give the picture the first time a word appears on a page or card, then use the word alone.
  • Lead with the object. A card opens with what you can hold or see (the piece, the stack, the fabric), then the result, then the paper number and DOI.
  • Last column: how many times the word (or its family) appears on the page today, so the most frequent words get their pictures first.

1. Shapes

TermEveryday pictureWhat it meansOn the site
polyhedronA cut gemstone; a die.A solid bounded by flat faces, straight edges and corners. Plural: polyhedra.0
face · edge · corner (vertex)A box: six faces, twelve edges, eight corners.Say corner; keep vertex in brackets the first time on a page.3
Platonic solidsThe five dice in a role-playing set (d12, d20, d6, d4, d8).The only five solids whose faces are all the same regular polygon, meeting the same way at every corner. Listed in D·I·C·T·O order below.14
dodecahedronThe 12-sided die.12 pentagons, three at each corner.51
icosahedronThe 20-sided die; the shell of many viruses.20 triangles, five at each corner.53
cubeA sugar cube; the ordinary die.6 squares, three at each corner.27
tetrahedronThe original Tetra Pak milk carton; a pyramid on a triangle.4 triangles, three at each corner; the simplest solid.25
octahedronTwo pyramids glued base to base; many natural diamond crystals.8 triangles, four at each corner.27
Archimedean / Catalan solidsA football is Archimedean.Archimedean: faces of two or more regular kinds, every corner alike (13 of them). Catalan: their duals (see dual).3
truncatedSlicing the corners off a cheese cube; the football is a truncated icosahedron.Corners cut off flat, so each corner becomes a new small face.20
rhombic dodecahedronGarnet crystals; the three-diamond floor of a bee's cell is part of one.12 identical diamond-shaped faces; fills space with copies of itself.6
cuboctahedronHalfway between a cube and an octahedron: cut a cube's corners to the edge midpoints.8 triangles and 6 squares; the shape around one orange in a tight stack.3
stellationA Christmas star ornament.Extend the faces of a solid outward until they meet again; the result is a star.4
deltahedronA solid you could build only from identical triangle tiles.Every face an equilateral triangle.2
antiprismA drum with zigzag lacing between top and bottom.Two equal polygons, one turned, joined by a band of triangles.1
convexA ball, not a banana: a rubber band stretched round it touches everywhere.No dents or hollows.11
dualSwap the roles: a dot on each face, then join the dots.Faces become corners and corners become faces. Cube ↔ octahedron, dodecahedron ↔ icosahedron; the tetrahedron is its own dual.10
dihedral angleHow far a book is opened.The angle between two faces along their shared edge.2
congruentTwo biscuits from the same cutter.Same shape and size; one can be moved onto the other exactly.1
symmetryTurn a snowflake by a sixth of a turn: it looks unchanged.A turn or mirror that leaves the object looking the same.5
netA flattened cereal box.The flat cut-out that folds up into the solid. (In crystal papers a net can also mean a network of bonds; say network there.)3

2. Filling space

TermEveryday pictureWhat it meansOn the site
tile · tiling · tessellationA bathroom floor.Covering a flat floor with shapes, with no gaps and no overlaps.27
space-fillingPacking a car boot so perfectly that no air is left between the boxes.Copies of a solid that fill all of space with no gaps and no overlaps: tiling, one dimension up.5
honeycombThe bees' comb, but with any solid as the cell.In geometry, a space-filling arrangement of solids.1
cellA room in a building; a cell of the comb.One solid piece of a space-filling, or one 3D piece of the surface of a 4D shape.29
latticeStreetlights on a perfect grid of streets, going on for ever, in three directions.Points repeated by the same steps in every direction; every point sees the same view.8
octetA space-frame roof or climbing frame of triangles (Buckminster Fuller's octet truss).Tetrahedra and octahedra together, two tetrahedra to each octahedron, filling space.25
stacking · close-packed layersOranges on a market stall: each new layer sits in the dips of the one below.Each layer can sit in one of three positions, A, B or C; the order of the letters is the stacking.51
c and h layers · hexagonalityKnitting Crystals (#83): linking yarns run straight on at a c layer and turn back at an h layer.A layer is h when the layers on both sides of it match (…ABA…), c when they differ (…ABC…). Hexagonality is the share of h layers: ABAB… 100%, ABCABC… 0%.15
FCC · hcpThe two classic ways greengrocers stack oranges.ABCABC… (face-centred cubic) and ABAB… (hexagonal close-packed). Same tightness, different order.0
polytypeThe same paving slabs laid in a different repeating order.Crystals made of the same layers stacked in different orders, as in silicon carbide (3C, 4H, 6H…).2
Dehn invariantThe cut-and-rebuild test: can this solid be sawn up and reassembled as a cube?A number attached to a solid. Two solids of equal volume can be cut into each other only if their Dehn invariants match; a regular tetrahedron cannot become a cube (Dehn 1900).2
shell · clusterRussian dolls; the layers of an onion.A group of solids around a centre (cluster), or one complete layer around it (shell).22

3. Crystals and nature

TermEveryday pictureWhat it meansOn the site
crystalGrains of table salt are tiny cubes.Atoms arranged in a pattern that repeats in every direction.23
KagomeThe kagome weave of a Japanese bamboo basket: triangles and hexagons.A pattern of triangles that touch only at their corners, with hexagonal holes between them.4
pyrochloreA climbing frame of pyramids touching only at their tips.Kagome's 3D cousin: tetrahedra joined corner to corner. Named after a mineral that turns green in a flame (Greek pyr, fire; chloros, green).7
spin iceTiny compass needles at every corner, two pointing in and two out at each pyramid.Magnets on a pyrochlore network that obey the same two-in, two-out rule as hydrogen in water ice.2
perovskiteThe crystal family behind the newest solar cells.A large family of crystals with one shared layout of atoms.3
icosahedralShaped like the 20-sided die: virus shells, footballs.Having the symmetry of the icosahedron, including five-fold turns.12
quasicrystal · aperiodicIslamic girih tilework: strict rules, yet the pattern never repeats.Ordered by a rule, but never repeating by shifting. Found in real alloys (Shechtman, Nobel Prize 2011).3
Penrose tilingTwo diamond-shaped tiles that cover a floor without the pattern ever repeating.The best-known aperiodic tiling (Roger Penrose, 1974).4
approximantAs 22/7 comes close to π.A repeating crystal that comes very close to a quasicrystal.1
Frank–Kasper phasesMetal alloys packed from near-tetrahedral pieces.Crystals in which every gap between atoms is close to a tetrahedron.2
golden ratio φIn a five-pointed star, every line is cut in this ratio.About 1.618: the diagonal of a regular pentagon divided by its side. It runs through every shape with five-fold symmetry.25
FibonacciSpirals on a pinecone: often 8 one way, 13 the other.1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13…: each number the sum of the two before; neighbours divide to about φ.8
entropyHow many ways a deck of cards can be shuffled.A measure of how many arrangements fit the same rules.4

4. Mirrors and higher dimensions

TermEveryday pictureWhat it meansOn the site
mirror · kaleidoscopeA kaleidoscope with three mirrors meeting at a point.Three mirrors at the right angles turn one small triangle into a whole solid; a dot placed inside it gives all the Platonic and Archimedean solids (Wythoff's construction).10
four-dimensionalShadow puppets: a 3D hand casts a flat shadow; a 4D shape casts a 3D shadow we can build.Having a fourth direction at right angles to all three we know.8
polytopeThe word that covers polygon, polyhedron and their cousins in any dimension.A flat-sided shape in any number of dimensions.1
24-cell · 120-cell · 600-cellThe 4D cousins of the Platonic solids.4D shapes whose surface is made of 24 octahedra, 120 dodecahedra or 600 tetrahedra.14
projectionA shadow on a wall.Flattening a shape onto fewer dimensions.1
E8The tightest way to stack balls, in eight dimensions.An 8-dimensional lattice; Maryna Viazovska proved in 2016 that it packs spheres most tightly.1
KeplerThe astronomer who also drew these solids (1596, 1619) and guessed the orange stack is the tightest.Johannes Kepler; many solids on the site first appear in his drawings.8

Notes

  • Knitting. The roadmap's "row-by-row patterns used in sweaters" is decoration, a pattern knitted into a fabric. Knitting Crystals (#83) is structural: the knitted sheets are the close-packed layers, and the linking yarns run straight on at a c layer and turn back at an h layer, so the fabric is the crystal. The glossary uses DICTO's version only.
  • Platonic order. The five solids are always listed Dodeca, Icosa, Cube, Tetra, Octa: D·I·C·T·O.
  • Facts checked: Tetra Pak's first carton (1952) was a tetrahedron; virus shells and the football are icosahedral and truncated icosahedral; garnet grows as rhombic dodecahedra; a bee cell's floor is three rhombi; kagome is the Japanese basket weave (kago, basket; me, eye); pyrochlore turns green in a flame; Dehn 1900 (Hilbert's third problem); Shechtman, Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2011; Viazovska, E8, 2016; Penrose tiling 1974; Kepler, Mysterium Cosmographicum 1596 and Harmonices Mundi 1619.
