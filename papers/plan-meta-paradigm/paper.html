2. The meta-arc: the rings are the beginning, the middle and the end

DICTO's rule for a book (13 November) is applied to the whole: a beginning that is a question, a middle that climbs, a summit, and an end that returns to the beginning, transformed. The question of the whole: what stays in place when the parts of space change places?

Sample opening paragraph for the whole (for the series page; DICTO's to change).

About 1645 the swordsman Miyamoto Musashi set down his way in five books: Earth, Water, Fire, Wind and the Void. Nineteen centuries before him Euclid had set down geometry as definitions, postulates and propositions, and ended by building the five regular solids and proving that there are no more. This work borrows from both. Its five rings are the five solids of DICTO's name, each in its element's colour: the cube for Earth, the icosahedron for Water, the tetrahedron for Fire, the octahedron for Wind, the dodecahedron for the Void. Earth lays out the pieces of space. Water follows them as they flow into one another. Fire is the meeting: two neighbours change places, and a cell of living skin is carried into four dimensions. Wind goes back to the old schools and their five solids. The Void goes beyond them, and comes home. One question runs through all five: what stays in place when the parts of space change places?

Sample closing paragraph for the whole (the last paragraph of Book Eleven; DICTO's to change).

We began on the ground, with a handful of pieces, and asked what stays in place when they change places. It was tempting to answer: the laws. DICTO put that as a proposition, that geometry gives its laws at the fixed points of its swaps, and it was tested against forty-five proved laws of this work. As a law of everything it is false. Nine of the forty-five sit at such a still point; many more concern a swapped pair, or stay the same on both sides; seventeen know no swap at all. What is true is smaller and firmer: a still point carries a law when the swap forces one there, because a quantity that changes sign must vanish, or a balanced and arched one must peak, or the fixed shape has a symmetry its partners lack. Three examples: Kelvin's cell; the solid whose faces all touch one sphere; the tiling that is its own partner. Each stands at such a point, and each for a stated reason. Musashi ends in the Void, where there is nothing but what is known truly. The pieces of Book One are the same pieces. The question is still open, and it is a better question.

Sources for the closing paragraph: the swaps study of 13 November (45 laws: 9 at a fixed point, 1 at a rotation axis, 1 near, 4 invariances, 10 pairs, 3 hops, 17 none); Kelvin's cell as the fixed point of the pair swap μ ↔ 3 − μ, with the only zero Dehn invariant on its line and the most wall per cell (the study's checks K3, K4, exact); the turn's fixed solid (The Turn Law, held draft; proved for regular convex solids, failing for 12 of 14 star polytopes); the Ammann–Beenker tiling (held draft, Theorem 3). The three mechanisms are known general principles, not DICTO's discoveries: parity; symmetric criticality (Palais 1979) with concavity, and Lindelöf's theorem (1869); extra symmetry of the fixed object. DICTO's are the swaps themselves on his lines and structures. The reading of Musashi's Void is a paraphrase and should be checked against a named translation before it is printed.

How the Platonic book honours Elements XIII without ending the series. Euclid's last book builds the five solids in one sphere (XIII.13–17) and ends with the remark that no other regular solid exists (after XIII.18). The Platonic book already does both: its Chapter 1 closes Euclid's cube in the dodecahedron (XIII.17) with Pacioli's and Kepler's inscriptions into one cell holding all five, and its Chapter 13, "Beyond the Five", gives the count of regular solids in every dimension (Schläfli's, credited). So Book Eight is the Euclidean close of the work: the last book whose subject can be finished, with "no more" proved. It stands in the Wind ring, among the schools, and the Void follows it. Euclid ends by closing; Musashi ends by opening; the series does both, in that order. Two sentences are added to the Platonic book for this, one in its Opening and one at Chapter 13 (Section 6); its title, order and content are otherwise untouched.

4. The swap thread, with its true outcome

The thread is kept: a swap is a move that undoes itself, and the books can be read by the swaps they use. The claim on the thread is corrected. False: every law sits at the fixed point of a swap. True (swaps study): a swap plays some role in 24 of 45 proved laws; 9 sit at its fixed point; a fixed point carries a law when the swap forces it, by parity, by symmetry with concavity, or by an extra symmetry of the fixed object. Moves that do not undo themselves (the Fibonacci substitution, the growth rule, the golden cycles) are hops, not swaps. The table gives each book's swaps with the class the study gives its laws.

Retelling: a deliberate duplicate of one to four pages, in the borrowing book's voice, headed "retold from" with the home named. Pointer: one line. Duplication is used where the arc needs the reader to hold the result in hand at that moment, and only there.
