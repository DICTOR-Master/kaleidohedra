Chapter 1. Plato: the five solids and their centres

Imagine a solid object like a box or a ball, and picture a single dot hidden safely inside it. If you were to connect that dot to every corner and every edge, you would carve the object into many small, pointy tents that all meet at the center. These tents fill the space perfectly, and the walls between them are the only parts that remain when we remove the outer skin of the shape.

The Five Solids and the Name DICTO

Plato described the universe using five perfect shapes, which he called the elements. In his dialogue Timaeus [CHECK], he assigned the pyramid to the element of fire. DICTO, the artist, noticed that his own name contains the initials of these five solids. He arranged them in a specific order: the dodecahedron, the icosahedron, the cube, the tetrahedron, and the octahedron. This sequence forms the foundation of his work on the Centre Pyramids.

Building the Centre Pyramids

Every solid with a centre O inside it is filled by its centre pyramids. We define the centre pyramid of a face as the pyramid with that face as its base and the centre O as its apex. DICTO proposed that if we take a solid and remove all the outer faces, what remains are the walls between these pyramids. For each edge of the solid, there is a flat triangle connecting the centre to that edge. DICTO calls this triangle a fin. The space between the fins is empty, leaving a structure that looks like a star of thin triangles meeting at the centre.

DICTO proposed that this structure is called the Vacuus. The name comes from the idea of a solid emptied down to its centre pyramids' walls. He suggested two ways to build this: SHEETS, which are the fins themselves, and STRUTS, which are rods from the centre to every corner, combined with the solid's edges. This method of building allows us to see the internal geometry of any shape that has a centre inside it.

[FIGURE: A cube built from six square pyramids meeting at the centre, with the outer squares removed to show the star of twelve thin triangles]

The Geometry of the Centre

When we join the centre O to each face, we create a set of pyramids. The walls between these pyramids are the fins. DICTO proposed that for every edge AB of a solid, the flat triangle O–A–B is the fin of that edge. These fins meet at the centre and form a network of walls. If the centre O lies strictly on the inner side of every face plane, the centre pyramids fill the solid without overlap. If the centre lies on or beyond a face plane, the pyramids do not fill the space correctly.

We found that all fins are congruent only for the Platonic and Archimedean solids, the rhombic dodecahedron, the rhombic triacontahedron, and Delilah, an octahedron. This means that for these shapes, the walls between the pyramids are all identical in size and shape. For other solids, the fins vary in size, but the method of construction remains the same.

[FIGURE: The fins of a cube, showing the twelve thin triangles meeting at the centre, each standing on an edge of the cube]

The Historical Line from Plato to DICTO

Plato's Timaeus [CHECK] introduced the idea that the elements are solids, with fire as the pyramid. DICTO extended this by focusing on the centre of these solids. He proposed that the centre is the key to understanding the internal structure. The fins and struts are the new elements that DICTO discovered. He named the family of solids the DICTO Vacuus Solids after Leonardo's vacuus bodies in Pacioli's De divina proportione.

DICTO came to this idea by building. He made a cube from his PET-bottle system, and the build showed him that a cube is six pyramids meeting at the centre. Earlier, he had built the octahedron's Vacuus from four slotted pieces of cardboard. These physical models confirmed the mathematical theory. The face fan itself is classical, but DICTO proposed building the centre-to-edge fins or the centre struts as solids. We found no source that builds the centre-to-edge fins or the centre struts as solids before DICTO.

The Census of Solids

We gave a census of 100 solids: the 69 solids of DICTO Solids v7 and the 31 Platonic, Archimedean and Catalan solids. 92 of them are in exact arithmetic. The Vacuus is well defined for 99; the one failure is Lotus Hexa, whose centre lies outside 24 of its face planes. This means that for Lotus Hexa, the centre pyramids do not fill the solid correctly. For all other solids, the method works perfectly.

DICTO proposed that twelve of his solids have all their centre struts of one length. Nine of them build their whole frame in at most three lengths. The cuboctahedron is the only solid whose whole frame has one length. This uniformity makes these shapes special in the world of geometry. Kagemusha holds a flat central regular pentagon of five 72° fins, and Lion six flat central decagons of 36° fins. These are unique features of specific solids in the census.

Origami and the Final Form

DICTO proposed making these structures in paper as modular origami. Each centre pyramid, folded open from one flat fan, nests with the others so that every fin is a double wall. Seven solids were netted, each from one to three nets. Every net folds back to its pyramid to 3.1 × 10 −15 of the longest strut. This precision shows that the geometry is exact and the construction is reliable.

The idea, the family and its name, the two built forms and the origami are DICTO's. "One-Strut Vacuus", "Disc Vacuus" and "Isofin Vacuus" are placeholders until DICTO names them. Computation, proofs, exact checks and drafting with Claude (Anthropic) were used to verify the results. The proof of the main theorem is one line from the classical facts that the face fan of P* is the normal fan of P and that a spherical polygon and its polar have supplementary angles and sides.

[FIGURE: The modular origami of a centre pyramid, showing how the fins nest to form a double wall]

We lead with a theorem on a solid P and its polar P* about the same centre. The apex angle of the fin of each edge of P* is π minus the dihedral angle of P at the dual edge. The wedge between two neighbouring fins of P* is π minus the face angle of P at the dual corner. The fins of an edge and of its dual edge lie in perpendicular planes. This theorem connects the geometry of a solid to its dual, showing the deep symmetry in the Centre Pyramids.

DICTO's work continues the line from Aristotle through his earlier work. It makes this paper a companion to #44 [1]. The Centre Pyramids are a new way to see the old shapes of Plato. They reveal the hidden structure inside every solid with a centre. DICTO proposed that this is the true nature of the elements, not just the outer faces, but the inner walls that hold them together.
