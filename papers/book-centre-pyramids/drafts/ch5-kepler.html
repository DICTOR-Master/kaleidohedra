Chapter 5. Kepler: twelve pyramids in the rhombic dodecahedron

Imagine a box made of six square rooms that all meet at a single point in the middle. Now imagine that if you add six more rooms on top of each wall, you get a shape that can stack perfectly with its neighbors to fill an entire room without any gaps. This is the secret geometry that connects the ancient cube to the honeycomb-like structures found in nature.

The Rhombic Dodecahedron and Kepler

Kepler described the rhombic dodecahedron in 1611 [CHECK where and how]. This solid is unique because it fills space completely. Dissect the rhombic dodecahedron into the central pyramids of its cube. This dissection yields 12 congruent square pyramids. Six of these pyramids form the cube at its centre, while the other six stand on the cube's faces. Consequently, the rhombic dodecahedron has exactly twice the cube's volume. This geometric fact matches Proposition 2 of #87 read backwards, where the cube's Star, with its centre pyramids turned out, is the rhombic dodecahedron.

Reconstruction and Halving

The rhombic dodecahedron also reconstructs into two cubes, which can then be arranged into a 1x1x2 brick. This transformation demonstrates how these complex forms relate to simple rectangular blocks. Further, halve the cube. In this process, the cube splits into two truncated octahedra, consisting of a central one plus eight corner eighths. These operations show that the rhombic dodecahedron is not an isolated shape but a bridge between the cube and the brick.

The Five Platonic Solids

To understand the context of these pyramids, we must list the five Platonic solids in DICTO order. The sequence begins with the dodecahedron, followed by the icosahedron, the cube, the tetrahedron, and finally the octahedron. This ordering reflects the specific focus of DICTO's work on the centre pyramids of these forms. The theorem states that of the five face pyramids, only the cube's alone and the tetrahedron's with the octahedron's fill space. The icosahedron's pyramid, known as the Cleo-Icos, needs a partner that is not a face pyramid.

The Delian Problem

The Delian problem is only a historical side remark, not DICTO's aim. While the ancient Greeks sought to double the volume of a cube, DICTO's work focuses on the tiling of space by the pyramids themselves. The mathematics of the centre pyramids reveals that the cube's pyramid is the only single face pyramid that fills space. When combined with the tetrahedron's pyramid, the octahedron's pyramid also allows for a perfect tiling. The dodecahedron's and the icosahedron's pyramids can never occur in such a set.

The Central Pyramids

For a Platonic solid with centre O, the face pyramid over a face F is the convex hull of F and O. Its dihedral angle along a base edge is half the solid's dihedral angle. Along a side edge, from O to a corner, it is 360° divided by the number of faces meeting at that corner. We write C, T, O, D and I for the face pyramids of the cube, tetrahedron, octahedron, dodecahedron and icosahedron. The icosahedron's pyramid, I, is DICTO's Cleo-Icos.
  1. The cube's pyramid alone fills space.
  2. The tetrahedron's and the octahedron's pyramids together fill space.
  3. The union of these two sets also fills space.
  4. The dodecahedron's pyramid can never occur in a space-filling set.
  5. The icosahedron's pyramid fills space only with a partner that is not a face pyramid.

The work confirms that the cube's pyramids are the foundation of space-filling geometry, while the other solids require specific partners or cannot fill space at all.
