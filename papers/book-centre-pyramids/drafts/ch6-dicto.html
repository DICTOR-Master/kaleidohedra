Chapter 6. DICTO: building the centre pyramids

Imagine a solid object as a collection of many small tents, where every tent shares a single central pole and spreads out to cover one face of the shape. If you remove the fabric of the tents and keep only the poles and the lines connecting them, you are left with a skeleton that reveals the hidden geometry inside. This is the core idea of the centre pyramids, a way to see how any three-dimensional form is constructed from a single point in the middle.

The PET-bottle Cube and the First Discovery

DICTO proposed a method to visualize this internal structure by building a cube from his PET-bottle system B1NR. Upon completion, DICTO observed that the cube is composed of six pyramids meeting at the centre. This observation led to the concept of the Vacuus, a solid emptied down to its centre pyramids' walls. In this system, one fin extends from the centre to every edge, and one strut extends from the centre to every corner. DICTO proposed that every solid with a centre O inside it is filled by its centre pyramids, one over each face with apex O. The Vacuus of the solid keeps only the walls between these pyramids: for each edge AB the flat triangle O–A–B, its fin.

The Cardboard Octahedron and Slot-together Mechanics

Before #87, DICTO built the octahedron Vacuus from corrugated cardboard in four pieces slotted together without glue. The construction involved square A with one slot from a corner to the centre, square B with three slots, and square C cut in two along a diagonal, each half with one slot. DICTO laid the pieces out as three squares to demonstrate the assembly. Slot-together works exactly for the solids whose fins close into whole discs through the centre: the octahedron (3 squares), cuboctahedron (4 hexagons), and icosidodecahedron (6 decagons). For the cube, rhombic dodecahedron, icosahedron and dodecahedron the fins meet only at the centre. In metal, specifically aluminium, stainless, or corten plate, the four octahedron pieces slide together with no welds or bolts. Standing on three corners, its own weight holds it; each half of C carries two drop-in tabs through slots in A. Squares of 0.9 m edge give a piece 1.27 m across that fits a small car. The cube Vacuus in metal needs 8 weld seams from 4 bent strips. DICTO proposed that the Vacuus is built in two forms, SHEETS (the fins) and STRUTS (a rod from O to every corner, with the solid's edges).

The Geometry of Fins and Dual Solids

We lead with a theorem on a solid P and its polar P* about the same centre. The apex angle of the fin of each edge of P* is π minus the dihedral angle of P at the dual edge. The wedge between two neighbouring fins of P* is π minus the face angle of P at the dual corner. The fins of an edge and of its dual edge lie in perpendicular planes. The proof is one line from the classical facts that the face fan of P* is the normal fan of P and that a spherical polygon and its polar have supplementary angles and sides. DICTO proposed a census of 100 solids: the 69 solids of DICTO Solids v7 and the 31 Platonic, Archimedean and Catalan solids. 92 of them are in exact arithmetic. The Vacuus is well defined for 99; the one failure is Lotus Hexa, whose centre lies outside 24 of its face planes. All fins are congruent only for the Platonic and Archimedean solids, the rhombic dodecahedron, the rhombic triacontahedron and Delilah, an octahedron. Twelve of DICTO's solids have all their centre struts of one length, nine of them build their whole frame in at most three lengths, and the cuboctahedron is the only solid whose whole frame has one length.

Origami and the Five Platonic Solids

DICTO's origami features open-pyramid fan nets, double walls, and the two-half-tab rule. DICTO proposed to fold the cube first. Each centre pyramid, folded open from one flat fan, nests with the others so that every fin is a double wall. Seven solids were netted, each from one to three nets, and every net folds back to its pyramid to 3.1 × 10 −15 of the longest strut. The face fan itself is classical; Leonardo's vacuus bodies for Pacioli (1509) are edge frames with no centre. We found no source that builds the centre-to-edge fins or the centre struts as solids. DICTO proposed the specific order for the five Platonic solids in his work: dodecahedron, icosahedron, cube, tetrahedron, octahedron. Kagemusha holds a flat central regular pentagon of five 72° fins, and Lion six flat central decagons of 36° fins. The idea, the family and its name, the two built forms and the origami are DICTO's. "One-Strut Vacuus", "Disc Vacuus" and "Isofin Vacuus" are placeholders until DICTO names them.

Conclusion: From Plato to Kepler

The journey from Plato to Kepler is re-examined through the lens of the centre pyramids. DICTO proposed that the ancient solids are not just static forms but dynamic systems of fins and struts. The Vacuus reveals the internal logic of these shapes. DICTO's work bridges the gap between the theoretical geometry of the past and the physical construction of the present. The five Platonic solids, listed in DICTO order, stand as the foundation of this new architectural language.
