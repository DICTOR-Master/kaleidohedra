Chapter 4. The Vacuus

From #87 DICTO Vacuus Solids (§§2, 3, 8; Figure 1 and Plates 1–5) and #107 The Vacuus of the Queens (§§1, 2); the table of §4.3 and Dogstar's discs from study 2026-11-11-orbit-polytopes.

Build a cube from six square pyramids with their tips at the centre, then take away the six squares. What is left is a star of twelve thin triangles, one on every edge, meeting at the centre: the walls between the pyramids. DICTO named the solids that keep only these walls the DICTO Vacuus Solids, after the vacuus bodies Leonardo drew for Pacioli, which are edge frames without a centre. Each wall is the fin of its edge, the triangle from the centre to the edge; a strut runs from the centre to each corner. He built the octahedron's Vacuus first, from four slotted pieces of cardboard: three squares through one centre.

Two questions are asked of each Vacuus. What are its angles? The polar law answers: the apex angle of a fin is π minus the dihedral angle of the polar solid at the dual edge, so the five Vacuus are fixed by the five dihedral angles, in dual pairs (#87). And which fins lie flat together on one plane through the centre, closing into a central disc? The Mirror Law answers for every solid built from a reflection group: the edges lie on mirrors exactly when the group's unringed nodes hold a root orthogonal to them (#107). For the five, every edge lies on a mirror, but only the octahedron gathers more than two edges on one: its three squares. On the hidden side of DICTO's own solids the same question finds discs again (the Angeli of Chapter 12).

What this chapter shows. (1) The polar law: for a convex solid P and its polar P* about the same centre, the fin of an edge of P* has apex angle π minus the dihedral angle of P at the dual edge; the wedges are π minus the face angles; the fins of an edge and of its dual edge lie in perpendicular planes (Theorem of #87). (2) Turned out over their faces, the centre pyramids double the volume, and each edge's dihedral angle doubles (Proposition 2 of #87). (3) The Mirror Law (Theorem 1 of #107). (4) The five Vacuus side by side: the octahedron has three central discs of four fins, the other four none (§4.3). (5) Hart's Dogstar, the dodecahedron's partner in space (Chapter 7), has 114 of its 138 edges on fifteen central discs (§4.4, measured).

4.3 Discs: which fins lie flat together

A disc is a plane through the centre carrying at least three edges, so that their fins lie flat together; a complete disc closes all the way round the centre. The census of study 2026-11-11-orbit-polytopes (vacuus_census.json, controls matching #87) gives for the five:

The planes in the fourth column are the mirror planes of each solid's symmetry: the fifteen of the icosahedral group for the dodecahedron and the icosahedron, the six diagonal planes of the cube, the six of the tetrahedron, and for the octahedron the three coordinate planes. So every edge of every Platonic solid lies on a mirror, as the Mirror Law says for every regular polytope (Corollary 2 of #107 in four dimensions; the same argument in three), and the law reads off how many: the octahedron's mirrors through its corners each carry a whole square of four edges, while the other four solids have one or two edges on each mirror and no disc. Among the solids round the five, the cuboctahedron (four hexagonal discs of six edges) and the icosidodecahedron (six decagonal discs of ten) have complete discs too, but these are not mirrors of their groups: a Vacuus need not be made of mirrors (study 2026-11-11-orbit-polytopes, observation 1).

4.4 Dogstar's fifteen discs

The Dogstar of Chapter 7, the space between dodecahedra on alternate cubes, is not convex, but its centre lies inside it and its Vacuus can be measured the same way (study 2026-11-11-orbit-polytopes, in floating point). Its 138 edges lie on 27 planes through the centre: twelve planes carry 8 edges each and three carry 6 each, so 114 of the 138 edges lie on fifteen central discs, and the other twelve planes carry two edges each. Only one of its 84 faces lies on a plane through the centre. The fifteen discs lie in the fifteen mirror planes of the icosahedral group, three coordinate planes and twelve golden ones; only the three coordinate planes are mirrors of the Dogstar's own pyritohedral group. With 83 % of its edges on discs, the Dogstar has a concentrated Vacuus, near the octahedron's pattern of few discs; among DICTO's solids only the Sunstar's tetrahedral cluster has a larger share (94 %).

4.5 Turned out: the Star

The pyramids that the Vacuus separates can also be turned out, each over its own face. For the five the result is a solid of twice the volume whose old edges have doubled dihedral angles: the cube closes into the rhombic dodecahedron, the tetrahedron keeps convex edges, and the dodecahedron, icosahedron and octahedron fold inward.
