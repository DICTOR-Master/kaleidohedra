Chapter 10. Filigree

This chapter sums up #47 Every Mix of the Platonic Solids Fills Space (DICTO DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.23551326), #106 One World for the Platonic Solids (DICTO DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24126400) and Space-Fillers and Lattice Moves (Book Two), Chapter 8 §6, The hybrid: one golden slab (DICTO DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24221378); the jewellery is checked and drawn in study 2026-11-15-filigree.

Chapters 8 and 9 filled space with every mix of the five, first with all edges equal, then with each solid at its own size. Both times the solids could not touch one another directly; something had to go between them. In a piece of jewellery that something is gold: the filigree that holds stones of different shapes close together, filling the spaces between them and leaving the piece flat where it must lie flat. This chapter reads the connecting pieces of Chapters 8 and 9 that way. Every one of them does one of three things, and the two rules, equal edges and free sizes, become two ways of setting the same stones.

What this chapter shows. (1) Every connecting piece of Chapters 8 and 9 has one of three roles: it sets a solid in a cell (setting), fills between the cells (ground), or makes a layer flat where two worlds meet (edging). (2) At edge 1 the icosahedron's setting, six Admiral Caps and eight caps, fills exactly the place of a dodecahedron, so icosahedra and dodecahedra share one golden slab with ground between them and no edging (Proposition 10.1, Theorem 10.2; proved in Space-Fillers and Lattice Moves, Chapter 8). (3) The edging can be thin: roofs and roof plates half a unit thick (Proposition 10.2). (4) Under each rule, the number of connecting pieces each of the 31 mixes needs in the constructions checked (Table 10.3). (5) Where no gold is needed at all: the cube; the tetrahedron with the octahedron; all three together, on triangle planes (Theorem 10.4). (6) Jewellery drawn from exact coordinates: a ring, a bracelet, a brooch, a pendant, a necklace, a pair of earrings and a pendant of all five solids nested in one cell of the octet, with pierced bands, closed loops and satellite stones as variations; the octet gives a closed ring of twelve stones round a tunnel (Proposition 10.3), and a stone seated on one face makes the earrings a true left and right pair (Proposition 10.4).

10.1 What needs something between

Only the cube fills space alone, and of the pairs only the tetrahedron with the octahedron (Conway, Jiao and Torquato 2011; Gabbrielli, Jiao and Torquato 2012). Every other choice of the five leaves gaps, and a counting map on dihedral angles shows that no arrangement of these solids alone can close them: every mix containing the dodecahedron or the icosahedron needs a connector, and so do the tetrahedron alone, the octahedron alone, and either of them with the cube (#47, Corollary 1; Chapter 3 for the counting map of #44). So the question is never whether something goes between, but what, and how little.

A roof here is always Euclid's: the dodecahedron of edge 1 is a cube of edge φ with a roof on each face (Elements XIII.17), and almost every piece of the kit is that cube and that roof put together in another way. The Dogstar is the cube without six roofs (the star George Hart catalogued among the stellations of the dodecahedron), X the cube without one, Y the cube with one, and the roof plate Z a slab of the cube's face with one roof taken out.

The settings are not only for space-filling. In the nest of Chapter 11, where each solid sits inside the next (O ⊃ I ⊃ D ⊃ C ⊃ T), the gaps between neighbours are settings too (#99, §1): the six Admiral Caps between octahedron and icosahedron; 12 Sheriffs and 30 Lone-stars between icosahedron and dodecahedron (the Sheriff is the pentagonal cap of the icosahedron over a face of the dodecahedron, the Lone-star the thin wedge between an icosahedron edge and the dodecahedron edge beneath it); Euclid's six roofs between dodecahedron and cube; and four copies of the octahedron's centre pyramid between cube and tetrahedron.

One setting works at equal edges as well as at free sizes, because the icosahedron and the dodecahedron then have the same edge. It is the setting on which the best equal-edge construction rests.

Proposition 10.1 (the icosahedron's setting; Space-Fillers and Lattice Moves, Proposition 8.1; #106, Table 1). Put Euclid's cube of edge φ at the centre of the dodecahedron of edge 1. The octahedron whose six corners are the midpoints of the six roof ridges lies inside the dodecahedron, and Pacioli's icosahedron of edge 1 lies inside that octahedron, its twelve corners dividing the octahedron's edges in the golden ratio (Pacioli 1509). So the dodecahedron is exactly the icosahedron, the six Admiral Caps (the six corner pieces of the octahedron left round the icosahedron) and eight caps (the parts of the dodecahedron outside the octahedron, one in each octant, all congruent and convex, each of volume (27 + 13√5)/96).

The proof is exact in ℚ(√5) and is given in Space-Fillers and Lattice Moves, Chapter 8 §6, with every containment and volume checked (study 2026-11-14-platonic-hybrid). The setting is drawn in Figure ⟦f-ring⟧ as a ring: six Admiral Caps alone hold the stone like six claws; with the eight caps it is closed.

10.4 The ground: the Dogstar

Put dodecahedra of edge 1 on alternate cubes of the cubic grid of edge φ. Each cube between them receives one roof from each of its six neighbours, and what is left of it is the Dogstar: a cube of edge φ with Euclid's roof pressed into every face, of volume exactly φ/2 (Hart; #42; Chapter 7). Dodecahedra and Dogstars fill space. The Dogstar is the ground of the whole golden world: whatever stands in a cell, a dodecahedron or any solid in its setting, presents the same twelve pentagons to the Dogstars round it, so the ground never changes. One construction of Euclid's, the cube and its roof, is behind all of it.

The ground and the icosahedron's claws weigh the same: the six Admiral Caps of one icosahedron and one Dogstar both have volume exactly φ/2 (#47, Corollary 3). #47 knows no reason for this.

10.5 The edging, and why it exists

A world built on one length cannot meet a world built on another without a seam. The golden grid has period φ; cubes of edge 1 have period 1; the octet's layers have rows √3/2 apart. To stack them, each world is cut into a slab with flat faces (#47, Lemma 1), and edging is what makes the golden slab flat: in #47, a layer of X (next to a dodecahedron, the cube less the roof that reaches into it) and Y (next to a Dogstar, the cube with the roof that the Dogstar lacks), each a full cube of edge φ thick. The edging can be much thinner.

Proposition 10.2 (the roof plate; Space-Fillers and Lattice Moves, Proposition 8.3). On the top face of one layer of the golden slab, a roof stands above every cell and a roof-shaped hollow is open above every Dogstar; all their ridges are parallel, and the roof is exactly ½ high. Lay a roof plate Z (a square plate φ × φ × ½ with one roof-shaped hollow, of volume (11 + 3√5)/24) hollow down over each cell; drop a roof R point down into each Dogstar's hollow and cover it with Z and a second R, which make a full plate. The face is then flat, and the same below. The slab is φ + 1 = φ² thick.

10.6 Two rules, two theorems

The two rules of Chapters 8 and 9 set the same stones in two ways.

Theorem 10.1 (equal edges; #47, Main theorem; Chapter 8). Every one of the 31 mixes of the five Platonic solids, all of edge 1, fills space with connecting pieces from one kit of seven: P, Q, Q′, A, S, X, Y. The tiling is a stack of flat slabs, one for each world the mix needs.

Theorem 10.2 (one golden slab; Space-Fillers and Lattice Moves, Theorem 8.3 and Corollary 8.4). In the golden slab any dodecahedron may be replaced by an icosahedron of edge 1 in its setting (Proposition 10.1). So every mix with the icosahedron fills space without #47's large octet and without Q′; all five solids of edge 1 fill space in three slabs (one golden slab, cubes, octet layers cut on their triangle planes) with the five connecting pieces A, cap, S, R and Z, against six in four slabs in #47; and the dodecahedron with the icosahedron needs no slab at all, only A, cap and S.

Theorem 10.3 (free sizes; #106, Theorems 1 and 2; Chapter 9). With the dodecahedron of edge 1, the cube, tetrahedron and octahedron of edge φ and the icosahedron of edge √2/φ, every one of the 31 mixes fills space in two worlds, the golden grid and one octet, meeting face to face in a single flat seam, with a kit of six: P, Q, A, S, X, Y. With every solid in its setting (Table 10.2), every mix fills space in one world, with no seam.

In filigree terms the hybrid of Theorem 10.2 is the best equal-edge form: at edge 1 the icosahedron's setting is the same shape as the dodecahedron it replaces, so the two golden stones share one slab, with the Dogstar as ground and no edging between them. Edging is then needed only where the golden slab meets the cubes or the octet.

10.7 Where no gold is needed

Theorem 10.4 (#47, Theorem 2, Version 3; Space-Fillers, Theorem 8.2). Cubes, tetrahedra and octahedra of edge 1 fill space together with no connecting piece: cubes in flat slabs, and between them layers of the octet cut on their triangle planes, flat on both sides. No such tiling is face to face.

So the stones need no gold at all in three of the 31 mixes: the cube alone, the tetrahedron with the octahedron (the octet), and all three together. Figure ⟦f-var⟧(a) shows twelve such stones as a ring. Without edging, but with settings and ground, live the golden mixes: dodecahedra alone with Dogstars, icosahedra alone in their settings (or in #47's large octet with A and Q′), and the dodecahedron with the icosahedron (Theorem 10.2). The gold that remains is the least that holds the pentagons: ground between the cells, and the icosahedron's setting.

10.8 Jewellery

DICTO designed the jewellery of this section as pictures of the kit, in two materials only: stones for the Platonic solids, in their element colours, and one gold for every connecting piece. Each figure is drawn from exact coordinates, so its caption's count of gold is the count of pieces drawn. The core designs set stones in the kit's own gold, as above: the ring (Figure ⟦f-ring⟧), the bracelet (Figure ⟦f-brace⟧) and the brooch (Figure ⟦f-brooch⟧). Three variations follow: a band pierced by a tunnel, a closed loop of stones with the hole in the middle, and satellite stones seated round a band or loop. In every design at least one solid stays a whole stone.

Pierced bands and loops. Solids with a tunnel through them, flat-faced toroids, were explored by B. M. Stewart in Adventures Among the Toroids (1964; revised 1980). The octet gives one at once.

Proposition 10.3 (a ring of the octet). In the octet of edge 1 take one layer between two neighbouring triangle planes, and one octahedron in it. The cells of that layer that share an edge with it are six tetrahedra and six octahedra; each shares a whole face with exactly two of the others, and they close up in a single ring of twelve, tetrahedra and octahedra alternating. The octahedron left out has a face in each of the two planes, so the ring is a band with a tunnel through it. The same holds in the octet of any edge.

Proof. Round each of the octahedron's six edges that are not in the two planes the octet has two octahedra and two tetrahedra, alternating; the octahedron's six side faces each carry one tetrahedron of the layer, and the octahedron opposite it across that edge shares a face with the two tetrahedra beside it. Checked exactly with integer coordinates (corners at the points of ℤ³ with even sum; render_jewels.py, study 2026-11-15-filigree): exactly these twelve cells, one cycle of face-sharing, all in the layer. ∎

A necklace or a bracelet is a chain in which solids alternate with links, and the kit gives the links. Between two dodecahedral cells (a dodecahedron, or an icosahedron in its setting) the link is a Dogstar, each joint a roof seated in a hollow (Figures ⟦f-brace⟧ and ⟦f-band⟧(b)); between a tetrahedron and an octahedron no link is needed, the joint is a face (Proposition 10.3); between an icosahedron of edge 1/φ and a dodecahedron the link is the Chain of Karma's shell (Chapter 6); and across a flat plane a CLEO joins an icosahedron face to a dodecahedron corner (#106, Theorem 3; Chapter 9).

Earrings. A pair of earrings is a matched left and right.

Proposition 10.4 (left and right). The icosahedron in its six Admiral Caps has mirror symmetry, but a tetrahedron of edge φ²/√2 seated face to face on one face of its octahedron makes it one-handed: the earring and its mirror image cannot be turned into each other.

Proof. A motion carrying the earring to its mirror image must carry the icosahedron to the icosahedron and the octahedron to the octahedron, so it is a rotation of the octahedron that keeps Pacioli's icosahedron: one of the twelve rotations of the pyritohedral group. These keep apart the two sets of four alternate faces of the octahedron, and a mirror in a coordinate plane exchanges the two sets; so no rotation carries the face that holds the tetrahedron to its mirror image. Checked exactly (twelve rotations found, none carrying the face +++ to −++; the tetrahedron's six edges all exactly φ²/√2; render_jewels.py). ∎

Satellites. The stones need not all sit inside the band. They can sit round it like satellites, each seated face to face where the tiling would put a neighbour, so that every satellite is exact by construction. Four alternate faces of the claw setting give the pendant a classical shape.

Proposition 10.5 (classical). A regular octahedron with a regular tetrahedron of its own edge on each of four alternate faces is a regular tetrahedron of twice the edge. So the icosahedron in its claws, with four tetrahedra of edge φ²/√2 on alternate faces, is a regular tetrahedron of edge φ²√2, the icosahedron showing through its four other faces (checked exactly: the four apexes pairwise φ²√2 apart, and the volumes add up; render_jewels.py).

The nest pendant. The nest of Chapter 11 is a pendant ready made. In every octahedron of the octet the five solids sit one inside the next, O ⊃ I ⊃ D ⊃ C ⊃ T (#99, Theorem 1), and the gaps between neighbours are the settings of §10.3. Drawn see-through, with the four outer stones as crystal shells and the tetrahedron solid at the heart, each stone shows through the ones round it, and with the front half of the gold left out all five show at once (Figure ⟦f-nest⟧).

10.9 What is open

(1) The least metal. Over all 31 mixes the kit stays at seven pieces either way (#47: P, Q, Q′, A, S, X, Y; with the hybrid: P, Q, A, cap, S, R, Z), and at least one is needed (§10.1); the smallest kit is not known, and neither is the least number of pieces for each mix (Table 10.3 counts constructions). (2) Whether one edging piece can do the work of R and Z. (3) Settings at edge 1 for the cube, the tetrahedron and the octahedron that would put them, too, in the golden slab. (4) The same with every piece meeting face to face. (5) Whether every mix fills space by a tiling periodic in three independent directions. (6) Chains whose links are pierced solids threaded through one another, which would need each tunnel to clear the next link's band; no clearance has been checked. (7) A necklace of the golden cycle, each station a bead.

The gold is what lets unlike stones lie together. Chapter 11 finds a lattice that holds all five at once with none: the octet, where each solid nests inside the next and the gold of §10.3 is only the space between them.
