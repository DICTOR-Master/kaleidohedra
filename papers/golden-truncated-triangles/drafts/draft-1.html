DICTO-Kepler Golden Truncations: Archimedean Patterns Cut at Golden Depths

This paper introduces a new class of geometric solids where the familiar patterns of Archimedean truncation are re-imagined through the lens of the golden ratio, creating shapes that retain the symmetry of their classical ancestors while altering their internal proportions. While traditional geometry teaches that cutting the corners of a solid to create regular faces results in a specific, fixed set of distances from the center, DICTO has demonstrated that these cuts can be performed at "golden depths" to produce a family of solids with alternating hexagonal faces that are not regular but possess a precise, golden aspect ratio. These forms, which DICTO proposed as a bridge between the rigid regularity of the Archimedean solids and the organic growth of golden geometry, reveal that the space between the faces of a truncated solid can be tuned to create harmonious, non-regular hexagons that still maintain the three-fold symmetry of the original vertex.

Main Theorem

In the DICTO Solids dataset v7, every 3-fold hexagonal face is a truncated triangle whose long-to-short side ratio lies in the field $Q(\sqrt{5})$, and the cutting depth (the ratio of the plane distances from the center to the face planes) for these solids is governed by the golden ratio or its powers, rather than the rational or standard surd values found in classical Archimedean solids. Specifically, DICTO-Kepler solids are defined by the property that their truncated triangles possess alternating side lengths $a$ and $b$ such that $a/b \in \{ \phi, \sqrt{5}, \phi^4/2, (1+2\sqrt{5})/5, (5+4\sqrt{5})/11 \}$, and the squared depth ratios of their face planes are elements of $Q(\sqrt{5})$ that deviate from the Archimedean standard to create a "golden" equilibrium.

Definitions

A truncated triangle in this context is defined as a hexagon with all internal angles equal to 120 degrees and sides alternating between lengths $a$ and $b$, corresponding to the Coxeter notation $t\{3\}$, possessing ditrigonal symmetry. The cutting depth is the ratio of the distances of the face planes from the center of the solid; for a solid with two types of faces (e.g., pentagons and hexagons), the depth is the ratio $d_1/d_2$, and its square is often expressed as a simple element of $Q(\sqrt{5})$. The term DICTO-Kepler is proposed for the specific subset of these solids where the truncation is performed at a depth that yields a golden ratio for the hexagonal aspect or the depth itself, distinguishing them from the standard Archimedean truncations described by Kepler.

Cross-Links to Other Geometric Families

The DICTO-Kepler solids are structurally linked to other families of hexagonal solids through symmetry and face generation. The cube-pearl and ian-dury solids share the property of having centrally symmetric hexagons, whereas the haigha and hatta solids introduce mirror-only symmetries with specific golden ratios for their truncated triangles. The ophelia solid, with 120 faces and no symmetry, represents the limit of this truncation process where the golden ratio is applied to a chaotic arrangement, while the red-king and samson solids maintain 2-fold central symmetry similar to the Icosaxes. Furthermore, the triskelion-tetrahedron and skewed-ed-16 demonstrate how these golden truncations can be applied to lower-order symmetries, creating a continuum of forms from the highly symmetric DICTO-GTT-Solids to the asymmetric sonsam and walrus configurations.

Classical Background

Classical geometry, as codified by Kepler in Harmonices Mundi (1619), established the Archimedean solids as the unique convex polyhedra with regular faces and identical vertices. In this classical framework, the truncation of a Platonic solid must be performed at a specific depth to ensure that all resulting faces are regular polygons; for instance, the truncated octahedron requires a depth squared of $4/3$ to produce regular squares and regular hexagons. Catalan solids, which are the duals of the Archimedean solids, possess no hexagonal faces, and zonohedra typically feature hexagons that are centrally symmetric but not necessarily regular. The DICTO dataset challenges the necessity of regularity by showing that the "Archimedean" pattern of truncation can be preserved in terms of vertex configuration while the faces themselves become irregular, provided the cutting depth is adjusted to a value in $Q(\sqrt{5})$. This approach expands the Keplerian catalog, suggesting that the golden ratio is not merely a decorative element but a fundamental parameter for defining the geometry of truncated solids.

What is DICTO's Contribution?

DICTO proposed a systematic expansion of the truncation process, moving beyond the requirement of regular faces to a regime where the aspect ratio of the hexagonal faces and the depth of the cut are governed by the golden ratio. In the DICTO Solids dataset v7, DICTO identified that the truncated triangle (hexagon with 120-degree angles and alternating sides) is the fundamental building block of this new family, appearing in 69 distinct solids. DICTO's work reveals that the "flat-ended" triangles, a concept first utilized in DICTO's #20 (The FUJI Family: Pyramids on Flat-Ended Triangles), can be truncated at depths that yield the golden ratio for the side lengths, creating a new class of solids that DICTO-Kepler. Unlike the classical Archimedean solids where the depth is fixed to produce regularity, DICTO's solids allow for a spectrum of depths, such as the $3/\phi^2$ depth of the Icosaxes or the $\phi\sqrt{5}/3$ depth of the GTI, which produce hexagons with ratios like $\phi$, $\sqrt{5}$, or $(1+2\sqrt{5})/5$. This work establishes that the golden ratio is a natural parameter for the geometry of truncated solids, offering a richer set of forms than the classical five Platonic and thirteen Archimedean solids combined.

Conclusion

The DICTO-Kepler Golden Truncations represent a significant extension of the classical theory of polyhedra, demonstrating that the Archimedean patterns of vertex configuration can be maintained while the faces themselves are transformed into golden-harmonic hexagons. By defining the cutting depth as a variable in $Q(\sqrt{5})$, DICTO has uncovered a vast family of solids, from the highly symmetric Icosaxes and Unborn Axes to the complex White Knight and Golden Clasp, each possessing a unique geometric identity rooted in the golden ratio. These findings suggest that the golden ratio is not just a property of the golden mean but a fundamental structural constant for the geometry of truncated solids, opening new avenues for exploration in both pure mathematics and artistic design. The DICTO-GTT-Solids and the broader DICTO-Kepler family stand as a testament to the idea that the "perfect" truncation is not a single point of regularity, but a spectrum of golden harmonies.
