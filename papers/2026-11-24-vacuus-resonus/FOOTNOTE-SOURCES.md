## (a) śūnya = "empty", and the word for zero

- S1, p. 1085: "śūnya mf(ā)n. empty, void ..., hollow, barren, desolate, deserted"; and, as a neuter noun, "(in arithm.) nought, a cypher, VarBṛS.; Gaṇit." The same entry gives "(in phil.) vacuity, nonentity, absolute nonexistence (esp. with Buddhists)".
- S2, notes to Bhāskara's Līlāvatī (Chapter I, on notation): "the place, when none belongs to it, being shown by a blank (sunya); which, to obviate mistake, is denoted by a dot or small circle."

Supported: yes (dictionary + a 19th-century translator's note).

## (b) Brahmagupta's rules for zero

- S3 (biography): Brahmagupta "wrote Brahmasphutasiddhanta (Correctly Established Doctrine of Brahma), in 628. The work was written in 25 chapters"; "he defined zero as the result of subtracting a number from itself."
- S2, Brahmegupta, Chapter XVIII (Cuttaca, algebra), Section II "Algorithm", pp. 339–340, Colebrooke's §31–36 (Colebrooke renders the Sanskrit word as "cipher"):
  - §31: "The sum of cipher and negative is negative; of affirmative and nought is positive; of two ciphers is cipher."
  - §32–33: "Negative, taken from cipher, becomes positive; and affirmative, becomes negative. Negative, less cipher, is negative; positive, is positive; cipher, nought."
  - §34: "The product of cipher and negative, or of cipher and affirmative, is nought; of two ciphers, is cipher."
  - §35–36: "Cipher, divided by cipher, is nought. ... Positive, or negative, divided by cipher, is a fraction with that for denominator: or cipher divided by negative or affirmative." [36 concl.] "The square of negative or affirmative is positive; of cipher, is cipher."

Citation: Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (628 CE), chapter 18, the rules numbered §31–36 in Colebrooke (1817), pp. 339–340. Modern editions number these verses 18.30–35 or so; that numbering was not checked here, so cite Colebrooke's. That the Sanskrit word in these verses is śūnya was not checked in the Sanskrit; S1 attests śūnya = nought in mathematical texts (Gaṇit.). So the footnote should say "the word for zero" generally, not quote Brahmagupta's Sanskrit.

## (c) śūnyatā in the Heart Sutra

- S4, p. 18 (the Hōryūji palm-leaf text, leaf 2, romanised with an interlinear gloss): "rûpam sûnyatâ sûnyataiva rûpam", glossed "colour empty empty indeed colour" (the scan's OCR is damaged; the line was read through it).
- S4, p. 55, Müller's translation (of the larger version): "There are five Skandhas, and these he considered as by their nature empty. Form is emptiness, and emptiness indeed is form. Emptiness is not different from form, form is not different from emptiness."
- In IAST: rūpaṃ śūnyatā śūnyataiva rūpam, "form is emptiness, emptiness itself is form".

"The key term" is a judgement; the sources support "a central term" (the line above is the sutra's best-known statement). The footnote below avoids "the key term".

## (d) Nattier 1992

- S5, p. 173: the pattern of agreement with the Chinese, "combined with an equally striking difference in vocabulary, can only be explained as the result of a back-translation - that is, by the translation of the Sanskrit Heart Sutra from the Chinese."
- S5, p. 181–182: "Hsüan-tsang must remain the most likely candidate for the transmission of this Chinese creation to India."
- S5, p. 199 (conclusion): the sutra as "created as a separate scripture in China, composed of an extract from the Large Sutra of Kumarajiva ... together with an introduction and conclusion composed in China".

Careful wording: Nattier argues the Chinese text came first and the Sanskrit is a back-translation; it is an argument, still debated, not a settled fact.

## Proposed footnote (2 sentences)

> Sanskrit *śūnya*, "empty", is also the word for zero in Indian mathematics, where Brahmagupta's *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* (628 CE) gives rules for adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing with it (ch. 18; Colebrooke 1817, pp. 339–340). Its abstract noun *śūnyatā*, "emptiness", is a central term of the Heart Sutra ("rūpaṃ śūnyatā śūnyataiva rūpam", "form is emptiness, emptiness is form"; Müller and Nanjio 1884), a text whose Sanskrit version Nattier (1992) argues was translated from a Chinese original.

References for the footnote: Colebrooke 1817 (S2); Müller and Nanjio 1884 (S4); Nattier 1992, DOI 10.2143/JIABS.15.2.3286001 (S5); optionally Monier-Williams 1899, p. 1085 (S1).
