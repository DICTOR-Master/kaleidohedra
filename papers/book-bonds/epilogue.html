Epilogue. From the walk to the law

The book began with a question of filling. Chapter 1 showed that every one of the 31 mixes of the Platonic solids (dodecahedron, icosahedron, cube, tetrahedron, octahedron) fills space, all at one edge length, with a kit of seven connector pieces; the cube alone and the tetrahedron with the octahedron need none. The connectors were the first sign of the theme: where solids do not fit, something has to fill the gaps. Chapters 2 and 3 gave exact moves that make shapes rather than glue them: a slide that keeps every edge equal (Chapter 2), and three moves, a shear, the equal-edged cell and a stretch, one setting of which gives each of five classical space-fillers exactly, corner by corner (Chapter 3).

Part II cut the gaps instead of plugging them. In the simple cubic lattice of icosahedra, cutting every AXE from the point 1/φ of the way along its axis turns the icosahedra and the Unborns into two convex space-fillers, the Icosaxes and the Unborn Axes, and no other cut point makes both convex (Chapter 4); in the Lotus Jewel network the same cut gives the keyed pair. One flat cut slid through the octet runs from Kepler's rhombic dodecahedra to cubes, and at one point the two pieces have equal volume, Samson and Delilah (Chapter 5).

Part III found the one dial behind the families. The golden and the Delian loops are each the weighted Voronoi (power) diagram of one fixed set of points of two kinds, with one weight slid between the kinds. Read as a web of bonds, shared faces between neighbours, the families change in only two ways: the bond between unlike pieces never breaks inside a loop, and the like bonds hand over, gradually in the golden loop and all at once, at the octet, in the Delian loop (Chapter 6). Read physically, the weight is a ball radius squared, and the range of touching balls is bounded by Pauling's radius-ratio limits (Chapter 6). The same two hand-overs recur in ten further crystal networks (Chapter 7).

Part IV followed the walk to new pairs. In A15, one exact weight gives Goliath, the hull of Jessen's orthogonal icosahedron, with David (Chapter 8). On the twinned octet the walk has its own equal-volume point, Sonsam and Lahdeli, and keys change the pieces but never the web (Chapter 9). Chapter 10 returned to the golden pair: twenty Icosaxes slid together close into one solid, the Icosaxes Seed, and eight Icosaxes round one Unborn Axes make the blocks of a network. The golden, keyed, Delian and twinned Delian pairs are the four DICTONIC bonds: each sits at one distinguished point, the golden cut or the point of equal volume, of a family of pairs that fills space all along.

Part V told the walk as one route (Chapter 11) and then turned the dial on every close-packed stacking at once. The two pure stackings are the octet of Chapter 5 (cubic) and the twinned octet of Chapter 9 (hexagonal), and Chapter 12 proves that these are the only ingredients: along the whole weight walk, a tetrahedral hole takes the piece of the pure stacking of its nearest layer, and an octahedral hole's piece is two halves, each borrowed from the pure stacking named by the layer on its side. So the pieces of all stackings together are finite in kind, 21 combinatorial kinds listed with exact volumes, and an octahedral piece's volume is the mean of its two parents'. Where a cubic layer meets a hexagonal one, the halves meet in the Janus pieces, Chucky and Fang. This is the Rule of Halves: however the layers are stacked, every piece is borrowed, whole or in half, from two pure orders.
