In three dimensions only the cube fills space alone, and of the pairs only the tetrahedron with the octahedron (Part Two). One dimension up the room curves: the cells of a four-dimensional polytope fill the three-dimensional sphere that bounds it, and there the five behave differently.

These are the six regular 4-polytopes (Schläfli; Coxeter 1973). No regular 4-polytope has icosahedral cells: the icosahedron's dihedral angle, 138.2°, is too wide: three of them round an edge already exceed 360°, so they cannot close up even in curved space.

11.2 The icosahedron needs the tetrahedron

Proposition 11.1 (Gosset 1900; Conway and Guy 1965). Remove from the 600-cell's 120 corners the 24 corners of an inscribed 24-cell. The other 96 corners span the snub 24-cell, whose cells are 24 regular icosahedra and 120 regular tetrahedra.

Checked by building the 600-cell's corners, removing those of its 24-cell, and taking the hull of the rest: 24 icosahedra and 120 tetrahedra, every one regular; the full 600-cell gives 600 tetrahedra (snub24.py). So the icosahedra are carved out of the White Queen: each sits round one removed corner, where twenty of the 600-cell's tetrahedra met.
