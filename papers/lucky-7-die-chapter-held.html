9. The fair fourteen-face die

A die is fair when every face is equally likely. The sphere is the perfect die: every direction lands equally. A cube is fair because its symmetry carries any face to any other; Diaconis and Keller call such dice fair by symmetry and show that a die can also be fair by continuity: cut a fair die, let the cut grow, and the faces' chances pass through equality [22]. This section finds that point exactly on one line of solids and reads its Vacuus. The answer is heptagonal: the fair die's numbers are the angles of the regular heptagon.

The line. As in #90 [28], S(μ) = [−1, 1]³ ∩ {|x| + |y| + |z| ≤ μ} runs from the octahedron (μ = 1) through Kelvin's truncated octahedron (3/2) and the cuboctahedron (2) to the cube (3). For 1 < μ < 2 it has 6 squares and 8 hexagons, corners the 24 permutations of (±1, ±b, 0), b = μ − 1. The model of a roll is the solid-angle model: a face's chance is the solid angle it subtends from the centre of mass, divided by 4π, the rule Rawlins uses for fair dice of 5 to 11 faces [23]. The solid angles are computed by Van Oosterom and Strackee's formula [27].

Theorem D (the fair 14-face die). Exact. Let c = cos(2π/7) and T = tan(π/14)·tan(2π/7).
(a) Exactly one member of the line has all 14 faces subtending 4π/14: μf = 1 + √(2T) = 1.7565821359…, with T = 9 − 4c − 16c², a root of x³ − 9x² − x + 1, and μf a root of μ⁶ − 6μ⁵ − 3μ⁴ + 52μ³ − 97μ² + 74μ − 13 (irreducible).
(b) b² = 2T = 1/c² − 2. Hence each of its 12 sloping Vacuus plates has unit normal (c, c, bc) up to symmetry, at angle 2π/7 to two cube axes, and the ratio of its inradius to its circumradius is r/R = cot(2π/7) = tan(3π/14) = 0.797473….
(c) Its 14 landing directions, equally weighted, are the 14 corner directions of the rhombic dodecahedron, the corner-first shadow of a 24-cell inscribed in the 600-cell. The solid itself is not a shadow, section or corner subset, up to affine maps, of any polytope with coordinates in a field of degree 2k under a map defined over that field.
Proof. (a) On 1 < μ ≤ 2 a square subtends 4·arctan(b²/(2√(1 + b²))), which rises strictly from 0 to 4·arctan(1/(2√2)) > 4π/14; on 2 ≤ μ < 3 the square grows into an octagon as nested sets and its share rises on to 1/6. So the crossing is unique and lies at b²/(2√(1 + b²)) = tan(π/14), that is b⁴ − 4t²b² − 4t² = 0 with t = tan(π/14), so b² = 2t(t + sec(π/14)) = 2t·tan(π/4 + π/28) = 2T. The expression of T in c and the minimal polynomials are by exact algebra. (b) c is a root of 8c³ + 4c² − 4c − 1. Then c²(2T + 2) − 1 = c²(20 − 8c − 32c²) − 1 = 20c² − 8c³ − 32c⁴ − 1, and reducing 8c³ and 32c⁴ by that polynomial gives 0; so 2T = 1/c² − 2. The square–hexagon edge from (1, b, 0) to (1, 0, b) spans with the centre the plate with normal (b², −b, −b) ∝ (b, −1, −1), of length √(2 + b²) = 1/c; up to symmetry the unit normal is (c, c, bc). The squares lie at distance 1 and the hexagons at μf/√3 = 1.0142, so r = 1; R² = 1 + b² = (1 − c²)/c², so r/R = c/√(1 − c²) = cot(2π/7). (c) At μf the 14 face normals carry equal weight: the 6 axis directions and the 8 diagonals (±1, ±1, ±1), the corner directions of the rhombic dodecahedron. Projecting the 24-cell's corners (±1, 0, 0, 0) and (±½, ±½, ±½, ±½) along the fourth axis gives exactly these directions, and the 24-cell's corners are corners of the 600-cell. For the solid: the parallel hexagon edges (0, −b, b) and (0, 1 − b, b − 1) have length ratio b/(1 − b), kept by every affine map, and of degree 6 over ℚ. A corner subset, a section by a flat over a field K, or a projection along a direction over K, of a polytope with corners in K has its ratios of parallel edges in K; a field of degree 2k has no element of degree 6. ∎

So the die cannot be cut or cast from the Queens over their own fields (ℚ(√5) and its relatives cover the 600-cell and the 120-cell), but the way it lands is a shadow of the White Queen. Projections along directions not defined over the field are not covered by (c).

Its Vacuus. Exact. The die is centrally symmetric, so it is of the heart kind (Lemma 1). Its 36 edges give 36 fins in two kinds: 24 square–hexagon fins with apex angle 50.5085° and 12 hexagon–hexagon fins with apex angle 15.7790°; neither apex is a rational multiple of π (2cos of the apex angles has minimal polynomials 7z³ − 70z² + 84z − 8 and 49z⁶ + 10976z⁴ − 50176z² + 32768, neither monic). The fins lie on 15 plates, all full sheets: the 3 coordinate planes, each with 4 hexagon–hexagon fins, and the 12 sloping plates of Theorem D (b), each with 2 square–hexagon fins. In the language of §2, with the cube as host, the die is partly hidden: 3 of its 15 plates are walls of the cube (its coordinate mirrors) and the 12 sloping plates are not, leaning at the heptagon's angle 2π/7.

No heptagonal Queen. Exact. Since the die's field is the heptagon's, we asked whether the construction of §2 runs with the heptagon in place of the pentagon. With the heptagon's side and diagonals 1, ρ = 2cos(π/7) and σ = ρ² − 1 as the gaps between sorted coordinates in the sum-zero space of ℝ⁷, exactly one pair of S7-orbits shares a sphere, as A and B do: Ah = perms(0, ρ, ρσ, σ², σ², σ², σ²) with 210 corners and Bh = perms(0, 1, 1, ρ², ρ², σ², σ²) with 630, Bh's gaps being Ah's in reverse order, as in the golden pair. But there is no hidden symmetry to complete them: the reflections in the symmetry group G of any S7-invariant set of sum-zero ℝ⁷ generate a finite reflection group of rank 6 containing W(A6), which by Coxeter's classification [25] is W(A6) itself (B6, D6 and E6 have orders not divisible by 5040, and H3, H4 stop at rank 4); G normalises it, so G ⊆ W(A6) × {±1}. The hull of ±Ah ∪ ±Bh (1680 corners, f-vector 1680 / 20160 / 62020 / 78750 / 42938 / 7728) has only the 21 mirrors of S7 as walls. The pentagon has its Queen because W(A4) lies inside W(H4); the heptagon has none. Its field rules the fair die and the truncated cube out of every shadow and section over ℚ(c).

Against the sphere. Table 5 scores the dice against the sphere, the perfect value: the isoperimetric quotient 36πV²/S³ and the roundness r/R (sphere 1), and the landing isotropy Q4, Q6 of the face normals weighted by solid angle (sphere 0; Steinhardt, Nelson and Ronchetti's invariants [24]). Of these dice the fair die is the roundest by r/R, second to the icosahedron by isoperimetric quotient, and its isoperimetric quotient is 99.96% of the line's maximum 0.781640 at μ = √3, where the faces are not fair (squares 0.0678, hexagons 0.0742). Its landing Q4 is √(7/12)/21, the bcc value.

Rolling and tiling. Computed. A quasi-static rolling model (the die lands with a random down-direction and rolls downhill without bouncing, after Várkonyi and Domokos [29]) agrees with the solid-angle model at five points of the line within about two standard errors (N = 80 000); at μf each square comes up 0.07136 ± 0.00029 against 1/14 = 0.07143. Bouncing is not modelled, and real dice depend on the table [22]. Exact. The die does not fill space alone: its Dehn invariant is 24√2(3 − 2μf) ⊗ arccos(1/√3) ≠ 0, and every space-filler has Dehn invariant zero (Debrunner [26]). With its partner S(3 − μf) it fills space as a CsCl pair, as every member of the line does (#90 [28]).

Credit and prior art. The existence of a fair member of this line is an instance of Diaconis and Keller's continuity argument [22]; the equal-solid-angle rule is Rawlins's [23], whose examples have 5, 7, 9, 10 and 11 faces. Not found in the sources checked (Appendix A): the exact value μf, its uniqueness on the line, the heptagonal identities of Theorem D (b), and the die's Vacuus.
