Knitted Octet

Which knitting and related textile techniques can make a fabric whose own construction is the octet lattice in three dimensions?

DICTO · dictospheres.com · 2 November 2026 · research note 2026-11-02-knitted-octet · text only · not published

DICTO proposed a knitted fabric whose loops and the way they connect are the octet structure: the face-centred cubic (close-packed) lattice of balls, each ball touching 12 others, with the space between them divided into tetrahedra and octahedra. The fabric must not carry a picture of the lattice, and must not be a flat slice of it.

Verdict. An exact knitted octet is achievable at the scale of struts, not at the scale of single stitches. (1) By hand: a network of knitted tubes (struts) joined at nodes gives the octet's connections exactly; its geometry is only approximate unless the tubes are stiffened. (2) By machine: a spacer fabric (two knitted faces joined by yarns that cross the gap) has the same build as one octet slab, which is two close-packed layers of balls with the tetrahedra and octahedra between them. Stacking slabs gives the full 3D lattice. This is the most promising route to a true fabric. (3) Stitch by stitch: none of the techniques checked gives each stitch 12 neighbours. Ordinary knitting is two-dimensional in its connections, so at this scale the octet is only approximate. In the sources checked we found no precedent for a knitted octet. The nearest precedents are woven-wire octet and Kagome trusses and crocheted single polyhedra.

1. What has to be matched

Our check (vector arithmetic; no source needed beyond [L1–L3]) In the octet, each node has 12 neighbours along 6 directions, the face diagonals of the cube. Every one of those directions is a straight line through the nodes, so the whole octet is six families of straight parallel lines, with six lines crossing at every node (6 lines × 2 sides = 12 neighbours). Take any close-packed layer: 3 of the 6 directions lie inside it (6 neighbours in the layer), and the other 3 tilt out of it at 54.7° (3 neighbours above and 3 below). Seen from above, the bonds between two neighbouring layers form a honeycomb (hexagon) net. Per node there are 2 tetrahedral holes and 1 octahedral hole. The smallest repeat unit is the 60° rhombohedron, which is 1 octahedron plus 2 tetrahedra: 8 corners and 18 equal struts, with volume a³/√2 for strut length a.

From [4, 5, 6] A knitted sheet is different. Its yarn is a curve in 3D, but its connections form a square grid on a surface: a stitch is linked to 2 neighbours along its row (the yarn runs on) and to 2 along its column (each loop is pulled through the one below). This is the "knit graph" of machine-knitting research [6]. Markande and Matsumoto treat knits and other 2-periodic textiles as tangles in a thickened torus [4]: a sheet, periodic in two directions only. Poincloux et al. model a knit's shape and stretch from yarn bending, fixed total yarn length and the topological constraints between neighbouring stitches [5]. A true octet therefore needs a third kind of link, between layers, and a way to get 6 in-layer neighbours instead of 4.

Metal, not knitted.
Crochet [19, 25, 26]Taimina's hyperbolic crochet controls curvature by adding stitches [25]. At an octet node the skin of a strut frame is saddle-shaped (our reading), so the same increases would shape the junction. From [27, 28] Mechanics: the stretch of a knit depends on the topology of its stitches, not only on the yarn [27]. Each part (struts, faces, spacer yarns) can therefore be given its own stiffness by choosing its stitch. Spacer yarns are usually stiffer than the face yarns [8]. Library item [L3] (Lalvani, NASA 1990) counts the parts of layered octahedral–tetrahedral trusses, which are the same two-layer slab as a spacer fabric.

3. First small hand-knittable test piece

Test piece: the Knitted Rhombohedron (placeholder name). This is the octet's smallest repeat unit: one octahedron with a tetrahedron on two opposite faces, together a 60° rhombohedron. It has 8 nodes and 18 struts, all the same length. The two tip nodes take 3 struts each and the six octahedron nodes take 5 each, so no junction needs more than 5 branches. Knit each strut as an i-cord of the same stitch count in a firm, low-stretch yarn (for example cotton or linen) on small needles. Join the struts at the nodes by picking up stitches, and graft the struts that close loops. Stiffen the piece afterwards if it sags. The test answers three questions: whether the struts stay equal, whether the angles at a 5-way junction come out right, and how much stiffening is needed. Copies of the unit placed side by side fill space, so a second unit tests a face shared between cells. The next step is the 12-way node: one ball with its 12 neighbours, whose outer struts form a cuboctahedron (13 nodes, 36 struts).

Conclusion. Make the hand test as a strut network. Make the real fabric as stacked spacer slabs whose two faces are open triangular nets, one pile-yarn set for each tilted bond direction, with neighbouring slabs sharing a face. Status: research note; no physical test yet.
