# History: the five elements and the gorintō

The gorintō (五輪塔, five-ring pagoda) is a memorial and sacred object: a stack of five stones, read from the bottom as earth (cube), water (sphere), fire (pyramid or triangular roof), wind (half-moon) and space (jewel shape) (JAANUS, "gorintou"). This section traces the idea of five elements behind it. Where scholars disagree, both views are given; the disagreement is not settled here.

## 1. India: five great elements

The Upaniṣads name five "great elements" (mahābhūta). Taittirīya Upaniṣad 2.1 gives them as a sequence of emergence: "From this very self did aether come into being; from aether, air; from air, fire; from fire, water, from water, the earth" (trans. Olivelle 1996, as quoted in Wikipedia, "Mahābhūta").

Early Buddhist texts more often speak of four great elements: "the four Great Elements (Pali: *cattāro mahābhūtāni*) are earth, water, fire and air" (Wikipedia, "Mahābhūta"). Space and consciousness appear as further elements in some discourses. The Dhātuvibhaṅga Sutta (MN 140) lists six: "These are the six properties: the earth property, the liquid property, the fire property, the wind property, the space property, the consciousness property" (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu, Access to Insight).

## 2. Greece: four roots, five solids, a fifth body

Empedocles, born in the early fifth century BCE, "pioneered the influential four-part theory of roots (air, water, earth, and fire)" (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Empedocles").

In Plato's *Timaeus* the elements receive solid shapes: "To earth, then, let us assign the cubical form", and "the pyramid is the solid which is the original element and seed of fire"; air and water take the next two figures, which commentators identify as the octahedron and icosahedron (trans. Jowett, Project Gutenberg). Of the remaining figure Plato writes at 55c: "There was yet a fifth combination which God used in the delineation of the universe" (Jowett). The text does not name it; it is usually identified with the dodecahedron.

Aristotle, *On the Heavens* I.2–3, argues for "some bodily substance other than the formations we know, prior to them all and more divine than they", and notes that earlier thinkers "gave the highest place a name of its own, aither" (trans. Stocks, MIT Internet Classics Archive). This fifth body is the later "quintessence".

## 3. Contact between Greece and India: the debate

Contact was possible. The Achaemenid Persian empire held the Greek cities of Ionia and, from the late sixth century BCE, reached "the western Indus basin" (Wikipedia, "Achaemenid Empire"). After Alexander, Greco-Bactrian and Indo-Greek kingdoms ruled at India's borders. The Indo-Greek king Menander I (c. 165/155–130 BCE) appears in the Milindapañha, a Buddhist dialogue with the monk Nāgasena (Wikipedia, "Menander I"; "Greco-Buddhism"). Gandhāran art shows clear Greek influence; Wikipedia ("Greco-Buddhism") reports that "Greek artists were most probably the authors" of early Buddha images.

Whether ideas such as the elements travelled is disputed. Thomas McEvilley, *The Shape of Ancient Thought* (2002), argues for wide exchange between Greek and Indian thought through trade, empire and migration (Wikipedia, "The Shape of Ancient Thought"). A reviewer, Emily Kearns (*The Classical Review*, 2004), found the book "rarely overstated" and noted that it allows that some parallels may be coincidences (as summarised in the same article). On the sceptical side, Wikipedia ("Greco-Buddhism") states that "the majority of scholars do not assume a noticeable Greek influence on Gandharan Buddhism beyond the artistic realm", and the general article "Classical element" records only parallel lists in several cultures, without claiming transmission. *Uncertain:* no free source checked here argues specifically against transmission of the element lists; the sceptical view above concerns doctrine in general.

## 4. From esoteric Buddhism to the gorintō in Japan

Kūkai (774–835) "arrived back in Japan in 806" from Tang China (Wikipedia, "Kūkai") and founded Shingon. Japanese Wikipedia ("両界曼荼羅") states that he received the Two-Realm mandalas from his teacher Huiguo (恵果) and brought them home. *Uncertain:* that article places this in the Kōnin era (弘仁年間), which does not match the 806 return given elsewhere; the year of transfer is taken here from the English article. In his *Sokushin jōbutsu gi* (即身成仏義) Kūkai sets out the six great elements (六大): the five plus consciousness (識), summed up in the verse "六大無碍にして常に瑜伽なり" (Japanese Wikipedia, "即身成仏").

The gorintō appears in the late Heian period. Japanese Wikipedia ("五輪塔") gives the oldest dated stone examples as Kasugayama, Nara (1157); Chūsonji Shakuson-in, Hiraizumi (1169); and the Nakao Kaō tower, Usuki (1170). The same article notes that Kakuban (1095–1143) and his *Gorin kuji myō himitsushaku* (五輪九字明秘密釈) are often cited as the origin, but "この著以前に五輪塔は出現しており" (gorintō had already appeared before this work), so treating it as the origin "は適切でない" (is not appropriate). The article says current opinion holds that the form was devised in Japan from descriptions in esoteric scriptures.

The rings carry seed syllables, from the bottom: a, va (ba), ra, ha (ka), kha (kya) (Japanese Wikipedia, "五輪塔"; English Wikipedia, "Gorintō"). According to the scriptures, "地輪は黄、水輪は白、火輪は赤、風輪は黒", and the space ring contains all colours; painted wooden gorintō are often coloured this way, "空輪は青に塗る" (the space ring painted blue) (Japanese Wikipedia, "五輪塔").

## 5. China: a different five

The Chinese wuxing (五行) is "a fivefold conceptual scheme", translated "Five Phases or Five Agents" (Wikipedia, "Wuxing"). Its members are wood, fire, earth, metal and water, with no air or space, and it describes processes rather than substances. It is a separate system from the Indian and Buddhist five.
