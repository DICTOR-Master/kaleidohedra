# DICTO Golayantra: the classical gola-yantra and the DICTO Yantra as rings

DICTO · dictospheres.com · 9 November 2026

DICTO proposed the Golayantra name and the question whether there is mathematics in the classical gola-yantra.
DICTO proposed the polar reciprocal form (Part 3).

## Findings first

**Part 1 (history).** Bhāskara I's gola-yantra (629 CE) is two concentric shells of equal-size wooden or bamboo rings on one iron rod, with a clay earth at the centre. The inner shell (bhagola) turns: equator (60 ghaṭikā marks), two rings through the poles, the ecliptic fixed at E and W at 24°, a movable moon's orbit at 4½°, and six movable day circles. The outer shell (khagola) stays fixed: meridian, prime vertical, horizon and six o'clock ring, with the rod re-seated at the local latitude. Three kinds of joint cut appear, and three-ring joints use one-third cuts. Bhāskara II adds a wooden polar axis, nested planet spheres, a third "double sphere" (dṛggola), corner verticals, a movable azimuth circle, and a spider-web of planet orbits.

**Part 2 (mathematics).** On the spherical DICTO Yantra (GF1), the 27 sides lie on exactly **27 distinct great circles (rings)**, for every cap radius ρ. Gnomonic projection is a projective map, so the count and every incidence are independent of ρ; only the crossing angles change. The rings meet in **32 antipodal pairs of points where three or more rings meet**:
- the 18 marmas (9 mirror pairs)
- 6 corner touches
- 7 apex-on-base points
- one 9-ring hub: all nine bases pass through the two opposite points ±X

There are **no hidden triple points**, behind the sphere or anywhere else on the full rings: every other triple misses by at least |det| = 2·10⁻⁴. The nearest miss is L6 against L8∩R5, off by 0.017° at ρ = 60° (0.0007 yantra diameters in the plane). It is not forced.

The natural frame is:
- **Polar axis:** the base hub ±X. The nine base rings are meridians through it, at longitudes λᵢ = arctan(2 tan ρ (½ − bᵢ)).
- **Equator:** the image of the yantra's symmetry line (the ring x = 0). It meets every base at exactly 90°, and all nine apexes lie on it, so every left/right leg pair is a pair of "ecliptics" with their nodes at the apex.
- **Horizon:** the ring z = 0, the image of the line at infinity, which acts as a tenth meridian.

Bhāskara I's three-ring equinox joint (equator, colure, ecliptic, one-third cuts) has an exact counterpart in the seven apex-on-base joints (left leg, right leg, base meridian; four rings if the equator ring is built). For the Cleo-sphere cap, tan ρ = 1/φ² exactly (ρ = 20.9052°).

**Part 3 (polar reciprocal, the antipol).** In the polar form the 27 rings become 54 beads (the poles) and the 32 joints become 32 threads (great circles). All 18 marma alignments (each marma's three rings give six beads, three antipodal pairs, on one great circle) are exact. There are no further alignments: exactly 115 collinear pole triples, equal to the dual of the joint list. The equator ring x = 0 carries all 18 base beads, because it is the polar of the hub ±X. The seven apex threads are meridians through ±X. Each polar triangle has sides of 180° minus the angles of its triangle. All nine polar triangles contain the core cap of radius 90° − ρ, which is the dual of the yantra lying in its cap. t3 and t7 **circumscribe** the core circle (all three sides tangent), and t1 and t9 touch it twice. This is the dual of corners lying on the circle: inscribed ↔ circumscribed.

**New?** As far as the sources checked go, nobody has published the Śrī Yantra drawn on a sphere as a ring armillary, its ring count, its ρ-invariance, the equator/hub frame, or the polar bead-and-thread form. The incidence facts follow directly from projective geometry (gnomonic projection keeps lines and concurrences). What is new here is the observation, the exact census (27 rings, 32 joints, no hidden ones) and the build. None of this is a deep theorem.

## Part 1: the classical gola-yantra

Main source: Peng Lu, "Bhāskara I on the Construction of the Armillary Sphere", *History of Science in South Asia* 3 (2015) 1–19, doi:10.18732/H2MW2W. Lu translates Bhāskara I's commentary on the Āryabhaṭīya (BAB, 629 CE) from K. S. Shukla's 1976 critical edition, pp. 240–243, in paragraphs ¶1–¶21 (the paragraph numbers are Lu's).

### Stages of assembly (Bhāskara I's text)
| ¶ | Stage |
|---|---|
| 3 | Carpenters (krākacika) make a ring like a wheel from Śrīparṇī (*Gmelina arborea*) or Vañcula timber, out of two half rings or three segments. |
| 4 | Three joint cuts (cheda): śarapuṅkha ("arrow-shaft"), pārśvaccheda ("side cut"), avayavārdhaccheda ("half of the component"). |
| 5 | Ring 1 runs E–W and ring 2 runs S–N, crossing (svastika) above and below. They join with a half cut (ardhaccheda) so the joint is as thick as one ring, and are fixed with copper nails (tāmrakīlaka). |
| 6 | Ring 3 goes round "like a girdle" (parikara), meeting the first two at the four cardinal points, with half cuts. |
| 7 | The E–W ring gets 60 marks (15 per quarter: the ghaṭikās of day and night) and is the **equator (viṣuvat)**. The other two get 360 marks each. |
| 8 | Ring 4, the same size and marked 360, is joined at the E and W intersections with an oblique **one-third cut** (tribhāgaccheda), so that three rings meet with the thickness of one. |
| 9 | Ring 4 crosses the S–N ring 24° north of the lower intersection and 24° south of the upper one. |
| 10 | Ring 4 is the **ecliptic (apamaṇḍala)**, "the circle of the zodiac signs placed obliquely". All joints are nailed with copper. |
| 11–12 | A **movable (sañcārin)** ring is tied at the moon's node, 4½° from the ecliptic at 90° from the node on both sides. This is the moon's orbit (**vimaṇḍala**, also vikṣepa-maṇḍala). Other planets get their own rings at their nodes; for Mercury and Venus the rings are made according to their śīghroccas. |
| 13 | **Day circles (svāhorātra-maṇḍala)**, also movable, are placed north of the equator at the declination of the end of Aries, Taurus and Gemini; Cancer, Leo and Virgo reuse them in reverse order. The same three go south for Libra to Pisces, so there are 6 small circles. |
| 14 | Threads (sūtra) stretched S–N on the day circles show the declination chords, using the six o'clock circle (unmaṇḍala). Not all rings are shown at once, or the sphere could not be turned. |
| 15 | A smooth, straight **iron rod** (ayaḥśalākā) with cow's-tail-shaped ends goes through the S and N intersections and carries the whole inner shell (pañjara). A **clay** ball (the earth) sits at its middle. One shell serves all planets, or each planet gets its own shell. |
| 16 | Reed stalks (śara-daṇḍikā), 3–4 aṅgulas long, fixed on the rod act as spacers. The **outer ring kha-kakṣyā** ("orbit of the sky") runs S–N, has two holes half a circumference apart, and is threaded onto the rod. |
| 17 | **Sama-maṇḍala** (prime vertical, E–W) and **kṣitija-maṇḍala** (horizon, as a girdle, with holes at S and N). At this stage the sphere is set for the terrestrial equator. |
| 18 | New holes are drilled at the local latitude above N and below S, and the rod is moved into them: the sphere is now set for the locality. |
| 19 | The **unmaṇḍala** (six o'clock ring), the same size as the outer shell, is threaded on the rod at N and S and fixed at E and W with the oblique cut. Every ring is marked with 360 divisions. |
| 20 | Variant: some sink the lower half of the sphere in a pit so that the horizon ring lies at ground level. |
| 21 | Material: wood, or rings of dry split **bamboo** (vaṃśa) with a narrow hollow channel. |

### The classical ring pattern
| Ring | Shell | Placement | Marks |
|---|---|---|---|
| viṣuvat (equator) | inner, fixed to the rod frame | perpendicular to the rod | 60 ghaṭikās |
| S–N ring and girdle ring (named yāmyottara and kṣitija in Shukla's brackets at ¶7) | inner | both through the poles, 90° apart (in effect the two colures) | 360 |
| apamaṇḍala (ecliptic) | inner, fixed | through the E and W points of the equator (three-ring joint, ⅓ cuts); 24° to the equator | 360 |
| vimaṇḍala (moon's orbit) and planet rings | inner, movable | 4½° to the ecliptic, tied at the node | 360 |
| svāhorātra (day circles) ×6 | inner, movable | small circles parallel to the equator at δ = arcsin(sin 24° · sin λ) for λ = 30°, 60°, 90°: 11.7°, 20.6°, 24° N and S (the values are computed here; the text gives the rule) | — |
| kha-kakṣyā (meridian) | outer, fixed | S–N, carries the rod holes (latitude) | 360 |
| sama-maṇḍala (prime vertical) | outer | E–W through zenith and nadir | 360 |
| kṣitija (horizon) | outer | girdle through the four cardinal points | 360 |
| unmaṇḍala (six o'clock ring) | outer | through the celestial poles and the E and W points | 360 |
| iron rod, clay earth | axis | through both shells | — |

All rings are the same size within a shell. The outer shell is larger, separated by the reed spacers.

### Text versus reconstruction
- **Bhāskara I's text:** every ring, angle, material, cut name and stage above.
- **Shukla's insertions:** the bracketed words in the Sanskrit, including the names yāmyottara and kṣitija in ¶7 and "dakṣiṇa" in ¶9.
- **Lu's reconstruction:**
  - the drawings of the three cuts, which Lu calls imaginary because the terms appear in no other text (fn. 11)
  - the 3D figures, drawn by Wei Liu
  - the reading of the "cow's-tail" rod ends as swellings (fn. 21)
  - the assumption of a self-rotating device (fn. 20, after Āryabhaṭa's globe)
  - the small Mercury and Venus rings in the final figure
  - the two-shell summary, using the later names bhagola and khagola
- **Mine:** the drawing `figures/classical-golayantra.png` is a fresh reconstruction from the text. The latitude of Ujjayinī (23.2°), the hour angle and the node position are my choices.

### Lalla and Bhāskara II (only from sources read)
- **Lalla (8th c., Śiṣyadhīvṛddhidatantra).** From Lu 2015 only: Lalla, like Śrīpati and Bhāskara II, treats the sphere in a separate chapter, gola-bandha ("tying the sphere"), not in the chapter on instruments. Lalla also teaches how to find the time and the lagna (the rising point on the eastern horizon) with the sphere (Lu cites Ôhashi 1994: 269). I found Bina Chatterjee's edition (INSA 1981), Part 1 (Sanskrit text and commentary), online but did not read its gola-bandha chapter, and I did not find Part 2 (translation) online. So nothing more is claimed for Lalla here.
- **Bhāskara II (1150, Siddhānta-śiromaṇi, Golādhyāya ch. VI, Golabandha).** Read in the translation of L. Wilkinson, revised by Bāpū Deva Śāstrī (Bibliotheca Indica, Calcutta 1861), pp. 151–160. His additions to Bhāskara I:
  1. Rings of polished straight **bamboo**, marked in degrees (v. 1).
  2. A wooden **dhruva-yaṣṭi** (polar axis) with a loose earth sphere. Spheres of the Moon, Mercury, Venus, Sun, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn and the fixed stars sit beyond it, and beyond them the **khagola** and the **dṛggola** ("double sphere", combining the circles of both). The outer shells are fixed on hollow cylinders round the axis, so the bhagola turns freely inside (vv. 2, 8–9, 31).
  3. The khagola has **four verticals**: prime vertical, meridian, and two **koṇa-vṛttas** (corner circles through NE–SW and NW–SE), plus the horizon (v. 3).
  4. The unmaṇḍala (v. 4), and the equinoctial with 60 ghaṭīs in the khagola, crossing the meridian at the latitude south of the zenith (v. 5).
  5. A smaller **azimuth (vertical) circle** pivoting on nails at zenith and nadir, one for all planets or eight (seven planets plus the dṛkkṣepa-vṛtta for the nonagesimal) (vv. 6–7).
  6. In the bhagola, two **ādhāra-vṛttas** (support circles) on the axis, the equinoctial (60 ghaṭīs), and the ecliptic marked with the 12 signs. The ecliptic is fixed at the equinoxes, with the solstitial points 24° N and S (vv. 10–12).
  7. A **kṣepa-vṛtta** for each planet, tied to the ecliptic at the nodes at the planet's greatest latitude (vv. 13–15).
  8. **Diurnal circles** at any declination, marked with 60 ghaṭīs; three on each side at the ends of the signs (vv. 27–29).
  9. As an alternative, the planets' orbits bound inside the ecliptic plane "like the circles woven by the spider", with cross diameters (v. 30).

## Part 2: the DICTO Yantra as rings

Model: GF1, the DICTO Yantra (Cleo-Antonius, p = 2/φ³), recomputed at 110 digits from the stored data. All 23 yantra conditions hold to below 10⁻¹¹¹. The yantra sits on the plane z = 1 with its circle (diameter 1) mapped to the cap of angular radius ρ about Z. A point (x, d), where d is depth below the top point, maps to (2x tan ρ, (1 − 2d) tan ρ, 1), normalised. Rings are named B1–B9 (bases), R1–R9 (right legs) and L1–L9 (left legs).

### (1) Ring count
**27 distinct rings for every ρ** (checked at ρ = 10°, 20.9052°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 75°, 85°, each with the identical incidence list). This is exact: the map is a scaling followed by a central projection, so lines stay lines and concurrences stay concurrences, and no two of the 27 side lines coincide (the smallest |n_i × n_j| is far above the error). The full rings cross in 254 antipodal pairs of points: 222 pairs where two rings cross, 31 pairs with three rings, and 1 pair with nine. That is 508 crossing points on the full rings.

**Cleo sphere** (20 yantras, ρ with tan ρ = 1/φ²). With the orientations of `cleo_sphere.py`, the 540 rings reduce to **320 distinct**. Two causes:
- **Antipodal caps** carry mirror yantras that share all 27 rings when their tops point to antipodal corners (8 of the 10 pairs in that file).
- **The legs of t3 run through the bottom point T**, which is the touching point with the neighbouring cap. A half turn about T keeps every great circle through T, so the t3 legs of the two caps coincide whenever both tops point away from the shared edge.

The number depends on the chosen orientations (see LEADS).

### (2) Crossing angles
The general rule is cos θ = |n_i·n_j|, with n = P×Q for the two endpoints P, Q of a side in the coordinates above (`data/rings.json`, `data/joints.csv`, `data/crossings_rho60.csv`). In closed form:
- **Bases** are meridians through ±X at longitude λᵢ = arctan(2 tan ρ (½ − bᵢ)), measured from the cap centre towards the yantra's top. Two bases cross at the hub at |λᵢ − λⱼ|.

- **Leg i crosses the equator ring at its apex** (longitude arctan(2 tan ρ (½ − aᵢ))) at inclination εᵢ = arccos|n_x|. Its mirror leg has −εᵢ.

### (4) The armillary frame
- **Polar axis = the base hub ±X**: the two opposite points on the horizon where all nine base rings meet. It is the image of the yantra's horizontal direction.
- **Equator = ring x = 0**, the image of the symmetry line. It is perpendicular to the polar axis and to every base. It carries all nine apexes: t7's apex is the top point O at +ρ and t3's apex is the bottom point T at −ρ. It also carries the seven apex-on-base joints.
- **Horizon = ring z = 0**, the image of the line at infinity. It passes through ±X and so belongs to the fan of meridians.
- **Ecliptic-like rings:** each leg pair (Lᵢ, Rᵢ) is a mirror pair of rings tied to the equator at one node (the apex), at ±εᵢ, much as the ecliptic is tied to the equator at the equinoxes.
- **Day-circle-like ring:** the yantra circle is a small circle of radius ρ centred on the equator ring at Z. It is not centred on the polar axis.

### (5) Build spec (ρ = 60°, sphere Ø 60 cm)
- **Rings:** 27 identical rings of radius 30 cm (circumference 188.5 cm), each marked 0–360° like Bhāskara's rings. The zero mark is where the ring crosses the equator ring on the cap side. Degrees count towards +x on bases and right legs and towards −x on left legs, so left and right legs carry the same marks. Recommended extras are the equator ring and the yantra circle (a small circle of radius 30 sin 60° = 25.98 cm).
- **Axle:** the nine base rings hang on one rod through ±X like Bhāskara's rings on the iron rod. They fan out at the longitudes λᵢ listed in (2), so each base is a meridian at fixed λᵢ.
- **Joints:**
  - 2 nine-ring hubs at the axle ends
  - 62 three-ring joints (31 + antipodes), using one-third cuts (tribhāgaccheda)
  - 444 two-ring crossings, using half cuts (ardhaccheda)
- **Per-ring marks:** `data/build_spec.csv` gives the yantra arc (from and to in degrees, length in cm) and every three-ring joint mark for each ring. `data/crossings_rho60.csv` gives every crossing on every ring with partner rings and angles.
  - Example, R1: apex joint at 0°, marmas at 21.695°, 36.467°, 48.070°, 61.906°, yantra arc 0–73.683° (38.6 cm), antipodes at +180°.
- **Cap-only build:** the 27 yantra arcs alone, lengths 18.5–56.8 cm, on a 60° cap.

## Part 3: the polar reciprocal (antipol)
On the sphere, polarity swaps a great circle with its pair of poles. Concurrent rings correspond to collinear poles.
- **(a) Pole constellation.** There are 54 beads (±n_i for 27 rings).
  - The 18 base beads lie on the equator ring x = 0, the polar of the hub. Each sits 90° from its base's longitude.
  - Left and right leg beads are mirror pairs under x → −x.
- **(b) Alignments.** Every three-ring joint (with its antipode) becomes one thread carrying three bead pairs. All 18 marma alignments are exact, as are the 6 corner-touch alignments and the 7 apex alignments.
  - The apex threads are meridians through ±X, because the apex lies on the equator.
  - Checked directly over all 2925 triples, there are exactly 115 collinear bead triples. This equals the dual of the joint list: 31 triples, plus the 84 triples among the nine base beads.
  - The smallest other |det| is 2.0·10⁻⁴, so there are **no further alignments**, matching the absence of hidden triple points.
- **(c) Polar triangles.** For triangle tᵢ with vertices A (apex), L, R, the polar triangle has vertices at the poles of the opposite sides, taken on the vertex side. Its sides are 180° minus the angles of tᵢ (checked to 10⁻⁸⁰).
  - All nine contain the core cap of radius 90° − ρ about Z (30° for ρ = 60°, 69.095° for the Cleo cap). This is the dual of "tᵢ lies in the cap".
  - t3 and t7 have all three vertices on the yantra circle, so their polar triangles are circumscribed about the core circle (all sides tangent).
  - t1 and t9 have their corners on the circle (the GF1 condition), so two sides of each touch the core circle.
  - The other five triangles stand clear of it.
  - Polar triangles of up-triangles point up and those of down-triangles point down: the polar form is again a nine-triangle yantra, inside out and round a circle instead of inside one (`figures/antipol-golayantra.png`, right panel).
  - Polar triangle areas at ρ = 60° are 2.985, 1.606, 1.225, 3.144, 4.186, 3.236, 1.217, 1.597 and 2.542 sr (t1…t9). The yantra triangles' own areas are 0.223–1.944 sr.
- **(d) Bead build note.** Thread 54 beads (two per ring, at opposite ends of a diameter) on 32 threads, each a great circle of the sphere:
  - **1 equator thread** carrying the 18 base beads
  - **7 meridian threads** through ±X, each carrying the bead pairs of two legs and one base (6 beads)
  - **18 marma threads** and **6 corner threads**, each carrying three bead pairs (6 beads)

  Each bead sits where its threads cross, so a frame of 32 great-circle wires (radius 30 cm), with beads soldered or tied at the crossings, fixes every bead without measuring. Positions are in `data/rings.json` → `polar.60.poles`.
- **Place in the Hopscotch Paradigm.** This is an antipol of DICTO's Hopscotch Paradigm (outline, §0: "a shape together with its polar, facing each other"). The quadric is the sphere and the slider is ρ. Along the slider the incidences never change (no walls); only metric quantities move. The natural candidate for the turn is ρ = 45°, where the core cap (radius 90° − ρ) and the yantra cap have the same size (see LEADS).
