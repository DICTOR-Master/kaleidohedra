# Cleo-Icos in the literature: reading study, round 1 (DICTO, 2026-10-14)

DICTO's question: the Cleo-Icos (the icosahedron's central pyramid on one face; equilateral base = icosahedron edge, side edges = its
circumradius, 0.951 of the edge) must do more in geometry than is remembered, once the regular tetrahedron took the stage. Read: the
reference library, then the web. Claims below are what the sources say; nothing here is new until checked.

## What is known (the Cleo-Icos under other names)

- **The tetrahedron of icosahedral packing.** Twelve balls round one make an icosahedron of 20 tetrahedra that are *not* regular:
  "slightly deformed" (spherical-confinement and supercooled-liquid literature). Regular tetrahedra leave a gap (five round an edge:
  5 × 70.53° = 352.6°, 7.36° short). This mismatch is the **geometric frustration** of icosahedral order: F. C. Frank (1952), the
  supercooling of liquids; reviews: Tarjus, Kivelson, Nussinov, Viot (2005, arXiv cond-mat/0509127); topological cluster
  classification (arXiv 1307.5517). The Cleo-Icos is the shape that actually closes the 13-atom icosahedron.
- **The 600-cell, flattened.** In curved space the 600-cell {3,3,5} has 20 *regular* tetrahedra round each vertex; Sadoc and Mosseri
  "decurve" it into flat space, with disclination lines carrying the lost curvature, and find the same lines in Frank–Kasper alloy
  phases (Mosseri and Sadoc, C. R. Physique 2014, doi:10.1016/j.crhy.2013.09.006; J. Phys. Colloques 1985). Flattening the 20 regular
  tetrahedra round a vertex gives the 20 Cleo-Icos: the Cleo-Icos is the 600-cell's cell brought into flat space.
- **"Golden tetrahedra."** S. Kabai, *Twenty Tetrahedra in Icosahedral Cluster* (Wolfram Demonstrations, July 2014, after K. Irwin and
  F. Fang): 20 "golden tetrahedra" make a regular icosahedron, where regular ones leave gaps. Same contributor: *Filling a Regular
  Icosahedron with Infinitely Many Golden Octahedra* (the icosahedron as 20 golden tetrahedra). Note that MathWorld's "golden
  tetrahedron" is a different shape (edges 1 and φ, edges parallel to two-fold axes).
- **Regular-tetrahedron packings near it.** Fang, Irwin, Kovacs, Sadler (arXiv 1304.1771): rotating 20 regular tetrahedra to close
  icosahedral gaps (the angle β = arccos((3φ − 1)/4)). Fang, Kovacs, Sadler, Irwin (arXiv 1311.3994): a quasicrystalline *packing* of
  regular tetrahedra (density 59.8%) from the Elser–Sloane quasicrystal (prototiles icosidodecahedron, dodecahedron, icosahedron and a
  "golden tetrahedron") and by decorating the Ammann prolate and oblate rhombohedra with regular tetrahedra (52 per prolate one).
  These are packings with gaps, not tilings, and use regular tetrahedra, not the Cleo-Icos.

## What was not found (so far)

- **Danzer's ABCK tetrahedra do not include it.** Danzer's family (Frettlöh 2011, ABCK tilings, in the library) is the tetrahedra whose
  faces lie in icosahedral mirror planes; every dihedral angle is a multiple of 90°, 60° or 36°. The Cleo-Icos's base angle 69.095°
  is not, so it is not an ABCK tile; nor is Antonius (110.905°). The Cleo-Icos + Antonius cut runs along planes square to three-fold
  axes, which are not mirror planes.
- **The split of the prolate golden rhombohedron into Cleo-Icos + Antonius + Cleo-Icos** (a tiling, not a packing) and the
  space-filling pair: not in Hafner–Žitko, Hafner–Komatar, Kabai's Demonstrations, the ABCK papers, or Fang et al. above.

## Round 2 (same day)

- **Gold nanoparticles: the strongest lead.** Icosahedral multiply twinned particles (MTPs) of gold and silver are 20 single-crystal
  fcc tetrahedra, twin-related on their faces; regular tetrahedra cannot fill the icosahedron, so the particles carry strain (about 6%
  for the icosahedron, about 2% for the decahedron; Marks and Peng 2016 review). Yacamán, Heinemann, Yang and Poppa (J. Cryst. Growth
  47 (1979) 187-195) found the units in gold to be "20 identical and twin-related tetrahedral units" that are "slightly deformed", whose
  "crystal structure ... is rhombohedral". Ino (J. Phys. Soc. Jpn 21 (1966) 346) is the early work.
  - **Our observation, consistent with that:** an fcc tetrahedron's three edges from one corner span the fcc rhombohedral cell (60°).
    Stretched uniformly into the Cleo-Icos, those edges meet at 63.43°, so the cell becomes exactly the **prolate golden rhombohedron**,
    the one that splits into Cleo-Icos + Antonius + Cleo-Icos. The rhombohedral angle in the 1979 paper is not in the abstract; to check
    in the full text before any claim.
- **Henley's canonical-cell tiling** (icosahedral quasicrystals): its edges run only along two-fold and three-fold directions (b and c
  bonds), so its cells cannot include the Cleo-Icos, whose side edges lie on five-fold axes.
- **The "20-group" (Sen, Aschheim, Irwin, Mathematics 5 (2017) 29) and its E8 link (Crystals 2024):** 20 *regular* tetrahedra round a
  point, with gaps, studied for a 6D lattice; not the Cleo-Icos. Fang, Irwin, Kovacs, Sadler (Fractal Fract. 3 (2019) 48): closing those
  gaps by rotating the regular tetrahedra (angle β).
- **Elser–Sloane (J. Phys. A 20 (1987) 6161):** the 3D slice's "golden tetrahedron" prototile; its shape is still to be read in the
  original (doi:10.1088/0305-4470/20/18/016).

## Round 3 (same day): the Cleo-Icos is Mackay's unit

- **Mackay (1962), Acta Cryst. 15, 916:** shells of equal spheres round one centre, with global icosahedral order. The Mackay icosahedron
  is built of 20 distorted tetrahedra in fcc stacking; its basic unit is a near-regular tetrahedron with two edge lengths in the ratio
  ≈ 1.05146 and faces at ≈ 63°26′ (as described secondhand, Struct. Chem. 2016, doi:10.1007/s11224-016-0843-5). Those are exactly the
  Cleo-Icos's numbers: base edge ÷ side edge = 1/sin 72° = 1.05146, and 63.43° = 63°26′ at the centre. **So the Cleo-Icos is the unit
  tetrahedron of the Mackay icosahedron**, the structure of most icosahedral metal clusters and nanoparticles.
- **Ino (1969, J. Phys. Soc. Jpn) began the strain models** of icosahedral multiply twinned particles; Marks (Phil. Mag. A 49 (1984) 81)
  reviews homogeneous and inhomogeneous strain; the rhombic or rhombohedral view (Bagley; Yang 1979, J. Cryst. Growth 47, 274; Yacamán et
  al. 1979) treats each unit as a slightly distorted crystal. Wikipedia, *Icosahedral twins*, gives the overview.
- **Coxeter's statistical honeycomb:** regular tetrahedra would need 2π / arccos(1/3) ≈ 5.1043 round each edge; Coxeter (*Regular
  Polytopes*, ch. 22; *Close-packing and froth*, 1958) used it to describe froth and random packing (cited via arXiv 1108.2494). The
  Cleo-Icos is one way of making exactly 5 fit: its side-edge angle is exactly 72°.

## What this means for DICTO's work (to word carefully)

- The Cleo-Icos itself is **known**: the icosahedron's central pyramid, Mackay's unit tetrahedron, Kabai's "golden tetrahedron". Our
  papers should cite Mackay (1962) where the Cleo-Icos is introduced.
- **Not found** in any source read: the Cleo-Icos as the corner of the prolate golden rhombohedron cut into Cleo-Icos + Antonius +
  Cleo-Icos, the Cleo-Icos & Antonius space-filling pair, the rhombic hexecontahedron as 20 + 20 + 20, and the octahedron as 20 Cleo-Icos
  + 12 half caps. The rhombohedral description of the units in gold (1979) is suggestive: if the units are strained into the Cleo-Icos
  shape, their cell is the golden rhombohedron (63.43°). Their published angle is still to be read in the full text.
